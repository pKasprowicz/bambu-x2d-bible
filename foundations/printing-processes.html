Printing processes compared

Melting plastic through a nozzle is only one way to build in layers. This page tours every major process, shows how each one works, compares them on detail, strength, cost, safety and post-processing, and helps you choose the right one for a given part.

BeginnerIntermediate depth⏱ 40 min read
What you will learn
  • How FDM, resin, powder-bed, jetting, binder and metal processes actually create a layer.
  • Which process delivers fine detail, which delivers strength, and which delivers low cost.
  • What post-processing and safety each process demands in practice.
  • How to choose a process for a given part with a simple decision tree.

🔰 FDM / FFF: extrusion of thermoplastic

Fused deposition modeling (FDM), also called fused filament fabrication (FFF), is the process behind almost every consumer printer you will meet. A thermoplastic strand (filament) is pushed into a heated nozzle, melts, and is laid down as a thin bead that fuses to the layer below and solidifies within a fraction of a second. The toolhead traces each layer's outline and fills the interior; then the nozzle rises one layer height and repeats.

🔰 Why FDM dominates hobby printing

  • Cost: capable machines are available from a few hundred dollars; filament is roughly US$15–30 per kilogram for common types.
  • Material range: PLA, PETG, ABS, ASA, nylon, polycarbonate, TPU, composites with carbon or glass fiber, and many specialty types (see Materials overview).
  • Simple workflow: there is no liquid, no powder and no curing station. Remove the part, remove supports and you are done.
  • Size: build volumes of 200–350 mm per side are standard and much larger machines exist.
  • Strength: functional parts in engineering plastics are realistic.

🔧 Limits you should know

  • Resolution: the nozzle diameter sets the smallest feature; with a 0.4 mm nozzle, features thinner than roughly 0.4–0.8 mm are unreliable.
  • Layer lines are visible and the surface is textured unless you finish it.
  • Anisotropy: parts are weaker across layers than along them.
  • Overhangs need supports or careful design; see Supports, bridges & overhangs.
FDM versus FFF

"FDM" is a trademark of Stratasys. "FFF" is the generic, community-created term with the same meaning. In practice, people use them interchangeably, and so does this compendium.

🔰 Resin printing: SLA, MSLA and DLP

In vat photopolymerization, the "ink" is a liquid photopolymer: a resin whose molecules link into a solid when exposed to the right wavelength of light (typically UV around 365–405 nm). The build plate dips into a vat of resin, light cures one layer, the plate lifts, fresh resin flows in, and the next layer is exposed. Three light-delivery methods exist:

MethodHow the layer is drawnStrengthsWeaknesses
SLA (laser)A UV laser and scanning mirrors trace the layer point by pointVery smooth, accurate large parts, mature industrial machinesSlower for filling large areas; more expensive optics
DLPA projector flashes the entire layer image at onceFast; pixel size is fixed by the projectorDetail drops as the build area grows; projector lamp or LED aging
MSLA (LCD)A UV LED array shines through a monochrome LCD that masks the layerLow-cost, high resolution on small areas, the common desktop typeLCD screens wear out and are consumables; light uniformity varies

🔰 What the layer cycle looks like

  1. Lower the plateThe plate descends until the part sits one layer height above the film.
  2. ExposeThe LCD shows the slice and the LEDs flash for a few seconds (bottom layers get much longer exposures so the part sticks to the plate).
  3. PeelThe plate lifts; the freshly cured layer has to separate from the film. Peeling creates suction and force that can pull parts off the plate or tear thin features.
  4. Rest and refillResin flows back under the part. Viscous resins need a short pause.
  5. RepeatOften several hundred to several thousand layers at 0.03–0.1 mm each.

🔧 Resolution and accuracy

On an MSLA printer the XY resolution is set by the pixel pitch of the screen (commonly around 20–50 µm on consumer machines; the same screen size with more pixels gives finer detail). Z resolution is the layer height. In practice you can print faces and fine text that would be impossible on a 0.4 mm FDM nozzle. Accuracy still depends on exposure calibration, resin shrinkage and orientation.

🔰 Design rules specific to resin

  • Tilt the part 30–45° rather than printing it flat. Smaller cross-section per layer means less peel force and fewer failures.
  • Avoid suction cups: a hollow shape with a closed bottom traps resin, causing blow-outs. Add drain holes (at least 2 mm, typically 3–5 mm, two of them so air can enter).
  • Hollow large solids to save resin and reduce stress, but only with drain holes.
  • Supports are dense: thin tips leave small marks, so aim supports at hidden faces.
Liquid resin is a chemical hazard

Uncured resin can cause skin sensitization (an allergy that can develop after repeated contact and then stay for life). Always wear nitrile gloves, avoid touching your face, work with ventilation, and never pour resin or its wash liquid down the drain. See Safety & workspace.

🔰 The wash-and-cure workflow

A resin print is not finished when the machine stops: it is coated in uncured liquid and only partly polymerized. Three more steps complete the part.

Waste handling in a hobby setting

Leave used wash alcohol in a clear sealed jar in sunlight for a day: dissolved resin cures into a sludge that settles, and you can pour the clear alcohol off for reuse. Cure liquid leftovers fully in the sun or in a UV chamber before disposing of them as solid waste according to your local rules. Wipe paper towels and gloves that contain resin, then cure them before binning.

Resin: strengths

  • Excellent surface finish and small-feature detail
  • Isotropic-looking parts with no visible beads
  • Fast for small batches of miniatures or jewelry patterns
  • Low-cost machines for what they do

Resin: trade-offs

  • Messy: gloves, solvents, curing station
  • Parts are usually brittle and degrade in sunlight
  • Small build volumes
  • Liquid waste and odor; needs a ventilated space

🔧 Powder bed fusion: SLS and MJF

In powder bed fusion, a thin layer of fine polymer powder (most often nylon, PA12 or PA11) is spread across a heated bed and selectively fused. The part grows inside a block of loose powder that acts as built-in support, so overhangs, interlocking assemblies and hollow structures are easy. When the job ends, you dig the parts out ("depowdering") and the unfused powder is partly recycled.

What makes powder-bed parts special

  • Strength and isotropy: nylon SLS/MJF parts are tough and are closer to uniform strength in all directions than FDM parts. They are commonly used for hinges, clips, ducts and end-use components.
  • Nesting: parts can be packed in 3D throughout the build volume, which makes batches of dozens or hundreds economical.
  • Surface: slightly grainy matte finish. Bead blasting, tumbling, dyeing and vapor smoothing improve it.
  • Powder logistics: powder is reused, usually mixed with a proportion of fresh powder to maintain quality, and fine powder must be handled with dust protection.

Desktop-sized SLS machines exist, but the cost of the equipment, powder handling and depowdering stations puts most users in the "order from a service bureau" category. Prices for the machine class typically run from the low five digits upward. See the cost section below.

🔧 Material jetting and binder jetting

Both processes borrow the inkjet idea: a printhead with many tiny nozzles sweeps across the build area and deposits droplets.

  • Material jetting (for example PolyJet): the droplets are the build material: liquid photopolymer cured immediately by a UV lamp. Because different nozzles can jet different resins, a single part can mix colors and hardness (rigid to rubber-like) with extremely fine layers (around 16–30 µm). A gel-like support material is washed away with water. Excellent for realistic prototypes, medical anatomy models and overmolded-look parts; costly in both machine and materials.
  • Binder jetting: the droplets are a glue printed into a powder bed. The result is a fragile "green" part that is cured and then either infiltrated, sintered (metal and ceramic) or dyed (full-color gypsum models). Sand binder jetting produces foundry molds and cores. There are no thermal stresses during printing, so large parts and dense nesting are possible.

🚀 Metal additive manufacturing

Metal printing covers several distinct technologies. All of them produce real metal parts, but with very different costs, sizes and post-processing.

ProcessHow it worksTypical materialsNotes
LPBF / DMLS / SLMA laser melts thin layers of metal powder in an inert-gas chamberStainless steels, aluminum alloys, titanium, nickel superalloys, tool steel"DMLS" is a trademarked name for essentially the same family as LPBF (laser powder bed fusion). Needs supports for heat conduction, stress relief and removal from the plate.

Why bound-metal parts shrink so much

The green part is part metal powder and part plastic binder by volume, with air gaps between the powder grains. Debinding removes the plastic and sintering fuses the touching grains by diffusion, closing the gaps. Volume drops, so every dimension shrinks, often by roughly 15–20 %. Software predicts and compensates, but dimensional accuracy still depends heavily on part geometry and furnace profile. Plan for flat, well-supported shapes and generous machining allowances on critical features.

Metal powder is not a hobby material

Fine metal powders (especially aluminum and titanium) are flammable or explosive as dust clouds, and require inert gas, grounding and trained handling. Do not attempt LPBF at home. If you want metal parts, order them from a service or use bound-metal workflows where the furnace step is done by a specialist.

🚀 CLIP, volumetric, pellet, concrete and bio printing

CLIP and other continuous resin processes

Developed to make resin printing fast and layerless. Part surfaces look smooth because there are no peel steps. Primarily industrial machines with specialized resins (elastomers, engineering plastics); used for production runs of lattices, midsoles, dental parts and consumer products.

Volumetric printing

A research-stage and early-commercial technique: a vial of photoresin is rotated while light patterns are projected from many angles. Whole parts form in tens of seconds and need no supports. Resolution, material choice and size are still limited.

Pellet and large-format extrusion

Instead of filament, plastic pellets are melted by a screw extruder, giving a far higher throughput (kilograms per hour) and much cheaper material. Used for furniture, molds, boat tooling and architectural pieces. Bead sizes of 3–10 mm or more; surface is coarse.

Concrete and construction printing

Pumped cementitious mortar is extruded in wide beads to build walls on site. Used for houses, small buildings and landscape elements. It is a materials and civil-engineering challenge as much as a printing one, with reinforcement and codes to consider.

Bioprinting

Cells in a hydrogel "bioink" are deposited into tissue-like structures. A research field for tissue models, drug testing and long-term organ goals. Not a hobby area and not something to attempt with desktop printers.

Sheet lamination (LOM, UAM)

Paper, plastic or metal foils are bonded and cut layer by layer. Ultrasonic metal lamination can embed sensors; paper lamination is a low-cost niche for full-color models.

Rule of thumb for ordering services

For a one-off part, FDM at home is almost always cheapest in money. For a batch of 50–500 functional nylon parts, MJF/SLS from a service often beats printing them yourself in both cost and strength. Compare a service quote with your own cost (machine time + filament) and the cost of failures.

🔧 Hybrid workflows: combining processes

Real projects often chain several processes, each doing what it is best at. These are four combinations that experienced makers use regularly.

Key takeaways

  • FDM is the best all-round starting point; resin is the detail specialist; powder fusion is the strong-batch specialist; metal is industrial.
  • Resin gives fine detail, but the workflow (gloves, wash, cure, waste) is part of the price.
  • Powder-bed parts print without supports and are strong, but powder handling and machine cost make them a service-bureau choice for most people.
  • Bound-metal and binder-jetted parts shrink during sintering; plan the design and tolerances accordingly.
  • Hybrid workflows (print then cast, print then machine, print then paint) combine strengths and are the norm in professional work.
  • Pick a process by asking about material, detail, strength, volume and budget, in that order.
