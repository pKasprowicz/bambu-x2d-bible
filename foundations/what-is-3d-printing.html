What is 3D printing?

3D printing builds objects by adding material one thin layer at a time, straight from a digital file. This page gives you the complete mental map: how it differs from other ways of making things, the processes, the workflow, the history, the vocabulary and what to realistically expect.

🔰 The big idea: three ways to make a thing

Every manufactured object, from a spoon to a jet engine bracket, was made by one of three broad strategies. Understanding them makes it obvious why 3D printing is exciting, and also why it has not replaced everything else.

Why "additive" is a big deal

With subtractive and formative methods, complexity costs money: more axes, more cutters, more mold slides. In additive manufacturing the cost is driven mainly by volume and print time, not by shape. A hollow, curved, internally ribbed part can cost less than a solid block. That is the one idea to remember.

Four practical strengths

🧩

Complexity is free

Hollow shapes, channels, lattices and organic curves cost no more than simple boxes.

⚡

No tooling

From file to part with no mold, jig or cutter set. Ideal for one-offs and small runs.

🔁

Fast iteration

Change a number in the model and have a new version the same day, often the same afternoon.

🎯

Customization

Every part can be different: a hand brace for one person, a bracket for one weird machine.

🔰 The seven process families

"3D printing" is a nickname. The formal term is additive manufacturing (AM), and the international standard ISO/ASTM 52900 sorts all additive methods into seven categories based on how layers are created and bonded. Nearly everything you will do at home or in a classroom is the first category (material extrusion), with resin vat printing a distant second. The others live in industry and service bureaus, but you should know they exist because they appear in the news, in job listings and in the parts you buy.

🔰 The complete workflow: idea → part

No matter which printer you own, every successful print follows the same five stages. When something goes wrong later in this course, the first question is always "which stage did the problem enter?" Knowing the pipeline makes troubleshooting far easier.

Stage 1 · Idea and requirements

Every good part starts with a question: what must it do? A phone stand only needs to hold weight and look decent. A bracket near a hot motor must survive temperature. A hinge must flex thousands of times. Writing down three things (size, load, environment) before you open any software prevents most disappointment and tells you which material to choose later. See the materials overview when you reach that question.

Stage 2 · The 3D model

A printer cannot print a picture; it needs a solid, watertight 3D shape. You get one in three ways:

  • Download from a repository such as MakerWorld, Printables or Thingiverse. The fastest way to start, and the way almost everyone begins.
  • Design in CAD (computer-aided design) software. Beginners use browser tools like Tinkercad; engineers use parametric tools like Fusion, Onshape or FreeCAD. See CAD & modeling software.
  • Scan a real object with photogrammetry or a 3D scanner, then clean the mesh. See file formats & mesh repair.

The model is exported as a mesh file (usually STL or 3MF). A mesh is simply a skin of thousands of tiny triangles describing the outside of the object. If the skin has holes the slicer cannot tell inside from outside, which is the origin of many "weird first print" stories.

Stage 3 · Slicing

The slicer is software that cuts the model into horizontal layers (hence the name) and calculates the exact path the nozzle or light source must follow for each one. You choose the material profile, layer height, wall count, infill, supports and speed. The result is a file of machine instructions called G-code (a 3MF project that bundles settings plus G-code is common on modern printers). Slicers are covered in Slicers compared, and every important setting is explained in Slicer settings explained.

Stage 4 · Printing

The printer heats up, measures or probes the bed, draws a purge line and then follows the G-code: move, extrude, move, extrude, thousands of times per layer, hundreds of layers per part. Your job is to watch the first layer (it decides whether the next ten hours succeed) and then let the machine work. On a printer with a camera and AI detection you can monitor remotely; on an older machine, stay nearby and check periodically. See Your first print for the walkthrough.

Stage 5 · Post-processing

Out of the printer, a part may still need support removal, trimming, sanding, painting, gluing, heat-set inserts or annealing. Post-processing is where hobby prints become products. Even a part you will never finish needs one routine step: measure it and write down what you learned, because that data drives the next iteration. See Post-processing.

Debug by stage

Part looks wrong? Ask: Is the model wrong (stage 2)? Did the slicer decide something strange (stage 3)? Did the machine fail to execute correctly (stage 4)? A quick look at the slicer preview often reveals the problem before you waste any filament.

🔰 Layer by layer: what a printed surface really is

The slicer cuts the model into slices, and the printer reproduces them as thin flat sheets of material stacked on top of each other. The thickness of each sheet is the layer height, typically 0.08–0.32 mm for plastic filament printers (a human hair is about 0.07 mm). Two consequences follow, and they shape almost every design and finishing decision you will ever make.

Consequence 1: the staircase effect

A vertical wall comes out smooth, a flat top comes out smooth, but a sloped surface becomes a staircase, because each layer is a flat step. The shallower the slope and the thicker the layers, the bigger the steps. Halving the layer height halves the step height but roughly doubles the print time, which is the classic quality-versus-speed trade-off.

Consequence 2: parts are weaker between layers

Within one layer the plastic forms continuous lines (beads) that are strongly fused. Between layers the bond relies on the new hot bead re-melting the surface of the one below. That joint is good but never as good as continuous material. A printed part therefore behaves differently depending on the direction of the load. This property is called anisotropy, and it is why print orientation matters so much. Details are in Strength & orientation.

Why layer adhesion depends on temperature

Each new bead is laid at roughly 190–300 °C (depending on the polymer) onto plastic that has already cooled. Bonding happens when the molecule chains at the two surfaces diffuse into one another before the joint drops below the polymer's glass-transition temperature. Hotter nozzle, slower cooling and a warm enclosure all give chains more time, so strength between layers goes up. That is why engineering materials are printed in heated chambers.

🔰 X, Y, Z: the coordinate system

Every printer works in a Cartesian coordinate system. X is left-right, Y is front-back and Z is up-down, measured from a defined origin near the front-left corner of the build plate. G-code is simply a long list of coordinates: "go to X100, Y60, extrude a little, go to X110, Y60…". Which physical part moves along which axis differs between machine designs (some move the bed in Y, others move the toolhead; see Anatomy of an FDM printer), but the slicer and G-code always use the same convention.

Why does Z only go up in tiny steps?

For each layer the printer raises the nozzle (or lowers the plate) by exactly one layer height. The XY movement draws the shape; the Z movement just moves to the next sheet. This is why 3D printers are sometimes called "2.5D" machines. Special techniques such as non-planar slicing and robotic-arm printing break this rule, but they are for advanced users.

🔰 Visual glossary: the words you will hear every day

Learn these terms now and the rest of the compendium (and every forum thread) becomes readable. The full A–Z list is in the Glossary.

🟫

Build plate

The surface the first layer sticks to. Also called bed, build surface or print sheet. Often flexible spring steel with a PEI coating, sometimes glass.

🔥

Nozzle

The small hole (commonly 0.4 mm) where melted plastic leaves the printer. Its diameter sets the line width and detail limit.

🧵

Filament

The plastic "ink": a thin strand, usually 1.75 mm diameter, wound on a spool. PLA, PETG, ABS and TPU are common types.

⚙️

Extruder

The gear mechanism that grips filament and pushes it toward the hotend, and pulls it back when needed (retraction).

♨️

Hotend

The heated assembly that melts the filament: a heater, a temperature sensor and the nozzle on its tip.

🔪

Slicer

Software that turns a 3D model into layer-by-layer instructions. Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, PrusaSlicer and Cura are popular.

📜

G-code

The plain-text command language the printer reads: move here, heat to that, extrude this much.

📏

Layer height

Thickness of each printed layer. Smaller means finer surface detail and longer prints; 0.2 mm is the standard compromise.

🕸️

Infill

The internal pattern that fills a part's interior. Expressed as a percentage: 10–20 % is typical, 100 % is solid.

🧱

Perimeters / walls

The outlines printed around each layer. The number of walls matters more for strength than infill does.

🏗️

Supports

Temporary scaffolding printed under overhangs and removed afterwards. Costs time, material and surface quality.

🥽

Brim / skirt / raft

Brim is a flat collar that widens the first layer to resist warping; a skirt is a test outline; a raft is a sacrificial base.

↩️

Retraction

Pulling filament back slightly before a travel move so the nozzle does not ooze. Wrong settings cause stringing.

🕷️

Stringing

Thin wisps of plastic between separate parts of a print, caused by oozing (wet filament, high temperature, weak retraction).

🌀

Warping

Corners lifting off the plate as plastic shrinks while cooling. Worst with ABS and other high-shrink materials.

🔰 A short history

3D printing is older than most people think. For decades it was a costly industrial prototyping tool; then a few patent expirations and one open-source project turned it into a consumer technology. Learning the timeline explains why today's machines look the way they do.

  • 1984 · Stereolithography. Chuck Hull patents a process that cures liquid resin with UV light layer by layer. He later co-founds 3D Systems, which sells the first commercial machines in the late 1980s. The ".STL" file format still carries the name.
  • 1986–1989 · Powder fusion. Carl Deckard at the University of Texas develops selective laser sintering (SLS): a laser fuses powder into a solid. Industrial parts without molds become possible.
  • 1989 · FDM. Scott Crump patents fused deposition modeling, the "melt and extrude a bead" technique, and founds Stratasys. The generic term FFF (fused filament fabrication) appears later because "FDM" is a trademark.
  • 2005 · RepRap. Adrian Bowyer's open-source project aims for a printer that can print most of its own parts. Its designs spread worldwide and create the hobbyist community.
  • 2009 · Patent expiry. Core FDM patents expire. Kits and cheap clones appear, MakerBot starts selling, crowdfunding makes small companies viable.
  • 2010s · Maturing. Josef Prusa's i3 design becomes the most-copied open printer; affordable resin printers appear; heated beds, auto bed leveling, Marlin and later Klipper firmware spread; cheap Cartesian printers reach very low price points.
  • 2022 onward · The fast, enclosed era. Bambu Lab releases an enclosed CoreXY machine with high acceleration, automatic calibration, cameras and a multi-spool system. The industry response is quick: competitors adopt similar architectures, and the expectation shifts from "tinkering project" to "appliance".
  • Mid 2020s · Multi-color and dual nozzle. Multi-material systems become mainstream and machines with two independent nozzles appear. The Bambu X2D is an example of this generation.

🔰 What you can and cannot make

A useful rule: 3D printing is superb at geometry and poor at volume, finish and certainty. If you need a strange shape once, it is perfect. If you need a million identical shapes or a mirror-smooth surface straight from the machine, it is not.

Great at

  • Custom brackets, mounts, clips, adapters and enclosures
  • Prototypes and form-fit checks in hours
  • Jigs, fixtures and tools for workshops
  • Replacement parts for discontinued items
  • Models, miniatures, props, cosplay armor
  • Education: seeing geometry and physics in your hands
  • Organic, hollow or internally channeled shapes

Poor at

  • Thousands of identical parts at low cost per piece
  • Glass-smooth surfaces or tight tolerances without finishing
  • Parts that must be food-safe, sterile or certified
  • High-temperature or high-impact loads with ordinary materials
  • Large solid objects (slow and expensive in plastic)
  • Transparent, optical-quality parts
  • Anything needing identical strength in all directions

🔰 Is 3D printing the right tool for this job?

Experienced makers do not print everything; they print what suits the process. Use this quick decision guide whenever a project comes up.

🔰 Common myths, corrected

"It is just for plastic toys and trinkets."

Toys are a fun beginning, but printed parts are used in jigs and fixtures, drones, robot frames, prosthetic-socket prototypes, dental models, motorsport ducting and production tooling. Engineering filaments (nylon, polycarbonate, carbon-fiber composites) and metal processes extend the range a long way.

"You press a button and a perfect object appears."

Modern printers are close to this for simple models, and calibration is largely automatic. But files, orientation, materials, humidity and surface choices still matter. The first weeks are about building the judgment that predicts failures before they happen.

"3D printed parts are weak."

Weak in some directions and when poorly designed. A well-oriented part with enough walls in PETG, nylon or a composite filament is strong enough for many functional jobs. Designing with the layer direction in mind is the key skill (see Strength & orientation).

"Filament is the only running cost."

Filament is the biggest consumable, but nozzles, build plates, lubricants, drying equipment, tools, electricity and your time all count. For most hobbyists these are modest; see the buying guide for a first-year budget.

"Any STL I find online will print."

Files may have thin walls, holes, unrealistic overhangs or be designed for another printer's tolerances. Look at the slicer preview, read the comments and check if the author has printed it.

"Resin printing is the premium version of filament printing."

Resin gives finer detail on small parts, but it involves liquid chemicals, washing, curing and gloves, and the parts are usually more brittle. It is a different tool, not a better one. See Printing processes compared.

"3D printers will replace factories."

Additive manufacturing complements conventional processes. It excels at low volume and complexity; injection molding and machining remain much better for mass production.

"I need to be a programmer or engineer."

No. Downloading and slicing existing models needs only patience. CAD skills grow gradually, and browser tools like Tinkercad are designed for complete beginners.

Expect failures, and plan for them

Even experienced makers lose prints to a dislodged part, a tangled spool or a clogged nozzle. A realistic planning figure for a beginner is that about one in five early prints needs a retry. The cost is small if you treat each failure as a lesson and keep a short log: what changed, what happened, what you will try next.

Four rules for a good first month

  1. Print designs other people have proven.Download well-reviewed models from a repository; save your own designs for later.
  2. Change one thing at a time.When tuning, adjust one setting and compare to the previous result. Two simultaneous changes teach you nothing.
  3. Use PLA first.It is forgiving, low-odor and inexpensive. Move to other materials only when you have a reason.
  4. Keep every failed print for a week.A tray of failures is the best textbook you will ever own: you can compare and recognize patterns (see the troubleshooting gallery).

🔰 What you need to get started

You do not need much, but a few items save a great deal of frustration. Tick them off as you collect them (your ticks are remembered in this browser).

  • A printer that suits your goals and space. See Choosing a printer.
  • One or two spools of PLA from a reputable brand, in colors you like (1 kg, 1.75 mm).
  • A slicer on a computer or phone. Most printer makers supply one that is preconfigured.
  • A stable, level table with a nearby power outlet and room around the printer. See Safety & workspace.
  • Basic tools: flush cutters, a scraper or spatula, tweezers, a small brush, digital calipers.
  • Dry storage: a sealable box with desiccant for filament you are not using. See Drying & storage.
  • Ventilation and a smoke detector in the room where the printer will run.
  • A first goal: a specific small model you want to print, such as a calibration cube or something practical.
  • Patience and a notebook for logging results.

Key takeaways

  • Additive manufacturing adds material where needed; subtractive removes it; formative shapes it in a mold. Printing wins when volume is low and complexity is high.
  • The official taxonomy has seven families; desktop printing is mostly material extrusion (FDM/FFF) and vat photopolymerization (resin).
  • Every print passes through five stages: idea, model, slice, print, post-process. Debug by identifying the stage.
  • Layers cause the staircase effect and a weaker direction between layers; orient and design accordingly.
  • G-code is just coordinates in X (left-right), Y (front-back) and Z (up).
  • Be realistic: print times are hours, failures happen, and skills grow in weeks and months, not minutes.
