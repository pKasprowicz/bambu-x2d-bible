Choosing a printer

The best printer is the one that fits your parts, your space, your patience and your budget. This guide turns that into a method: define the need, pick a machine category, look past the marketing numbers, then count the real cost over a year.

Beginner ⏱ 30 min read
Goal of this page You will be able to write down what you need from a printer, map it to a machine type, compare candidates on the specs that actually matter, and estimate the first-year cost honestly. Brand names appear only as examples; specifications and prices change quickly, so check current sources before buying.

🎯 Start with the need, not the machine

Most regrets come from buying for a hypothetical use. Before you read a single spec sheet, answer six questions honestly. Write the answers down; they will filter the market far more effectively than any review.

1 · What will you print?

Display models and figurines, functional brackets and enclosures, tools and jigs, cosplay, miniatures, prototypes? Each leans toward a different process and size.

2 · How big are the parts?

Measure your biggest wish-list part. Most useful parts fit in a 250 mm cube; helmets, furniture parts and large props do not, but they can be split and glued.

3 · Which materials?

PLA and PETG are easy on any machine. ABS, ASA, nylon and carbon-fiber blends need a heated, enclosed chamber and a hardened nozzle. Flexible TPU wants a good direct-drive extruder.

4 · How many colors?

Single-color with painting, a few colors via a multi-material system, or many colors with little waste? Color changes cost time and filament.

5 · How much tinkering?

Do you want to press print and get a result, or do you enjoy tuning, repairing and upgrading? Honest answers prevent frustration either way.

6 · Where will it live?

Noise, fumes, space, power and neighbors matter. See Safety & workspace before you commit to a spot.

Reading the categories in practice

Entry bedslingers are where the hobby grew up. The bed slides back and forth under the nozzle, which is simple and cheap but limits acceleration because the mass of the bed (and the print on it) must change direction. They teach you a lot because they reward understanding: levelling, tuning, retraction and cooling all matter. If you enjoy the machine as a project, this is a fine place to start. If you only want parts, the time you spend tuning may feel like a tax.

Enclosed CoreXY machines keep the bed moving only up and down while a light toolhead moves in X and Y. That allows high acceleration with good quality, and the enclosure stabilizes temperature, reduces drafts and contains fumes and noise. For most people who want to print functional parts reliably, this is the sweet spot. See the kinematics section for how the belts work.

Multi-material machines add automatic filament switching, a second nozzle or both. They unlock multi-color models, dissolvable supports and mixed material parts, at the cost of purge waste and more things that can jam. We look at them in their own section below.

Large-format printers are for objects that do not split well: helmets, enclosures, furniture. Remember that doubling a part's length multiplies its print time by far more than two, and that big flat parts in warp-prone materials fight you.

Resin printers deliver detail that filament cannot match: miniatures, jewellery masters, dental-style models, fine mechanical parts. The price is a chemical workflow. Read Printing processes and the resin safety section first.

IDEX and toolchanger systems have two or more independent heads. IDEX (independent dual extruders) can print two copies at once in mirror or duplicate mode, which doubles throughput for identical parts, or use two materials. Toolchangers switch between many heads for multi-color printing with very little waste. They are powerful but more expensive and need careful offset calibration.

📏 Specs that matter, and specs that mostly do not

Spec sheets are written to sell. A few numbers carry real information; many are noise. Use the table as a filter.

Specs worth comparing

  • Build volume that matches your biggest regular part, with a little margin.
  • Maximum nozzle and bed temperatures: determine which materials are possible.
  • Enclosure and chamber heating: decide whether ABS, ASA and nylon are realistic.
  • Extruder type: direct drive handles flexible filament far better.
  • Nozzle material and swap-ability: hardened nozzles for abrasive filament.
  • Bed surface and levelling: automatic levelling saves time.
  • Sensors: runout, tangle, power-loss recovery, first-layer inspection.
  • Noise level at typical speeds.
  • Spare-parts availability and price.

Specs that mislead

  • Peak speed (mm/s): rarely sustained; quality and acceleration decide real time.
  • Peak acceleration: often only for infill or special modes.
  • Layer resolution of 0.05 mm: not the same as visible quality.
  • Heat-up time to 100 °C: a marketing number; chamber soak matters more.
  • Peak flow rate: measured with a special nozzle and temperature.
  • Count of features: more sensors do not mean fewer failures.
  • Screen size and apps: nice, not decisive.
Judge by the finished part Ask for or find print time and a photo of the same model on the machines you consider, printed with default settings. A 40-minute spec-sheet claim that becomes 2 hours for a decent part tells you more than the headline speed. Independent reviewers who time standard test models are the best source.

Speed claims versus real throughput

A printer rated at 500 mm/s does not finish parts five times faster than one rated at 100 mm/s. Time is lost in acceleration and deceleration around corners, in heating, in levelling and calibration, in color changes and purging, and in the slower speeds needed for outer walls and overhangs. Throughput also depends on the slicer profile; a conservative profile will not reveal the machine's potential.

🌐 Ecosystem, support and openness

A printer is a long relationship with a company or a community. These questions often matter more than a tenth of a millimeter of accuracy.

  • Closed ecosystems integrate the printer, slicer, filament tags and cloud app. They tend to give excellent out-of-the-box results and little to tweak. You depend on the maker for updates, parts and policy changes.
  • Open ecosystems use standard firmware (Marlin, Klipper, RepRapFirmware), standard parts and any slicer. You can repair and modify nearly everything, but you assemble the experience yourself.
  • Hybrids offer some openness (local network mode, G-code export, third-party slicer profiles) around a polished core. Check what is truly possible before you buy.

Support, warranty and community

Look at the length and terms of the warranty, whether the company sells spare parts (hotends, belts, boards) at reasonable prices, how fast support answers, and whether software updates continue for several years. A large active community (forums, wikis, video tutorials) is a free support desk and a sign that models and profiles will keep appearing. Be sceptical of pre-order promises of features that do not yet exist.

Maintenance and consumables

Every printer needs regular care: cleaning the nozzle and bed, lubricating rails or rods, checking belts, replacing wear parts like nozzles, PTFE tubes, build plates and fans. Ask what maintenance the machine expects and whether the manufacturer publishes a schedule. Consumables extend beyond filament: spare nozzles, build plates, glue stick or cleaning solvent, filament dryers, purge waste on multi-color machines, and replacement filters for enclosed printers.

♻️ Used printers, kits and refurbished machines

Used printers

Can be a bargain. Ask for print samples, the number of hours or kilograms printed, any replaced parts, and test it under power. Check the hotend, the bed flatness, loose belts and cable wear. Avoid sellers who will not let you see it print.

Kits

Partial assembly saves money and teaches you how the machine works. Expect hours of building and some tuning afterwards. Not ideal if you need parts quickly or dislike fiddling.

Refurbished

Often come with a limited warranty from the manufacturer or reseller. Compare against new during sales; the saving may be small once warranty is considered.

🎨 Dual-nozzle and multi-material machines

Multi-material printing comes in three broad flavours, and understanding them helps you ignore the marketing language.

  • Single nozzle plus a filament switcher (the common AMS-style hub). One nozzle prints every color in turn; the old filament is retracted and a new one loaded, with the leftover melt purged. Simple and affordable, but purging wastes filament and time.
  • Dual nozzle printers carry two nozzles on one head or on separate carriages. Each can be loaded with a different material, so switching means selecting the other nozzle rather than purging. This reduces waste and makes soluble or breakaway supports practical. The price is mechanical complexity, nozzle alignment and keeping the idle nozzle from oozing.
  • Toolchangers pick up whole toolheads. Each head keeps its own filament and temperature, so color changes cost seconds and almost no waste. They are flexible but expensive, and calibration is demanding.
Example: Bambu Lab X2D The Bambu Lab X2D is one example of a dual-nozzle enclosed CoreXY machine combined with an automatic filament system, and shows how these ideas fit together in a single product. This site has a dedicated section on it; start with the X2D overview. The same buying questions above (what you print, which materials, how many colors, how much waste you accept) apply to it exactly as to any other printer.

🔧 Upgrade path and resale

Think about where you will be in a year. Printers with standard parts and open firmware can be improved step by step, such as a better hotend, a larger build plate or an input-shaper board. Closed machines usually improve through firmware and official add-ons. If your needs might outgrow the machine (larger parts, new materials), ask whether the platform can grow with you, or whether you would sell it and move on. Popular machines hold resale value better, which lowers the true cost of trying something new.

👤 Three example buyers

Abstract advice is easier to apply with concrete cases. These are invented but typical.

The curious beginner

Wants to print toys, cable clips and the occasional replacement part, has a small desk, and does not want to read forum threads. Best fit: an enclosed or well-supported entry machine with automatic levelling and strong default profiles. Spend the savings on a dryer and a few spools. Avoid kits and large formats.

The maker and tinkerer

Enjoys modifying things, wants to learn firmware and mechanics, and has a tolerance for downtime. Best fit: an open, well-documented bedslinger or CoreXY kit with a large community. Budget time and spare parts. A second, reliable machine later is a common path.

The small-batch seller

Prints many copies of a few designs and values uptime. Best fit: a fast enclosed machine with good sensors, possibly dual-head or IDEX for duplicate mode, and two of them rather than one big one so a failure does not stop production. Check license terms of the models you sell and local consumer-product rules.

🔍 How to read reviews and sales pages

  1. Look for time-stamped reviews. Firmware changes quickly; a review a year old may describe a different product.
  2. Prefer long-term reports to unboxing videos. Failure modes show up after hundreds of hours: loose belts, clogged hotends, worn plates.
  3. Check who paid for the unit. Sponsored or supplied review units are not automatically wrong, but treat uncritical praise with caution and look for a negative section.
  4. Read the complaints on forums for the specific model. Repeated reports of the same problem matter; one-off complaints do not.
  5. Compare like with like. A reviewer's default profile on one machine and a tuned profile on another is not a fair comparison.
  6. Watch for vague quality claims. Phrases such as "industrial grade" or "hyper speed" mean little without numbers and test conditions.

⚠️ Common buying mistakes

  • Buying for the biggest imaginable part. Large build volume costs space, time and money. Splitting a part usually works.
  • Chasing speed. A faster printer that needs constant tuning prints slower in practice than a slower reliable one.
  • Forgetting the room. Noise, smell and floor vibration decide whether you keep using the machine. See Safety & workspace.
  • Ignoring materials. If you need ABS, ASA or nylon, a machine without an enclosure and a suitable nozzle will frustrate you.
  • Counting only the printer. Filament, dryers and tools are part of the purchase.
  • Buying on launch hype. New models can have early firmware bugs; waiting a few months often costs nothing and avoids the worst.
  • Overbuying multi-material. If you print one color most of the time, a simple machine plus painting may serve better than a complex color system.

❓ Questions to ask before paying

New machine
  • What is the warranty length and what does it exclude (wear parts, damage from third-party filament)?
  • Where do I buy spare nozzles, hotends, belts and build plates, and what do they cost?
  • Can I use third-party filament and slicers? Does the printer work without a cloud account?
  • How long will the manufacturer provide firmware updates?
Used machine
  • Why are you selling, and how many hours or kilograms has it printed?
  • Which parts have you replaced or modified? Can I see it print a first layer?
  • Are there signs of heat damage around the hotend connector or bed cables?
  • Do you have the original profile, firmware version and receipt?
Multi-material or large machine
  • How much filament is wasted for a typical multi-color print?
  • What happens when a spool runs out or tangles during a long print?
  • How much floor space and power does it need, including the filament system?

📝 Before you buy: the checklist

  • I wrote down the largest part I want to make and the materials I need.
  • I picked a category from the decision tree and a shortlist of 2–3 machines.
  • I compared real print times and sample photos, not just spec-sheet speeds.
  • I checked warranty length, spare parts, software support and community size.
  • I budgeted 15–25% extra for consumables and tools.
  • I have a stable bench, an outlet and a ventilated spot (see Safety & workspace).
  • I know what maintenance the machine needs and who can help me when it breaks.

✅ Key takeaways

Remember these
  • Define your parts, materials and colors first; the category follows.
  • For most people who want reliable functional prints, an enclosed CoreXY machine is the sweet spot; bedslingers suit tinkerers and tight budgets; resin suits small, fine parts.
  • Ignore peak speed numbers; compare real print times on the same model.
  • Ecosystem, support and spare parts matter as much as hardware.
  • Budget for the whole first year, not only the printer.
  • Multi-material comes in hub, dual-nozzle and toolchanger forms, with different waste and complexity trade-offs.
