Your first print

A calm, step-by-step walkthrough from the closed box to a finished part on the plate. The steps apply to almost any filament printer; where a machine-specific detail matters, you will see a short note. By the end you will know what good looks like, what to do when it does not, and what to print next.

🔰 The mental model: what is happening

Before touching the machine, it helps to know what the machine is going to do. A 3D print is a loop of instruction, motion, melting and feedback. You provide a model; software turns it into thousands of tiny moves; the printer follows them while checking its temperatures and positions.

Three ingredients decide success

Almost every first-print failure comes from one of three things: the nozzle is the wrong distance from the plate (first layer), the filament is wet or not flowing (extrusion), or the part is not stuck down or not sliced sensibly (adhesion and orientation). Keep these in mind and the troubleshooting section at the end will read like common sense.

📦 Step 1: unboxing and inspection

Keep the box and the packing materials until the machine has printed successfully; you will need them if you must return it. Unpack on a clear table and take your time. Many "broken" printers are simply still locked for transport.

Voltage and mains

Some printers ship with a mains voltage selector on the back. Check it matches your wall supply before the first power-on, and use the supplied or a certified cable in a properly grounded outlet. See Safety & workspace for outlet, surge and fire safety.

📍 Step 2: placement

Where a printer lives shapes how often you will use it. A good location is stable, ventilated, near a power outlet and not in a bedroom or somewhere you would not smell a problem. Leave space for the door, the lid and the filament spool.

  • Table or shelf is level and does not wobble when you push it.
  • There is a power outlet within cable reach; avoid extension cables and adapters for heated machines.
  • Room is ventilated and has a working smoke detector.
  • Nothing flammable sits next to or above the printer.
  • Filament has a dry, dust-free place to wait.

🔌 Step 3: first power-up and calibration

On first start most printers walk you through a series of self-tests. Do not skip them. They teach the machine its own geometry and tune it for vibration.

  1. Connect and update.Join Wi-Fi or insert an SD/USB stick if needed, and install the firmware update. Updates fix real bugs and safety issues.
  2. Home the machine.It moves each axis until it finds its limit. Stand clear and listen for grinding or knocking; if you hear it, stop and re-check transit locks.
  3. Run vibration and bed calibration.These can take several minutes; the head moves and the bed heats. Let it finish; do not touch the plate while it probes.
  4. Check the Z offset.If the printer asks for a first-layer calibration, accept it. See the first-layer section below for what to look for.
Manual leveling

If your printer is a basic model with adjustable bed screws, level it hot: heat the bed to printing temperature, then use the paper test at each corner. You want light drag on a sheet of copy paper. Re-check after the first few prints because springs settle.

🧵 Step 4: loading filament

The loading procedure differs slightly between printers, but the idea is identical: heat the nozzle, push filament until it flows, and stop when the plastic extrudes cleanly.

Heat means burns

The nozzle reaches 200 °C or more. Never reach into the print area to guide filament with the nozzle hot unless the manual says it is safe; use pliers or wait for it to cool.

Start with PLA

PLA is the right first material: low temperature (about 190–220 °C), minimal warping, little odour, and tolerant of imperfect settings. Move to PETG, ABS and engineering materials later; see the materials section of this compendium.

🎯 Step 5: choose a first model

Your first print should be small, flat on the bottom, forgiving, and tell you something. Avoid your dream project; you will be learning the machine at the same time. Three classic candidates exist, and each teaches something different.

Why not start big?

A large print is a long bet: any early flaw ruins hours of work and wastes filament. A small print gives you a result and a lesson in under an hour. Every successful print builds trust in the printer and in your settings.

🍰 Step 6: slicing the model

The slicer turns a 3D model into G-code: the list of moves and extrusions the printer will execute. Use the slicer that matches your printer (the manufacturer's slicer or a well-known open one), and select the printer, nozzle size and filament profile first. A good built-in profile already sets temperatures, speeds and fan curves; you rarely need to change them for a first print.

Always read the preview

Before you send a job, scrub through the layer preview. Check that the first layer covers the plate where you expect, that no part floats in mid-air, and that the time estimate makes sense. Two minutes in the preview saves two hours of wasted print.

▶️ Step 7: start the print

  1. Check the plate.Clean and dry; no old prints, no fingerprints on the printing area.
  2. Check the filament.The loaded colour matches the slicer's choice; the spool unwinds freely.
  3. Send the file.From the slicer, over Wi-Fi, USB or SD card, according to your printer.
  4. Let it warm up.The printer heats the bed and nozzle, may run an automatic bed scan, and then purges a line or blob at the edge of the plate.
  5. Stay and watch.Do not walk away until the first layer is complete.

🔍 Step 8: watching the first layer

The first layer sets the foundation. If it is wrong, the print will be wrong; if it is good, the rest usually follows. You are looking for the right amount of "squish": the molten line is pressed slightly flat against the plate so it bonds across its whole width, and neighbouring lines fuse into a smooth sheet.

The adjust-while-printing trick

Most printers let you change Z offset while the first layer is still running (usually in steps of 0.02 mm or 0.05 mm). Change one step, wait for a lap of the pattern, and compare. You are done when adjacent lines just fuse, without gaps and without ridges between them. Newer machines offer a flow or first-layer calibration routine; use it when available.

Stop if the first layer fails

If the filament is dragging, curling up behind the nozzle, or not sticking at all, cancel the job. Clean the plate, re-check the Z offset, and restart. Continuing only wastes filament and can pull a part off the plate and entangle the nozzle.

🧤 Step 9: removing the print

  • Bed has cooled, or you are wearing heat-safe gloves.
  • Keep hands away from the scraper's path.
  • Remove brim and stray strands with flush cutters, not fingernails.
  • Wipe the plate with a clean cloth and a little dish soap before the next print.

🧹 Step 10: clean up and storage

  • Printer: brush or vacuum stray bits from the bed and the purge area. Remove loose filament dust. Never wipe a hot nozzle with a bare finger.
  • Filament: unload (or leave loaded if you print soon) and keep the spool sealed with desiccant, especially in humid climates. Moisture makes filament pop, string and weaken.
  • Records: note what worked: model, profile, temperatures and anything you changed. A habit of recording saves time later.
  • Power: switch the machine off or leave it in idle; do not leave a hot printer unattended.

✈️ Pre-flight checklist

Run through this before every print until it becomes automatic. The boxes keep their state in your browser.

  • Plate is clean and properly seated.
  • Nozzle is clean, with no leftover plastic.
  • Correct filament is loaded, dry, and flows freely.
  • Spool unwinds without tangles; the spool holder is free.
  • Printer is level or auto-calibrated; the first layer looked right last time.
  • Slicer profile matches printer, nozzle and filament.
  • Layer preview checked; supports and brim set on purpose.
  • Door or lid state suits the material (open for PLA, closed for ABS).
  • Nothing flammable or loose near the printer; smoke detector working.
  • You will be present for at least the first layer.

👀 What you will see during a print

Knowing the normal sequence makes it much easier to spot something abnormal. A typical job passes through these stages:

  1. Heat-up and purge.Bed and nozzle reach temperature. The printer may wipe the nozzle, scan the bed and extrude a short line or blob at the edge to prime the hotend. A little plastic waste here is normal.
  2. First layer.The nozzle traces the outline of the part and fills the footprint at slow speed. This is the stage to watch closely.
  3. Walls and infill.For every layer the printer draws outer and inner walls first, then fills the interior with the infill pattern. You will notice the speed changes: slow for the outer wall, faster for infill.
  4. Top layers and bridges.The final layers close the top of the part. They are printed over sparse infill, so thin top surfaces can look rough if there are too few top layers.
  5. Cool-down.The nozzle retracts, the head parks, and the printer cools. Parts stay slightly soft for a short time; wait before handling.

Normal sounds and smells

A printer is not silent. Fans whir, steppers hum, belts chirp as direction changes, and the bed heater clicks on and off. PLA gives off a faint sweet smell; other plastics smell stronger, which is covered in Safety & workspace. Sounds that are not normal include rhythmic clicking from the extruder (a jam or a nozzle too close), a sharp grinding from an axis (a collision or a locked part), and a burning or chemical smell (power off, investigate).

⚠️ Common beginner mistakes

Changing many things at once

If you change temperature, speed and retraction together, you will not know which fixed (or ruined) the print. Change one variable per attempt and write it down.

Ignoring wet filament

Filament absorbs moisture from the air. Symptoms (pops, bubbles, strings) look like slicer problems. Keep spools sealed with desiccant and dry them when in doubt.

Touching the plate

Finger grease ruins adhesion. Handle the plate by its edges and wash it regularly.

Skipping the first-layer check

People start a print and leave. Half of all failures show up in the first minutes; staying for the first layer is the cheapest insurance you have.

Printing huge models first

A big failure teaches the same lesson as a small one, at ten times the cost. Climb the ladder.

Unattended long prints early on

Until you trust the machine and have fire detection, stay nearby for long jobs. See the safety page for the sensible limits.

Failure is data

A failed print is not wasted effort. Keep it, look at where it went wrong (which layer, which corner), and ask which of the three ingredients (distance, flow, adhesion) was involved. Photograph it and note the settings. Experienced makers keep a small "wall of fails" for the same reason: each one explains a rule they now follow without thinking.

Where to find models

Beyond the three starters, the large public model repositories host millions of free designs. Look at the printing notes in each listing, filter by "no supports" while you are learning, and prefer models with photos of real prints. Always check the licence before you sell or redistribute copies; many designs are free for personal use only.

✅ Key takeaways

Remember these
  • A print is a pipeline: model, slicer, G-code, printer, feedback.
  • Unlock transit parts, level the machine and run the calibration routines before the first job.
  • Start with PLA, a small model and the manufacturer's default profile.
  • The first layer decides everything: aim for flattened, touching lines without gaps or ridges.
  • Most failures are adhesion, moisture or a clogged nozzle, not "a broken printer".
  • Work up the ten-print ladder, changing one thing at a time.
