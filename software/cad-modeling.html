Four ways to describe a shape

All modeling programs end up producing a mesh that your slicer can read, but they get there by very different routes. Choosing the right route for the job saves more time than learning any particular program.

🔰 Think "clay" or think "blueprint"?

If you ask "how should it look?" (a figurine, a creature, a smooth grip), mesh sculpting is natural. If you ask "how big must it be to fit?" (a bracket, a case, a gear), you need exact numbers and an editable history, which is what parametric CAD or code provide.

🔧 Exact vs approximate

Parametric and code models are mathematically defined (planes, cylinders, splines), and only converted to triangles when you export. Sculpted models are triangles from the start, so you cannot reliably say "make this hole 5.2 mm" without rebuilding it.

How to pick in 30 seconds

Total beginner or a quick one-off fixture: Tinkercad. Functional parts you will revise: Fusion, Onshape or FreeCAD. Families of parts with a few numbers changing: OpenSCAD or CadQuery. Creatures, faces and miniatures: Blender or Nomad. Reverse-engineering a broken part: calipers + parametric CAD.

Why learn parametric CAD

  • Change one dimension and the whole model updates
  • Reuse parts, assemblies and tolerances
  • Transferable skills across Fusion, Onshape, FreeCAD, SolidWorks

What it costs you

  • A few weekends to feel comfortable
  • Habit changes: constrain sketches, name features
  • Cloud accounts or licensing to keep an eye on

A learning path with a timeline

You do not need to learn everything. This sequence builds skills in the order they get used on real printed parts. Times are rough guidance for casual evening practice.

  • Week 1 · Blocks. Tinkercad or a basic CAD sketch-and-extrude tutorial. Goal: a coaster, a phone stand and a simple box with lid. Learn: units, workplane, grouping, alignment.
  • Weeks 2–3 · Sketches and constraints. Fusion, Onshape or FreeCAD. Goal: fully constrained sketches with named dimensions. Learn: extrude, cut, hole, fillet, chamfer.
  • Weeks 4–6 · Real parts. Model something you actually need: a bracket, a cable clip, a replacement knob. Learn: measuring with calipers, tolerances from Tolerances & fits, printing and revising.
  • Months 2–3 · Parametric habits. User parameters, patterns, mirrors, multi-body parts, assemblies and joints. Learn: design for splitting and for joints and fasteners.
  • Months 3–6 · Specialize. Add code (OpenSCAD/CadQuery), surfacing (loft/sweep), or sculpting (Blender/Nomad), depending on what you build. Learn: version control, naming, exporting STEP and 3MF.
  • Beyond. Simulation, topology optimization, CAM, or professional certification if your goals require it.

Core CAD concepts, illustrated

Almost every parametric program shares the same vocabulary. Once you know these ideas, switching programs feels like learning a new dialect, not a new language.

Sketches and constraints

A sketch is a flat drawing on a plane (lines, arcs, circles). Dimensions fix sizes (a length of 40 mm), and constraints fix relationships (this line is horizontal, these two circles are equal, this point lies on that line). A fully constrained sketch cannot move unexpectedly when you edit something else, which is what makes a model robust. Most programs color under-constrained geometry (often blue) and fully constrained geometry (black or green).

Features: extrude, revolve, loft, sweep

A feature turns a 2D sketch into 3D volume. The four workhorses cover most printable parts.

Fillets, chamfers and Booleans

A fillet rounds an edge; a chamfer cuts it at an angle. For printing, fillets on inner corners reduce stress concentrations, and chamfers on the bottom edge hide elephant foot and avoid overhang. Boolean operations combine solids: union joins, subtract (cut) removes one from another, and intersect keeps only the shared volume. In code-based CAD, Booleans are the main tool; in GUI CAD they hide behind "join / cut / intersect".

Patterns, assemblies and parameter tables

Patterns repeat a feature along a line (linear) or around an axis (circular), and mirror duplicates across a plane. Use them for bolt circles, vent slots and symmetrical parts; changing the count or spacing updates every copy. Assemblies place several parts in one file and connect them with joints or mates (rigid, revolute, slider), letting you check clearances and motion before printing. Parameter tables (user parameters or spreadsheets) keep the key numbers in one list, such as wall = 2.4, clearance = 0.3 and boss_d = 8, so you change one place instead of hunting through sketches.

Modeling for printing: the basics

A model that looks perfect on the screen can still fail on the bed. These habits cost nothing and prevent most problems. The full theory is in Design principles, Tolerances & fits and Strength & orientation.

📏 Units

Work in millimeters and export in millimeters. Many slicers assume millimeters, so a model exported in inches will arrive 25.4 times too small, and one exported in meters will look gigantic. Check the scale in the slicer's object info.

🧭 Orientation

Decide how the part will sit on the plate while you model it. Put the flattest, largest face down, keep load directions across layers where possible, and avoid steep overhangs that would need supports.

🧱 Wall thickness

Make walls at least two nozzle widths thick (≈ 0.8–1.2 mm on a 0.4 mm nozzle); structural walls of 2–3 mm are common. Thickness in multiples of your line width slices cleanly, with fewer gaps.

🔍 Export resolution

When exporting STL, choose a tessellation fine enough that curves look round (typically a chord deviation of 0.01–0.05 mm) but not so fine that the file becomes enormous. 3MF preserves units and colors; see File formats & mesh repair.

Reverse engineering and measuring

Many useful prints replace or adapt something that already exists. Measure carefully, model what you measure, then test-print a small section before committing to the full part.

  • Digital calipers (≈ ±0.02 mm) measure outer dimensions, hole diameters and depths. Measure three times, in different places; parts are rarely perfect.
  • Calipers + templates: trace the outline on paper, scan it flat, then import the scan as a sketch background and scale it to a known dimension.
  • Photos with a reference: photograph the part flat on graph paper or beside a coin or ruler, perpendicular to the camera, then calibrate the image scale inside CAD. Fine for flat parts such as gaskets and clips.
  • Photogrammetry: software builds a mesh from dozens of overlapping photos. Free and cheap, good for large or organic objects, but shiny, transparent and featureless surfaces fail.
  • 3D scanners (structured light, laser or phone LiDAR) produce a mesh quickly. Accuracy ranges from about a millimeter on phones to hundredths of a millimeter on professional units. Expect to clean, repair and then remodel critical features in CAD.

Text, logos and SVG import

Text and logos are 2D outlines that you extrude into 3D. Text tools in most CAD programs create a sketch from a font; pick a bold, simple font and keep strokes at least 0.8–1.2 mm wide (about two to three nozzle widths) so letters print cleanly. SVG import brings vector artwork into a sketch: clean the paths first (convert text to outlines, remove tiny details, close open shapes, simplify nodes), then extrude by 0.6–2 mm. Embossed (raised) text prints best on a top face; engraved text should be at least 0.6 mm deep. Raster images (PNG, JPEG) must first be traced into vectors. Mind font and logo licenses before selling anything that includes them.

Remixing models and licensing

Remixing means modifying someone else's model, often the fastest way to a solution. Download a mesh or, better, a native or STEP file, and modify it in CAD or a mesh editor. Before sharing or selling, check the license on the source page. Creative Commons licenses are common on model sites: CC0 (no restrictions), CC BY (credit required), CC BY-SA (credit and share-alike), NC (no commercial use) and ND (no derivatives, so no remixing for publication). Some sites use their own terms in addition. When in doubt, ask the author. See also repositories and licensing and Economics, licensing & sustainability.

This is general information, not legal advice

License terms vary by site and by country. A remix of an "ND" model, a model of a trademarked character, or a part derived from a patented design may be restricted even for personal sale. Keep a note of the source URL, author and license beside every remix.

AI text-to-3D and image-to-3D

Generative tools can turn a sentence or a photo into a 3D mesh in minutes. They are developing fast, and results vary widely by tool and prompt. For printing, treat them as a source of concepts and rough shapes, not as engineering drawings.

What they are good at

  • Quick ideas for figurines, props and decorative shapes
  • Starting meshes for sculpting in Blender or Nomad
  • Turning a photo into a rough 3D base

Where they struggle

  • Exact dimensions, holes and mating surfaces
  • Thin features, tiny shells and non-manifold geometry
  • Symmetry, text and mechanical logic (gears, hinges)
  • Unclear training-data and licensing situations; check the tool's terms

Typical clean-up: import the mesh, check for holes, non-manifold edges and wall thickness (see mesh anatomy), decimate or remesh, scale to real-world size, and rebuild any functional features, such as screw holes or mounting plates, in CAD. Do not rely on an AI-generated model for anything that bears load or safety.

Worked example: design a bracket in ten steps

We will make an L-shaped shelf bracket: a 40 × 30 mm leg pair, 4 mm thick, 30 mm wide, with two screw holes and a gusset. The steps are the same in Fusion, Onshape or FreeCAD; the menu names differ.

  1. Define parameters. Create user parameters for thickness t = 4, width w = 30, leg lengths a = 40 and b = 30, hole diameter d = 4.5 (3.5 mm screw plus clearance for countersink and print variation).
  2. Sketch the L-profile on the side plane: a base leg and an upright leg with a thickness of t. Start from the origin so the part sits at a known location.
  3. Constrain it. Use horizontal/vertical constraints, tie lengths to a, b and t, and confirm the sketch shows fully constrained.
  4. Extrude the profile symmetrically or one-sided by w.
  5. Add a gusset: sketch a right triangle in the inside corner, extrude it by t at the center, and join it to the body. It adds stiffness where the bracket is loaded.
  6. Add holes. Place one clearance hole per leg using the hole tool or a sketch circle cut through t. Keep at least 1.5× the hole diameter from any edge.
  7. Fillet the inside corner (1.5–3 mm) and add a small chamfer to the bottom edge. Apply fillets before adding holes and patterns, since fillets on complicated edges are the most likely feature to fail.
  8. Check orientation. Lay the bracket on its side, with the L profile flat on the plate, so the layer lines follow the bend. Printed upright, the corner would be loaded across its layer lines, which is where brackets crack. The trade-off is that the screw holes now point sideways, so use teardrop-shaped holes or let the slicer add small supports.
  9. Export a 3MF (or STL in millimeters) and save the native file. Also store the parameter list in your notes.
  10. Slice and test. Preview walls (3–4), infill (25–40 %), and orientation; print; test with real screws; adjust d or t and re-export.

Short code samples

Code-based modeling pays off when you need many variations. The two examples below produce the same bracket without a gusset fillet. They are deliberately short; see Parametric CAD workflow for more.

// L-bracket with gusset and two clearance holes (mm)
t = 4;  w = 30;  a = 40;  b = 30;  d = 4.5;
$fn = 48;

difference() {
  union() {
    cube([a, w, t]);                       // base leg
    cube([t, w, b]);                       // upright leg
    // gusset: right triangle extruded t, centered on the width
    translate([0, w/2 + t/2, 0])
      rotate([90, 0, 0])
        linear_extrude(t)
          polygon([[t, t], [a*0.6, t], [t, b*0.6]]);
  }
  // hole through the base, near its free end
  translate([a - 10, w/2, -1]) cylinder(d = d, h = t + 2);
  // hole through the upright, near its free end
  translate([-1, w/2, b - 10]) rotate([0, 90, 0]) cylinder(d = d, h = t + 2);
}
import cadquery as cq

t, w, a, b, d = 4, 30, 40, 30, 4.5      # mm

# L profile on the XZ plane, extruded along +Y by w
bracket = (
    cq.Workplane("XZ")
    .polyline([(0, 0), (a, 0), (a, t), (t, t), (t, b), (0, b)])
    .close()
    .extrude(-w)
    .edges("|Y").fillet(1.0)             # round the profile edges
)

# clearance holes: one per leg, centered on the width
hole_base = cq.Workplane("XY").center(a - 10, w / 2).circle(d / 2).extrude(t)
hole_wall = cq.Workplane("YZ").center(w / 2, b - 10).circle(d / 2).extrude(t)
bracket = bracket.cut(hole_base).cut(hole_wall)

cq.exporters.export(bracket, "bracket.step")   # real B-rep, opens in any CAD
cq.exporters.export(bracket, "bracket.stl", tolerance=0.01)
Which one first?

OpenSCAD is easier to start with (one program, instant preview). CadQuery and build123d give you real fillets, chamfers and STEP export, which makes them better for parts that need to be shared with other CAD users. Both keep your design in a text file, which works well with Git, as described in archiving and version control.

Choosing a tool for a specific job

When you start a project, a short decision path beats a long comparison. Ask what the object must do, how precise it must be, and how many variations you expect.

🔧 A sculpting workflow for printable figures
  1. Block out the proportions with low-polygon primitives. Keep the model in real-world size from the start (for example 80 mm tall).
  2. Sculpt forms with large brushes first, details later. Use symmetry for faces and bodies.
  3. Check printability: avoid razor-thin features such as hair strands or sword edges thinner than 0.8 mm; thicken them or plan to cast or paint them.
  4. Split into parts that print flat and glue or pin together (see Design patterns & recipes).
  5. Decimate and repair, then export. Details at the mesh level are covered in File formats & mesh repair.
🚀 Common beginner mistakes
  • Unconstrained sketches that shift when another dimension changes. Fix: constrain fully before extruding.
  • Modeling the "ideal" size without allowing for printer tolerance. Fix: use a named clearance parameter and test-print a coupon.
  • Ignoring orientation until the model is finished. Fix: decide on the print face first and design with it in mind.
  • Too many tiny features: ornamental details below 0.4 mm simply disappear. Fix: exaggerate details for the printer's resolution.
  • Exporting at the wrong tessellation, producing visible facets on round parts. Fix: lower the chord deviation or export 3MF/STEP.
  • Losing the source file. The STL is not editable. Fix: keep the native file or script under version control.
  • Over-modeling. A screw thread or a knurl can be created by the slicer's fuzzy skin or by a printed insert nut at a fraction of the effort. Fix: ask whether a feature needs to exist at all.
🔰 STEP, STL, 3MF: which should I export from CAD?

STEP keeps exact geometry for other CAD users and is ideal for sharing or archiving, but a slicer cannot read it directly in many cases (some can import it and tessellate). STL is a plain triangle soup: universal, but unitless and without color or settings. 3MF is a modern container with units, multiple objects, colors, materials and, in slicer projects, print settings. Export 3MF to the slicer when you can; keep STEP (or the native file) as your master. A deeper comparison is in File formats & mesh repair.

Quick self-check

You need a hole diameter you can change in one place for ten variants of a part. Which approach fits best?

  • Sculpt each variant in Blender
  • A parametric model or code with a named parameter
  • Download an STL and scale it

Named parameters (or code variables) propagate to every feature that references them.

A model you downloaded arrives 25.4 times smaller than expected. Most likely cause?

  • The slicer's layer height is too low
  • Unit mismatch (inches treated as millimeters)
  • A non-manifold edge

Inches exported as raw numbers look like millimeters, so everything shrinks by 25.4×.

Which is the best use of AI text-to-3D for printing?

  • A load-bearing bracket with exact bolt spacing
  • A rough starting shape for a decorative figure that you then clean up
  • A replacement gear for a machine

AI meshes are unreliable at exact dimensions; use them as concept bases, not for mechanical parts.

Key takeaways

  • Pick the paradigm first: sculpt for looks, parametric for fit, code for families, AI for ideas.
  • Constrain sketches, name the parameters that matter, and order features logically.
  • Model in millimeters, add clearances as parameters and orient for the print.
  • Measure real parts with calipers and test-print critical sections before the whole object.
  • Check the license of anything you remix and keep the source and author noted.
  • Keep the editable source file; export 3MF or STEP/STL only as deliverables.
