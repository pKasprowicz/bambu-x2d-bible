Slicers compared

What a slicer does

🔰 The plain-language version

Your printer cannot understand a 3D model directly. A model (usually an STL or 3MF file) is only a description of a surface: a skin made of thousands of small triangles. A printer, on the other hand, needs a long list of simple instructions such as "move to X=100, Y=80 while pushing out 0.4 mm of plastic". The program that converts one into the other is called a slicer, because its core trick is to cut the model into thin horizontal layers, much like slicing a loaf of bread, and then work out how to draw each slice with a thin line of molten plastic.

The output of a slicer is G-code (see G-code, firmware & ecosystem), a text-like list of commands that the printer firmware executes line by line. Everything you set in the slicer (layer height, wall count, infill, speeds, temperatures, supports) ends up as numbers inside this file.

🔧 What the slicer actually computes

Behind the friendly buttons a slicer solves several distinct problems, and knowing them helps you understand why a setting exists:

  1. Intersect the mesh with planes.For each layer height step the slicer finds where the triangles cross the plane. The result is a set of closed 2D outlines (polygons). Holes become inner outlines. This is why a mesh must be watertight: an open edge means the outline cannot close (see File formats & mesh repair).
  2. Classify regions.Each polygon is analyzed against the layers above and below. Areas with nothing below are overhangs (they need support or bridging), areas with air above are top surfaces, and the rest is interior.
  3. Generate perimeters (walls).The outline is offset inward by one line width at a time to create the outer wall and inner walls. Modern slicers use variable width algorithms (Arachne) to fill thin features cleanly.
  4. Fill the interior.The remaining area gets sparse infill in a chosen pattern, plus solid infill for the top and bottom shells.
  5. Plan supports, brims and skirts.Support columns, interface layers, and adhesion aids are generated per layer.
  6. Order and optimize the paths.The slicer decides where each loop starts (the seam), which line to print first, when to retract, when to lift the nozzle and how to avoid crossing walls.
  7. Assign speeds, temperatures and cooling.Each feature type gets its speed and acceleration, layers that finish too fast are slowed, and fan speeds are set.
  8. Emit G-code.Finally, every path is converted to G1 moves with an extrusion amount calculated from line width, layer height and filament diameter.
How much plastic is "E"?

The slicer calculates the extrusion for each move from the volume of the line. A 0.42 mm wide, 0.2 mm tall line that is 10 mm long has a volume of about 0.42 × 0.2 × 10 ≈ 0.84 mm³ (a rounded-rectangle model gives slightly less). A 1.75 mm filament has a cross-section of about 2.405 mm², so the feed length is about 0.35 mm. Multiply by your flow ratio and you have the E value in the G-code.

The slicer landscape

There are dozens of slicers, but almost all of them descend from a few families. Knowing the lineage tells you which interface and feature set to expect.

Meet the slicers

Bambu Studio

Free, open source (AGPL), by Bambu Lab. Best integration with Bambu printers: cloud/LAN sending, AMS color mapping, MakerWorld, filament RFID profiles, and the X2D's dual-nozzle logic. Based on PrusaSlicer's code base.

OrcaSlicer

Free, open source fork of Bambu Studio with support for many brands (Bambu, Prusa, Creality, Voron/Klipper, Anycubic…). Famous for its built-in calibration wizards (flow, pressure advance, temperature, retraction, max volumetric speed) and extra tuning options.

PrusaSlicer

Free, open source, by Prusa Research. Mature and polished, excellent documentation, strong profiles for Prusa printers, variable layer height, modifiers, multi-material and Arachne wall generator. Also runs other Marlin and Klipper printers.

UltiMaker Cura

Free, open source, huge community and by far the largest list of ready-made machine profiles. Simple "recommended" mode, deep "custom" mode with thousands of settings, and a plug-in marketplace (post-processing scripts, Arachne origin).

SuperSlicer

Community fork of PrusaSlicer with extra expert options: built-in calibration prints, more infill patterns, more seam and thin-wall tweaks. Development is slower than the main projects, so check activity before committing to it.

Simplify3D

Commercial, paid slicer from the early 2010s era, once the go-to "pro" tool with powerful manual support placement and process-per-layer-range. Updates have been sparse; today most users have moved to free alternatives. Treat as legacy.

Kiri:Moto & online slicers

Browser-based slicers (Kiri:Moto is the best known) need no installation, run on Chromebooks and tablets, and are handy for a quick job. Limited calibration, profile and multi-material depth compared with desktop apps.

Vendor slicers

Creality Print, Elegoo Slicer, Anycubic Slicer, Flashforge/Qidi tools and others are usually branded forks of Cura, Orca or Bambu Studio with the manufacturer's profiles pre-loaded. Convenient out of the box; often lag behind upstream.

Others worth knowing

IdeaMaker (Raise3D, closed source), MatterControl, Slic3r PE legacy, Ultimaker Digital Factory (cloud), and resin slicers such as Lychee or ChiTuBox (different technology, covered in Printing processes).

Bambu Studio, Orca and the lineage

If you learn Bambu Studio you can pick up OrcaSlicer in an hour, because the layout, setting names and project format are nearly identical. The reverse is also true. PrusaSlicer uses different tab structure (Print Settings / Filament Settings / Printer Settings) but the same underlying vocabulary.

Profiles, presets and project files

🔰 Three kinds of preset

Almost every slicer separates its settings into three bundles. Understanding the split removes most "where did my setting go?" confusion.

🖨️ Printer (machine)

Bed size, nozzle diameter, kinematics limits, max speeds and accelerations, retraction defaults, start/end G-code, number of extruders. Changes only when you change hardware (or nozzle).

🧵 Filament

Temperatures, flow ratio, pressure advance or flow dynamics, fan curves, max volumetric speed, retraction overrides, drying hints, filament cost and density. One per material (and often per brand).

🎚️ Process (print)

Layer height, walls, infill, supports, speeds, seam, adhesion. The settings you change most often. Typically one per quality level such as 0.12 Fine, 0.20 Standard, 0.28 Draft.

Never open G-code from strangers blindly

G-code is a program for a machine with a heater and motors. A shared .gcode file that was sliced for another printer can crash into limits or set unsafe temperatures. Prefer sharing .3mf projects and slicing them yourself with your own printer preset, and read the start and end G-code if a file came from an unknown source.

Importing, exporting and sharing profiles

  1. Modify a preset.Change values in the settings panel; changed fields get a marker. Click the save icon next to the preset name and give it a descriptive name such as PETG-CF 0.4 – strong.
  2. Inherit, do not copy.Your user preset inherits from a system preset. Updating the app updates the parent and keeps your differences, which is exactly what you want.
  3. Export.Use File → Export → Export Preset Bundle (or the per-preset export). Slicers write a .json, .ini or a zip. Filament profiles can be exported individually.
  4. Import.On another computer use File → Import → Import Configs (menu names vary). Check the printer and nozzle assigned to the profile before use.
  5. Share the project instead of settings when you can.A .3mf carries the settings for exactly that model and gets people to a working state faster than a list of numbers.
  6. Back up.Presets live in a user data folder (for example under your user profile or AppData). Include it in your regular backup; it holds months of tuning.
Naming that scales

Use a naming pattern like Brand Material nozzle@printer for filaments (Bambu PLA Basic @X2D 0.4) and layer height – purpose for process presets (0.16 – visual, 0.24 – functional). You will thank yourself when you have thirty of them.

Reading the preview

The preview is the single most underused feature in slicing. It shows the exact paths the printer will follow, layer by layer, and you can scrub through them with the layer slider. Spend one minute here before every print that matters; it catches failed supports, missing walls, strange travel moves and "this will take 31 hours" surprises.

What to look for, every time

  • Scrub to layer 1: does the first layer cover the whole footprint, and is the brim where you want it?
  • Scrub through overhangs and bridges: is support placed where you expect it, and is anything floating without it?
  • Look at thin walls and small gaps: are there missing or sparse areas where the mesh is thinner than two lines?
  • Check the seam column: is it a neat line at the back or a scar down the front face?
  • Switch to speed or flow view: any extreme values on tiny features or on infill?
  • Read the estimate: time and filament weight plausible? Too much waste for a multi-color print?
  • For multi-color: does the color-print view show every part in the correct AMS slot?

Working on the plate

🔰 The essential tools

Move, rotate, scale

Hotkeys: M/R/S in most slicers. Use "place on face" to lay a flat surface on the bed. Scale with caution: uniform scaling changes wall counts and hole fits; non-uniform scaling changes proportions of threads and text.

Auto-orient & arrange

Auto-orient picks an angle that reduces overhang area; arrange packs objects with spacing for brim, prime tower and nozzle clearance. Good starting suggestions, but always judge strength and visible faces yourself.

Cut tool

Slice a model with a plane to print in parts, to lay a flat face on the bed or to hide seams. Many slicers add pegs or dovetail connectors so the pieces register and glue easily. See the figure below.

Text / emboss & shapes

Add text, simple shapes and SVG-based logos directly on a face. Great for labels. Keep line width in mind: letters narrower than about 2 nozzle widths get fuzzy; use bold fonts and at least 0.8 mm raised depth.

🔧 Per-object settings and modifiers

The global process preset applies to the whole plate. Right-click an object to give it its own settings: more walls for a load-bearing bracket, a thinner layer for a figurine, or support only on one object. A modifier is a shape (box, cylinder, sphere, or an imported part) placed inside or around an object; any setting you attach to it applies only where the shape overlaps the model. Typical uses:

  • A modifier cylinder around a bolt hole with 100% infill and extra walls to resist crushing.
  • A box over a thin region with lower speed, or a taller layer range with a different infill pattern.
  • A support blocker to prevent useless support, or a support enforcer to add it in one tricky spot.

A height-range modifier changes settings between two Z heights, which is great for inserting a pause, changing filament, or tweaking temperature at a specific layer. Modifiers are explored further in Advanced techniques.

🔧 Painting: supports, seams, colors

Most modern slicers include "paint-on" tools that let you brush onto the 3D model:

Paint-on supports

Brush enforcers (where support must exist) or blockers (where it must not). Use a smart-fill option to select whole faces by angle threshold.

Seam painting

Paint where the layer start/stop scars may be placed or avoided. Ideal for hiding the seam in a sharp inner corner or on the back.

Color painting

For multi-color printing, brush filaments directly onto faces or fill a region by surface angle. The slicer figures out the layer-by-layer toolpath; the printer swaps filament or nozzle as needed. See Multi-color & multi-material.

Seam hiding trick

Paint a thin vertical strip on a sharp outside corner as the "preferred" seam zone. Round faces always show a seam; sharp corners hide it because the nozzle's start and stop blob gets absorbed into the corner geometry.

A reliable slicing workflow

Best-practice checklist

  1. Verify the model first.Check dimensions in the slicer (it shows the size in mm), unit scale (a model in inches imports 25.4× too small), and any warnings about open edges. See File formats & mesh repair.
  2. Orient for the load, not the looks.Layers are weakest between each other. Orient so forces run along layers, and so that critical holes are vertical (round) rather than horizontal (teardrop). See Design principles and Strength & orientation.
  3. Confirm the three presets.Correct printer and nozzle, the exact filament you loaded (not just "PLA"), and a process that suits the goal.
  4. Change a few things on purpose.Start from a known-good process preset. Adjust walls, infill and supports with intent; consult Slicer settings explained for what each does.
  5. Slice, then read all warnings.Messages such as "object floating", "thin walls ignored" and "flush volume large" are free advice.
  6. Preview with the checklist above.Always check layer 1 and the first overhang.
  7. Print, and watch the first layer.Most failures are visible within the first 3–5 minutes. Watch via camera or in person.
  8. Save and annotate.Save the project; write the outcome in the file name or a notes field.
Common mistakes
  • Slicing for the wrong nozzle (0.4 preset, 0.6 nozzle installed). The first layer will be wrong in width and flow.
  • Using a "Generic PLA" preset for a silk or wood-filled PLA, which needs different temperatures and speeds.
  • Editing settings while a preset marked "system" is selected and not saving, so changes disappear on restart.
  • Trusting auto-support blindly on overhangs that are better solved by reorienting.
  • Never updating the slicer, or updating in the middle of a project without a backup; settings names sometimes change between major versions.

Cloud, LAN and other ways to send a job

  • Cloud sending is the easiest: log in once and send from anywhere, with remote monitoring in the app. The trade-off is that your files and camera stream pass through the manufacturer's servers.
  • LAN mode keeps everything inside your home network; you enter the printer's address and its access code in the slicer. Typically faster for big files and independent of the internet.
  • USB / SD card is the lowest-tech and most robust. Good when the printer is isolated on purpose.
  • Host software such as OctoPrint, Mainsail or Fluidd gives queueing, timelapse and plug-ins on printers that run open firmware.

When to switch slicers

You do not need to be loyal. Reasons to try another slicer:

  • A bug or limitation blocks you (say, a seam bug on your geometry). Slice the same 3MF in a second slicer to see if the issue is software or physical.
  • You bought a different brand of printer and the vendor profiles are in another slicer.
  • You want better calibration tools (Orca) or documented stability (PrusaSlicer).
  • You need a feature, e.g. a particular infill, wall ordering or support style that only one slicer offers.
Moving projects between slicers

A 3MF from Bambu Studio opens cleanly in OrcaSlicer, with settings mapped by name. Going to PrusaSlicer or Cura keeps geometry and often object placement, but process settings are mostly lost; plan to rebuild your presets. Painted colors and supports travel only between close relatives.

Bambu Studio specifics for the X2D

Bambu Studio is the reference slicer for Bambu printers. Rather than repeat everything about the X2D here, this section highlights what is different from a "generic" slicer. For deeper coverage go to Bambu Studio settings & profiles and Dual-nozzle printing.

What changes with a dual-nozzle X2D
  • Two nozzles, two extruders. The left nozzle is the main hotend with a direct-drive servo extruder in the toolhead; the right nozzle is the auxiliary hotend, fed by a stepper-driven auxiliary extruder at the back via a PTFE tube. Bambu Studio assigns each filament to a nozzle ("left" or "right") and shows the assignment in the plate and preview.
  • Build area differs by nozzle. Printing with the main nozzle uses the full 256 × 256 × 260 mm volume; the area both nozzles can reach is 235.5 × 256 × 256 mm. The slicer warns when an object cannot be reached by the nozzle it is assigned to.
  • Auxiliary limits. The auxiliary extruder is limited to about 200 mm/s and roughly 1000 mm/s² acceleration, and needs longer retraction and a much larger flow-dynamics K value than the main nozzle. Expect slightly lower quality on fine detail from the auxiliary nozzle and keep it for supports, secondary colors or less demanding parts.
  • Flow Dynamics Calibration applies to the main nozzle only; the auxiliary nozzle uses built-in values.
  • Fewer purges. Switching nozzles replaces many filament changes, so flush volumes and prime-tower waste can be much lower than with a single-nozzle AMS printer. The Dual-nozzle chapter explains when the slicer reduces tower use automatically.
  • Filament mapping. The AMS 2 Pro gives you four slots per unit. Bambu Studio's Filament Track Switch / dynamic mapping logic decides which path (left or right nozzle) takes each AMS slot; review the mapping dialog before every multi-color job.

X2D-aware tips

  • Pick the printer and nozzle in the preset bar first; filament and process presets filter by what the printer supports.
  • Use presets with RFID-tagged Bambu filament so the slicer syncs the type, color and remaining spool length from the AMS.
  • Print supports from the auxiliary nozzle if you want an easy-peel interface material, for example a different PLA or PETG support interface under a main-nozzle ABS part, subject to the compatibility table in Filament compatibility.
  • Mind the plate type setting. If the plate selected in the slicer differs from the plate actually installed, the printer may refuse or warn before starting.
  • Check the Device tab for live camera, AMS drying status and printer messages once the job is sent.

Quick self-check

Which statement best describes G-code?

  • A 3D model description made of triangles
  • A list of machine commands that the firmware executes line by line
  • A setting preset for the slicer

The mesh describes the shape; G-code describes the motion and extrusion that produces it.

You want to share a complete job with a friend so they get the same settings. What should you send?

  • The STL file
  • A project .3mf
  • A screenshot of the settings

The project file embeds meshes, plate layout, per-object overrides and the presets used.

A per-object setting overrides which level of settings?

  • The global process preset for that object only
  • The printer firmware
  • Nothing; it is cosmetic

Per-object settings and modifiers are the most specific scope and win over the global values.

Key takeaways

  • A slicer converts a mesh into G-code: slice into layers, generate walls and infill, add supports, order the paths, write commands.
  • Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer and PrusaSlicer share a lineage; Cura is separate but covers the broadest printer list.
  • Presets come in three layers (printer, filament, process) with per-object overrides on top.
  • Share .3mf projects instead of raw G-code; treat unknown G-code with suspicion.
  • The preview is your cheapest quality-control step; use the legends and the layer slider every time.
  • On the X2D, filament-to-nozzle assignment, build-area limits and auxiliary-nozzle limits are the new things to learn.

Practice: Level 1 exercises include slicing and preview tasks, and X2D challenges use the dual-nozzle workflow.
