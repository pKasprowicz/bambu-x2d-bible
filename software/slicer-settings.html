Slicer settings explained

Hundreds of sliders, dozens that matter, a handful that decide most of your results. This page explains each important setting in plain language, with typical values, the trade-offs, and pictures of what actually changes inside the print.

BeginnerIntermediateAdvanced⏱ 55 min read
How to use this page
  • Skim the priority pyramid first. It tells you where to spend your attention.
  • Each setting lives in a collapsible panel with the same four rows: What it does, Typical, Change it when, Trade-offs. Names vary slightly between Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, PrusaSlicer and Cura; the descriptions use the most common wording.
  • Values are starting points for a 0.4 mm nozzle on a well-tuned printer. Tune from there, one change at a time, and check the starting profiles at the bottom.

Where to focus: the priority pyramid

A slicer exposes an overwhelming number of settings, but they do not all matter equally. A wrong filament temperature ruins a print; a slightly different seam gap does not. Work from the base of the pyramid upward: only fine-tune the tip after everything beneath it is right.

🔰 Vocabulary you will see everywhere

Perimeter / wall / loop

A closed line of plastic following the outline of a layer. "Wall loops" and "perimeters" mean the same thing.

Infill

The pattern that fills the inside of the part, mostly air with thin lines of plastic to hold the walls up.

Shell

The solid skin: walls plus the top and bottom solid layers. Together they carry most of the strength.

Line width

How wide each extruded line is, normally 100–125 % of the nozzle diameter.

Extrusion multiplier / flow ratio

A scale factor on the amount of plastic extruded; 1.0 = theoretical amount, tuned per filament.

Volumetric speed

Plastic volume per second (mm³/s); the true limit of a hotend, more meaningful than mm/s.

Anatomy of a printed part

Before the individual settings, picture what a slice looks like from the side. Every setting below acts on some part of this cross-section.

Quality settings

These control the resolution and finish of the surface: how smooth, how accurate and how clean it looks.

Layer height

🔰 Layer height0.08–0.32 mm (0.4 mm nozzle: 0.20 mm)
What it does
Sets the thickness of each layer (the Z distance between slice planes). Thinner layers make smaller steps on curved and sloped surfaces; thicker layers print faster.
Typical
0.12 mm fine, 0.16 mm quality, 0.20 mm standard, 0.24 mm functional, 0.28–0.32 mm draft. A good range is 25–75 % of the nozzle diameter; 0.4 mm nozzles work from roughly 0.08 to 0.30 mm.
Change it when
Fine details, small text, curved figures or smooth threads call for 0.08–0.16 mm. Large functional parts that will be sanded or hidden suit 0.24–0.32 mm. Tall thin prints can benefit from thicker layers to reduce vibration time.
Trade-offs
Print time scales roughly with 1/layer height: halving it about doubles print time. Thick layers have stronger inter-layer bonds per line (more contact area per line) but visible banding; very thin layers on a 0.4 nozzle may have flow problems.
Adaptive / variable layer height

Rather than one layer height for the whole part, let the slicer use thin layers on curved or shallow areas and thick layers on vertical walls. In Bambu Studio and OrcaSlicer it is under the "adaptive layer height" or "variable layer height" tool; in Cura it is "Use adaptive layers". Expect 10–40 % time saving compared with using the fine height everywhere.

First-layer height, width and speed

🔰 Initial layer height0.20–0.30 mm
What it does
Sets the thickness of the very first layer. A slightly thicker first layer is more forgiving of a bed that is a little uneven, and sticks better because the line is squeezed wide.
Typical
0.20 mm on a 0.4 mm nozzle (0.25–0.30 mm if your bed is not perfectly flat or you print big parts). Some slicers also set a wider first-layer line (e.g. 120–150 % of nozzle).
Change it when
Poor adhesion on large parts, slightly uneven plates, or textured plates that need more squish. Thinner (0.12–0.16 mm) for very smooth, precise bottoms on a perfectly calibrated bed.
Trade-offs
Thicker first layer = more tolerance but a rougher bottom and a larger elephant's foot. First-layer speed should be slow (20–50 mm/s) for good contact.

The first layer is everything. See First layer & bed adhesion for squish, cleaning, glue and calibration.

Line width

🔧 Line width (per feature)100–125 % of the nozzle
What it does
Controls how wide each extruded line is. The slicer flattens the round filament into a rounded rectangle, so line width and layer height together decide how much plastic is laid down per mm.
Typical
Outer wall 0.40–0.42 mm (about 100–105 %) for crisp edges; inner wall, infill and top surface 0.42–0.50 mm; first layer 0.42–0.50 mm. For a 0.4 mm nozzle the usable range is roughly 0.35–0.60 mm.
Change it when
Wider lines fill thin gaps and make faster, stronger infill; narrower outer lines resolve fine detail. Using 0.6 mm lines from a 0.4 mm nozzle is a classic speed hack, but only if the hotend can melt enough plastic.
Trade-offs
Wider = more flow needed, faster fill, stronger bond to neighbors, softer corners. Narrower = more detail, more lines (slower), and a risk of gaps if narrower than the nozzle opening.

Seam position and scarf seams

Every closed loop has a start and end. The point where they meet leaves a small scar called the seam (the "z-seam"). You cannot remove it, but you can choose where it appears.

🔧 Seam positionAligned · Nearest · Back · Random
What it does
Chooses where each layer's outer wall starts and stops. Aligned stacks the seams into a single vertical line; nearest starts at the closest point to the previous move (hides in corners but scatters); back puts them at the back; random spreads them everywhere, avoiding a clear line.
Typical
Aligned or back for decorative parts so the scar is a single line you can sand or face away. Random for round parts that must look uniform from all sides. Nearest for quick draft prints. Seam gap 0–10 % of the nozzle diameter.
Change it when
The seam shows as a blob or gap on a visible face; you want to hide the seam in a concave corner; you plan to paint or sand.
Trade-offs
Random hides the line but makes the whole surface slightly rough. Aligned is neat and sandable but looks like a visible stripe. Seam quality also depends on pressure advance and retraction.
🚀 Scarf seam (Orca / Bambu "scarf joint seam")length ≈ 10–20 mm, start height 0.3–0.5 layer
What it does
Instead of ending the loop abruptly, the line tapers: the first part of the outer wall ramps up in height (and the last part ramps down) so that the two ends overlap along a diagonal "scarf" joint, like a woodworker's scarf joint. It reduces the visible bump and gap.
Typical
Enable on smooth, round or flat vertical outer walls; scarf length 10–20 mm; start height about 0.3–0.5 of a layer; use on outer wall only. Often combined with "seam gap" 0 and slower outer wall.
Change it when
You print smooth cylinders, vases or product-style parts and the straight seam bothers you.
Trade-offs
Slightly longer print, requires well-tuned flow and pressure advance, may cause artifacts on tiny perimeters, and can leave a faint diagonal mark. Not useful on very small loops where the scarf would be longer than the perimeter.

Ironing, fuzzy skin and arc fitting

🔧 Ironingflow 8–15 %, speed 15–40 mm/s, spacing 0.1–0.25 mm
What it does
After the top surface is printed, the nozzle makes an extra pass at the same height with very little extrusion, smoothing out the ridges between lines (like ironing a shirt).
Typical
Ironing type "top surfaces only"; flow 10–15 %; speed 20–30 mm/s; line spacing 0.10–0.20 mm; works best with PLA and PETG, poorly with very stringy or soft materials.
Change it when
Flat top faces need a glossy, smooth finish (lids, nameplates, sliding surfaces). Disable for strength-only parts and for textured finishes.
Trade-offs
Adds substantial print time on large flat areas (often +20–60 % on lid-like parts), can leave scratches if the flow is too low or too high, and will smudge fuzzy-skin or lines intended to be a pattern.
🔧 Fuzzy skinpoint distance 0.3–0.8 mm, thickness 0.1–0.3 mm
What it does
Randomly jitters the outer wall in and out as it prints, creating a rough, matte, tactile surface that hides layer lines, fingerprints and imperfections.
Typical
Point distance 0.4–0.8 mm; thickness 0.1–0.3 mm. Apply to the outer wall only ("outside only"), sometimes with a modifier on grips and handles.
Change it when
You need grip, anti-glare surfaces, or want to mask layer lines on large objects.
Trade-offs
Reduces dimensional accuracy, weakens detail, slows printing slightly, and creates rough spots that collect dirt. Never use on precise fit surfaces.
🚀 Arc fitting (G2/G3)on/off, tolerance ≈ 0.05 mm
What it does
Replaces runs of tiny straight segments on curves with arc commands (G2/G3), shrinking the G-code file and reducing the stream of tiny moves the controller has to process.
Typical
Off for most users on Bambu printers (the slicer already outputs a compact, tuned stream). On for Marlin or Klipper machines with limited serial/processing bandwidth or stutter on high-resolution curves.
Change it when
You see surface blobs on fine curves due to controller bottlenecks; or your G-code is huge and you print over a slow connection.
Trade-offs
Requires firmware support for arcs. A tolerance that is too large can slightly distort circles. See G-code & firmware for how arcs work.

Strength settings

A printed part is a laminate of lines. Its strength depends far more on walls and orientation than on infill, which surprises many beginners. The usual order of influence is: orientation → wall count → top/bottom layers → infill density → infill pattern. See Strength & orientation for the physics of layer adhesion.

Walls (perimeters)

🔰 Wall loops / perimeters2–6 loops (3 for standard)
What it does
Sets how many concentric outlines are printed around each layer. The outermost shows on the surface; the inner ones carry load and give the part its stiffness.
Typical
2 for decorative/draft, 3 for general use, 4–6 for functional parts that bend, clamp or hold screws. A wall total of about 1.2–2.4 mm is a good target regardless of nozzle size.
Change it when
Parts break at thin sections (add walls); you need a lightweight decorative piece (use 2); screws or inserts go into the part (use 4+ so the wall, not the infill, grips the thread).
Trade-offs
Each extra wall adds time and material but gives a bigger strength gain per gram than infill does. Beyond ~5 walls the interior is mostly solid anyway, so also consider raising infill only if loads act inside the part.
🔧 Wall printing order & directioninner/outer, outer/inner, inner/outer/inner
What it does
Chooses whether the outer wall prints first, last, or sandwiched. Inner-first lets the outer wall rest on an already-laid inner line and typically gives the best dimensional accuracy and surface; outer-first improves overhang quality and reduces visible bulging at tiny details; inner/outer/inner is a compromise.
Typical
Inner → outer for most prints. Outer → inner for threads and fine, precise exterior details if layer adhesion is not a concern.
Change it when
You see wall ripples ("ghost" lines) or bumps at seams, or an overhang that sags.
Trade-offs
Changes seam appearance and dimensional bias by tens of microns; test on a small calibration piece.

Wall generator: Classic vs Arachne

The wall generator is the algorithm that builds the loops. Classic offsets the outline inward in constant-width steps; where a feature is narrower than a whole number of lines it leaves a gap or overlaps. Arachne (a variable-width-line algorithm, originally developed for Cura and adopted by PrusaSlicer, Bambu Studio and OrcaSlicer) fits lines of varying width so that thin features and tapered shapes are filled without gaps.

🔧 Wall generator choiceArachne (default in most modern presets) or Classic
What it does
Selects the algorithm. Arachne also exposes options such as minimum feature size, minimum wall width and the wall-transition length/angle that control when the line count switches.
Typical
Arachne for general use, including thin walls and organic shapes. Classic for ultra-predictable constant-width loops, e.g. precise calibration or when comparing with older results.
Change it when
You see gaps in thin features or odd width changes; or an old profile behaves differently after updating.
Trade-offs
Arachne can create noisy flow changes on small features (line width oscillation) and larger G-code; Classic is predictable but leaves gaps. Tune pressure advance well if using Arachne.

Top and bottom shell layers

🔰 Top shell & bottom shell layerstop 4–7 layers, bottom 3–5 layers (≥ 0.8–1.2 mm thick)
What it does
Specifies how many solid layers cap the infill. The top shell bridges over the sparse infill; the bottom shell forms the plate-contact skin.
Typical
Aim for a shell thickness of 0.8–1.2 mm (e.g. 5 layers at 0.2 mm). Raise to 1.5–2.0 mm for waterproof or pressurized parts. Many slicers can enforce a minimum shell thickness in mm instead of counts, which keeps results consistent across layer heights.
Change it when
You see "pillowing" (bumpy, holey top surfaces) or low-density infill showing through; at 0.08–0.12 mm layers the layer count must be higher to reach the same thickness.
Trade-offs
More layers = stronger, smoother tops and better sealing, but slower. Too few top layers over low infill density cause sagging and gaps; this is usually fixed by 1–2 more layers or more infill under the top.
🚀 Top surface pattern & bottom surface patternmonotonic · monotonic lines · concentric · Hilbert · Archimedean
What it does
Controls how the visible solid top (and bottom) is filled. Monotonic prints all lines in one direction for a uniform look with no overlapping stripes; concentric echoes the outline; space-filling curves (Hilbert, Archimedean chords) distribute stress evenly and eliminate direction bias.
Typical
Monotonic (or "monotonic lines") for the top surface of most parts; rectilinear for the bottom. Use concentric for round parts.
Change it when
Lines show a visible stripe pattern, or flatness/reflection quality matters.
Trade-offs
Fancy space-filling patterns can be slower and show corners; monotonic is a reliable default.

Infill density and pattern

🔰 Sparse infill density0–100 % (decorative 5–10 %, general 15–20 %, functional 30–50 %)
What it does
Sets how much of the interior volume is plastic versus air. It is the fill that supports the top layers and shares compression and bending loads.
Typical
10–15 % for display parts; 15–20 % standard; 25–40 % for functional brackets; 60–100 % only for tiny parts, screw bosses or where weight is irrelevant. With 3+ walls and 20 % infill, most parts are already stiff enough for ordinary use.
Change it when
Compression-loaded parts (higher), flexible/soft TPU (lower), parts that float or need low weight (lower), and when material is cheap and time is not (higher). For tall columns subject to crushing, raise the wall count first.
Trade-offs
Infill time and weight grow almost linearly with density, but strength grows sub-linearly: doubling infill from 20 % to 40 % gives much less than double the stiffness. Density also changes sound, heat dissipation and perceived quality.
🔧 Sparse infill patternGyroid · Cubic · Grid · Lines · Triangles · Honeycomb · Lightning · TPMS
What it does
Decides the geometric shape of the sparse interior. Patterns differ in strength direction, print speed, noise, material use and how well they support top layers.
Typical
Gyroid (isotropic, good strength to weight, smooth motion), cubic or grid (fast, simple), lightning (only supports top surfaces: minimal material for display pieces), honeycomb (even, classic, slower), TPMS variants (Fischer-Koch, Schwarz diamond; continuous lattices, good for impact absorption and heat exchange).
Change it when
You need uniform strength in all directions (gyroid/cubic), you want the fastest prints (lines, grid, lightning), or you need impact damping (TPMS/gyroid).
Trade-offs
Gyroid and TPMS use curved paths that are slightly slower and larger in G-code; grid overlaps lines at intersections (extra flow and bumps); lightning has weak bending performance beyond supporting the top.

🚀 Solid infill thresholds & internal solid infillthreshold area ≈ 2–5 cm², infill/wall overlap ≈ 10–25 %
What it does
When a small region of sparse infill would be tiny (below a threshold area, often a few cm²), the slicer converts it to solid infill, because sparse infill in a small pocket just adds travel and weak spots. Other related settings: infill/wall overlap (how much infill is pushed into the walls for bonding), infill anchor length (extra stub that ties infill lines into walls), and ensure vertical shell thickness (adds solid layers where walls are thin or steep).
Typical
Keep the default threshold (about 2–5 cm²). Infill/wall overlap 10–25 % for good bonding without ghosting; anchor 400 % of line width or a few mm. Enable "ensure vertical shell thickness" for strength and sealing.
Change it when
You see infill pattern ghosting through thin walls (reduce overlap), weak infill-to-wall bond (increase overlap/anchor), or spongy tiny pockets (raise the threshold).
Trade-offs
More solid area = more weight and time; less overlap = cleaner surface, weaker bond. Tune for the filament: stiff materials hold a cleaner surface; soft ones need stronger anchoring.

Why walls beat infill

In bending, the outer fibers carry the largest stress, and the stiffness of a hollow section grows with the cube of its thickness, so thick outer shells contribute disproportionately. The core carries comparatively little load. Adding infill mainly helps in compression and in resisting local crushing, not in bending. This is the same reason a tube is nearly as stiff as a solid rod at a fraction of the weight.

Speed, acceleration and cooling

Speed is the most misunderstood group of settings. The headline "mm/s" number is only a ceiling; what you actually achieve depends on acceleration, the hotend's ability to melt plastic, and how long each move is. The deeper physics is in Speed, cooling & quality.

Per-feature speeds

Slicers let you set a separate speed for each feature type, because different features have different priorities: outer walls need precision, infill needs throughput, overhangs need cooling time.

If you print with the right (auxiliary) nozzle of an X2D, its extruder is limited to roughly 200 mm/s and about 1000 mm/s² acceleration, so the fast-CoreXY numbers above do not apply to features printed by the auxiliary nozzle. See Dual-nozzle printing.

Acceleration, jerk and junction deviation

🔧 Acceleration (mm/s²)print 1,000–20,000 depending on printer
What it does
Sets how quickly the toolhead speeds up and slows down. It limits the real speed on short moves, which never reach the set cruise speed; a printer set to 300 mm/s that spends most of its time on tiny moves is really running much slower.
Typical
Bed-slingers: 500–3,000 mm/s². Light CoreXY printers with input shaping: 5,000–20,000. Outer wall accelerations are usually set lower (e.g. 2,000–5,000) than infill (10,000+). Bambu and Klipper printers tune this together with vibration compensation (input shaping).
Change it when
Ringing (ghosting) appears behind sharp features, or the printer rattles; reduce acceleration on tall, thin, wobbly parts.
Trade-offs
Higher accelerations = faster prints, more ringing and more load on belts and frame. Use lower accelerations for the outer wall and top surface to protect surface quality.
🚀 Jerk / junction deviation / square-corner velocityjerk 5–10 mm/s · junction deviation 0.01–0.05 mm · SCV 5–10 mm/s
What it does
Defines how fast the toolhead may take a corner without stopping. Classic jerk is the instantaneous speed change allowed; junction deviation (Marlin 2.x) and square corner velocity (Klipper) do the same job using corner geometry: sharper corners cause lower corner speeds.
Typical
Leave at the printer vendor's defaults unless you troubleshoot. Common: jerk 5–10 mm/s on Marlin; junction deviation 0.013–0.05 mm; Klipper SCV 5 mm/s.
Change it when
Corners bulge or lose precision; the printer shakes at corners; you want smoother, quieter motion.
Trade-offs
High corner speed = faster but corner bulging and vibration; low = cleaner corners but slower and more pressure-advance sensitivity. On Bambu printers this is not an exposed setting.

Volumetric flow limit

🔧 Max volumetric speedPLA 12–25 mm³/s · PETG 8–14 · ABS/ASA 10–18 · TPU 3–8
What it does
Caps the amount of plastic per second (width × layer height × speed) so the hotend is never asked to melt more than it can. Speeds are automatically reduced where features would exceed this ceiling.
Typical
Standard hotends manage about 10–15 mm³/s with PLA, high-flow hotends 20–30+, and soft TPU about 3–8 mm³/s. The exact value depends on hotend, nozzle, temperature and material; calibrate it with a "max volumetric speed" test.
Change it when
You see under-extrusion on infill or thick layers at high speed, or clicks from the extruder. Raise temperature slightly before lowering speed.
Trade-offs
Higher limit = faster but risks under-extrusion, weak layers and dull surfaces. Lower limit = more reliable but slower, and may hide a thermal problem.
🔧 Slow down for small perimeters / overhangssmall perimeter: ≈ 50 % speed on loops under ≈ 15 mm
What it does
Reduces speed on tiny loops (small holes, thin posts) and overhanging walls so the plastic has time to cool and the toolhead isn't jerked around.
Typical
Small perimeters at 40–60 % of normal speed (threshold roughly 10–20 mm); overhang slowdown in tiers by overhang angle (e.g. 10 % / 25 % / 50 % / 75 % / 100 % overhang with progressively lower speeds).
Change it when
Holes come out blobby or small posts break or smear; overhangs curl.
Trade-offs
A little extra time for much better detail. Disabling it speeds up prints but increases defects on tiny features.
🔰 Layer time and part coolingminimum layer time 4–10 s · fan 0–100 %
What it does
If a layer prints faster than the minimum layer time, the slicer slows down (and increases fan speed) to give the plastic time to solidify before the next layer lands on it. Fan thresholds define when the fan starts and how it ramps up with bridges and overhangs.
Typical
PLA: 100 % fan after layer 2–3, min layer time 6–10 s. PETG: 30–60 % fan, min layer time 8–15 s. ABS/ASA: 0–20 % fan, as little as possible. TPU: 30–80 % fan with slow speed. Bridges: raise the fan to 100 % for almost every material.
Change it when
Tiny details, towers and sharp tips droop (more cooling or slower); layers delaminate (less cooling); ABS warps (less cooling, closed chamber).
Trade-offs
More cooling = better overhangs and small detail but weaker layer bonds and more warping risk. Slower layers = cleaner tips but longer print.

Support settings

Supports are temporary scaffolding for overhangs and bridges that are too steep or too long to print in mid-air. The best support is the one you avoid by orienting and designing the part sensibly, but when you need it, these settings decide how much it costs you in time, material and surface quality.

🔰 Support type & stylenormal (grid) · tree (organic/slim) · custom enforcers/blockers
What it does
Normal support builds a straight-walled lattice column from the plate (or the model) up to every overhang. Tree support grows thin branches that fan out from a few trunks and touch the model only where needed; it uses much less plastic and leaves fewer marks. Slicers also let you paint enforcers (force support here) and blockers (never here).
Typical
Tree for organic, figurine-like parts and most general prints; normal for flat, boxy overhangs under large horizontal surfaces, where a tree's few contact points can leave a poor underside.
Change it when
Support scars on a visible face are unacceptable (tree + interface or paint a blocker), support collapses mid-print (switch type or add base width), or tree branches fuse to the model.
Trade-offs
Tree is faster and leaves less waste but can be less stable on tall, thin prints and slow to compute; normal is predictable and strong but wasteful and harder to remove from complex shapes.
🔧 Overhang threshold angle≈ 30–50° (support when steeper than this from vertical)
What it does
Defines the overhang angle beyond which supports are generated. Many slicers measure from the vertical, so a lower value means more support; always check the tooltip, since some tools measure from the horizontal.
Typical
PLA with good cooling often prints 45–55° overhangs unsupported; a threshold around 30–40° (from vertical, as Bambu Studio and OrcaSlicer define it) is a safe default for general use.
Change it when
You have clean overhangs and want less support (raise the angle), or a material that droops (lower it). Use a test overhang ladder before trusting a number.
Trade-offs
Aggressive thresholds save plastic but risk sagging and failure; conservative ones cost time and surface quality.
🔧 Support Z distance & interface layersZ gap ≈ 1 layer (0.15–0.25 mm) · 2–3 interface layers · interface spacing 0–0.2 mm
What it does
The top Z distance is the vertical air gap between support top and model underside; it decides how easily the support breaks away. Interface layers are dense layers at the contact zone that form a smooth platform for the model's first layer above it. A bottom Z distance does the same where support sits on top of the model.
Typical
Top Z distance 0.1–0.2 mm (PLA) up to 0.25 mm (PETG, which bonds strongly). 2–3 interface layers with a tight pattern for a smooth underside. Dissolvable or purpose-made support filaments can run with 0 gap.
Change it when
Support is welded on (increase gap), or the underside is rough and sagging (decrease gap, add interface layers or use a denser interface pattern).
Trade-offs
A bigger gap releases easily but gives rougher underside; a smaller gap is smoother but harder to remove and can break details.
🚀 Support density, pattern, and basedensity 10–20 % · pattern rectilinear / grid / snug · base spacing ~2.5 mm
What it does
Controls how much material the support body has. Lower density = quicker removal and less plastic but less stable. Snug or contoured support follows the model outline closely, while default expands a bit for stability. Support also benefits from a slightly wider base layer to anchor it to the plate.
Typical
10–15 % density, a rectilinear or zig-zag pattern, and an anchoring base of 2–3 layers.
Change it when
Supports tip over (more density, wider base), or take forever to print (less density).
Trade-offs
Wasteful if over-built; unstable if under-built. In multi-material printers, supports in a separate filament or a different material can also be purged or flushed, which costs extra waste (see multi-material below).
Paint, don't generate

Rather than turning on supports everywhere, enable them only on build plate or use the support-paint brush to enforce them under the two or three places that need it. It often halves support volume and keeps visible faces clean. For parts with mating faces, orient so those surfaces face upward or sideways.

Bed adhesion settings

A print that lifts at the corners or slides on the plate is usually a first-layer problem; start with a clean plate and a good first layer. When geometry or material still threatens adhesion (tall, narrow, ABS-like warping) add one of these.

Skirt

One or more loops around the part, not touching it. Primes the nozzle and shows the first-layer quality before the print starts. Typical: 1–2 loops, 3–6 mm away.

Brim

A flat ring of extra lines attached to the part's base to increase contact area and resist corner lift. Typical width: 3–8 mm (5–10 lines); tight "inner/outer" brims for tricky shapes.

Raft

A multi-layer platform under the whole model, separated by an interface gap. Fixes poor plate contact and warping at the cost of a rough underside. Typical: 2–4 raft layers + 1–2 interface layers.

🔰 Brim width, type and gap0 · 3–8 mm · "brim-object gap" 0–0.1 mm
What it does
Adds adhesion area. Types include outer brim, inner brim (inside holes) and "mouse ears" (small discs on corners only). A small gap makes removal easier.
Typical
5 mm outer brim for ABS/ASA, nylon and tall narrow parts; mouse ears for flat rectangular parts that only lift at the corners; no brim for well-adhering PLA on a clean plate.
Change it when
Corners curl or the part has a small footprint compared with its height.
Trade-offs
Costs time and leaves a ring to remove; an aggressive brim can mark the part's lower edge.
🔧 Raft layers & contact distanceraft 2–4 layers · contact Z distance 0.1–0.2 mm · raft expansion 1–3 mm
What it does
Creates a flat, wide base with an air gap to the part so the part can be peeled off. Useful on warped or uneven plates, for materials that don't stick to the plate, and when supports must sit on a clean surface.
Typical
A raft is the last resort for normal printing; for nylon on a glue-stick-coated plate it's common.
Change it when
A brim isn't sufficient, or your bed isn't flat.
Trade-offs
Rougher bottom face, dimension change on the Z axis (the part starts higher), more time and plastic.
🔧 Elephant foot compensation0.1–0.3 mm
What it does
The squished first layers bulge outward by a tenth of a millimeter or so ("elephant's foot"), especially with a hot bed or an over-squished first layer. This setting shrinks the first layer(s) inward by a set distance to compensate.
Typical
0.1–0.2 mm for PLA; 0.2–0.3 mm for hotter-bed materials (ABS, PETG). First fix the first-layer height and nozzle-to-bed distance; then measure.
Change it when
Parts don't fit into pockets or lids bind at the lower edge. Check with a calibration cube measured with calipers.
Trade-offs
Too much compensation causes a thin, weak first perimeter and a visible undercut; keep it minimal and fix squish first.

Filament settings

The filament preset carries the material-specific numbers: temperatures, flow, retraction, cooling and pressure advance. They interact, so change one at a time and keep notes. Material properties themselves are covered in Materials; for the X2D, check Filament compatibility.

Temperatures

🔰 Nozzle & bed temperaturePLA 190–230 / 50–65 · PETG 230–260 / 70–85 · ABS 240–270 / 90–110 · TPU 210–240 / 30–60
What it does
Nozzle temperature sets melt viscosity, layer bonding and flow; bed temperature sets first-layer adhesion and warp resistance. A separate (usually 5–10 °C higher) first-layer nozzle and bed temperature is common.
Typical
Start at the filament label's mid-range. Higher speed and flow need higher nozzle temperature; thin, detailed or bridged prints prefer the lower end.
Change it when
Stringing or oozing (lower by 5–10 °C), weak or dull layers (raise), clicking extruder (raise or slow down), warping (raise bed / chamber).
Trade-offs
Hot = strong layers, glossy surfaces, more stringing; cold = crisp details, weaker layers. Use a temperature tower to find the sweet spot for each spool.

Flow ratio, pressure advance and Flow Dynamics

🔧 Flow ratio (extrusion multiplier)0.94–1.02 (start at 1.0 or vendor value)
What it does
Scales the amount of plastic extruded for every move. It compensates for filament diameter variation, material shrinkage and how soft the plastic is.
Typical
PLA ≈ 0.98–1.0; PETG ≈ 0.95–0.98; TPU ≈ 0.95–1.0. Calibrate with a flow test (single-wall cube or a pass-based print) rather than copying other people's numbers.
Change it when
Top surfaces show gaps (raise) or ridges, bulging corners and rough surfaces (lower); dimensions are consistently off.
Trade-offs
Too high: blobs, elephant foot, rough walls. Too low: under-extrusion, weak parts, pinholes on top surfaces. Changes of 1–2 % matter.
🚀 Pressure advance / linear advance / Flow DynamicsPA K ≈ 0.02–0.1 (direct drive), 0.3–1.0 (Bowden)
What it does
Plastic is compressible, so pressure in the nozzle lags the extruder motor. Pressure advance (Klipper, Marlin's linear advance, Bambu's Flow Dynamics Calibration) pushes slightly more filament while accelerating and pulls it back while decelerating, keeping the line width constant through corners and at start/stop points.
Typical
Calibrate per filament and nozzle. Typical K values for direct-drive extruders are 0.02–0.1; for Bowden setups much higher. On Bambu printers this is stored as a K factor per filament in the preset.
Change it when
Corners bulge (K too low) or are rounded and gappy (K too high); seams show a blob or a gap.
Trade-offs
It has to be recalibrated after changing the nozzle, temperature or filament brand. On a dual-nozzle X2D, Flow Dynamics Calibration applies only to the main nozzle.

Retraction, wipe and Z-hop

When the nozzle crosses an open gap, molten plastic can ooze and leave strings. Retraction pulls the filament back a short distance before a travel move and pushes it forward again afterwards. Wipe drags the nozzle along the last part of the wall to clean the tip, and Z-hop lifts the nozzle during travel to avoid knocking printed features.

🔧 Retraction length & speed0.4–1.0 mm direct drive · 3–7 mm Bowden · 30–60 mm/s
What it does
Defines how far and how fast the filament backs up before travel. Also includes extra length on restart and minimum travel for retraction (skip retraction on very short travels, typically 1–2 mm).
Typical
PLA 0.5–1 mm; PETG 0.8–1.5 mm (stringy, so keep it hotter-bed and cooler-nozzle); TPU 0–1 mm (long retractions jam flexible filament); ABS 0.5–1 mm.
Change it when
You see strings between towers (increase slightly, lower temperature, try wipe), grinding or clicking, or gaps after travel moves (reduce).
Trade-offs
Aggressive retraction can cause heat creep and jams, particularly on all-metal hotends with soft filament.
🔧 Z-hop & wipeZ-hop 0.2–0.4 mm (only when needed) · wipe distance 1–2 mm
What it does
Z-hop lifts the nozzle on travel moves so it cannot knock over printed features or scratch the top surface. Variants include "only on top surfaces" and "slope" hops. Wipe retracts while moving back along the last bit of the printed path to clean the nozzle.
Typical
Leave Z-hop off for most prints (it adds strings and time); enable it for warp-prone parts, for ironing or on top surfaces. Wipe distance ~1–2 mm with a small retraction.
Change it when
The nozzle hits curled edges or leaves scars (enable Z-hop), blobs sit at seams (try wipe), or you see stringing at the hop point (disable).
Trade-offs
Z-hop slows printing, can string, and with some firmware causes visible Z-axis artifacts.

Cooling fans, bridges and overhangs

🔰 Fan speed & fan thresholdsmin/max fan 0–100 % · full fan from layer 3 · bridge fan 100 %
What it does
Adjusts cooling per layer time; the first layers get little or no fan to maximise adhesion, then the fan ramps up. Overhang and bridge fan boosts add extra cooling for those moves only. Bambu printers also have an auxiliary part cooling fan and chamber fans that profiles control.
Typical
PLA 80–100 %, PETG 30–60 %, ABS/ASA 0–30 % (higher only for bridges), TPU 40–80 %, nylon 0–30 %.
Change it when
Corners curl at the top (more fan), layers split (less fan or higher temperature), overhangs sag (more fan, slower).
Trade-offs
More fan = crisper details, weaker layers. Closed-chamber printers with hot materials need less fan and may need the chamber vented for PLA.
🚀 Bridge flow, speed and thick bridgesbridge flow 0.85–1.0 · speed 20–50 mm/s · fan 100 %
What it does
A bridge is a line printed across thin air; reducing its flow (a "thinner" line) and cooling hard helps it stay straight and tight instead of sagging. "Thick bridges" prints round, overlapping strands that are more robust over longer spans.
Typical
Bridge flow ~0.9; speed 25–50 mm/s; fan 100 %; bridge angle auto (or perpendicular to the supports).
Change it when
Bridges sag (lower flow, cool more, slower) or break/pull apart (raise flow, slower, hotter).
Trade-offs
Very long bridges (> 50 mm) still need support. Better bridging often costs a small slowdown.

With two nozzles, each filament can stay loaded in its own nozzle, so filament changes between the left and right nozzles do not need a purge tower at all. Assign the filaments that alternate most (for example a model material and its support) to different nozzles. Details: X2D settings and profiles.

Other useful settings

🚀 Spiral vase (vase mode)1 wall · no top infill · continuous Z rise
What it does
Prints a single continuous outer wall that rises gradually, with a solid bottom and no seam. Ideal for vases, cups and decorative shells.
Typical
Line width of 0.6–1.0 mm with a 0.4–0.6 mm nozzle for sturdy, watertight walls; 0 % infill; no top layers; seam smoothing on.
Change it when
You want fast, seamless thin-walled objects. It cannot do solid objects or multiple loops.
Trade-offs
Wall strength is limited to a single line; thin tall vases tip over easily on bed shaking.
🔧 Sequential (by-object) printingprint each object to completion before the next
What it does
Prints one object completely, then the next, instead of layer by layer across all objects. Reduces stringing between objects and lets you cancel individual failed items.
Typical
Objects must be shorter than the gantry clearance height (a few cm on many printers), spaced to avoid the toolhead hitting printed parts. The slicer warns when it detects a collision.
Change it when
Printing many small, identical parts where one failure should not ruin the batch.
Trade-offs
Needs wide spacing, limits height, and the extruder or fan duct can strike finished parts. Some printers cannot do it safely.
🔧 Adaptive layer height0.08–0.28 mm range, smoothing
What it does
Varies layer height along Z: thin where curves or details need fidelity, thick on vertical walls. See layer height.
Typical
Min 0.08–0.12 mm, max 0.24–0.28 mm, with a quality-vs-speed slider.
Change it when
Organic or sloped models that are both large and detailed.
Trade-offs
Mixed layer heights may produce uneven strength; it can conflict with variable-height supports and tuned pressure advance.
🔧 Slicing resolution & gap closingresolution ≈ 0.01 mm · gap closing radius 0–0.5 mm
What it does
Resolution (or "max deviation") simplifies the sliced outline: high values merge points and shrink G-code, low values keep every vertex. Slice gap closing radius bridges tiny cracks in the mesh so thin cracks do not cut the outline.
Typical
Resolution 0.01–0.02 mm for most prints (higher for huge, faceted models); gap closing radius ≈ 0.001–0.05 mm. Arc fitting can then convert the polyline to G2/G3 arcs.
Change it when
The G-code file is gigantic or the printer stutters on dense curves (raise resolution slightly), or a model with tiny cracks has missing walls (raise the gap closing radius).
Trade-offs
Too coarse a resolution rounds off fine details; too wide a gap closing fills real features.
🔰 XY contour & hole compensation± 0.02–0.15 mm
What it does
XY contour compensation grows or shrinks the outer outline; XY hole compensation does the same for inner holes. Because printed holes tend to be undersized and outer dimensions slightly oversized, small corrections make press-fits and screws work.
Typical
Start at 0; measure a calibration plate with holes of 3–10 mm; apply +0.05–0.15 mm on holes if they print small. Combine with design clearances from design principles.
Change it when
Dimensions are consistently off in XY after you have tuned flow ratio and first layer.
Trade-offs
Compensations are global; if one part needs a different value, use per-object settings or fix it in the CAD model instead.

Tuning order that saves time

Dry the filament → calibrate flow ratio and pressure advance → find the temperature that gives clean surfaces → set volumetric flow → then adjust cooling and speed → only then touch retraction and supports. Doing it in a different order means retesting everything.

Quick self-check

You want a stronger bracket that does not take much longer to print. Which change gives the best return?

  • Infill from 15 % to 60 %
  • Increase walls from 2 to 4 and orient the layers across the load
  • Switch to a rectilinear infill

Walls and orientation dominate strength; infill beyond 20–40 % mostly adds time and weight.

A print at 250 mm/s shows under-extrusion on infill. What is the most likely limit?

  • The slicer seam position
  • The hotend's maximum volumetric flow
  • The elephant foot compensation

Feed speed × line width × layer height must stay under the hotend's melt rate.

Strings between two posts keep appearing in PETG. Which is the best first move?

  • Increase the fan to 100 %
  • Lower the nozzle temperature a few degrees, dry the filament and tune retraction
  • Add a raft

Stringing is a combination of wet filament, excess heat and insufficient retraction.

Key takeaways

  • Orientation and wall count decide strength; infill density has diminishing returns.
  • Layer height trades surface quality for time; adaptive layer height and tuned seams hide most artifacts.
  • Real speed is limited by acceleration and volumetric flow, not by the headline mm/s.
  • Use tree support and painted enforcers to reduce scars; keep the Z gap around one layer.
  • Calibrate filament by filament: temperature, flow ratio, pressure advance, then retraction.
  • Change one setting at a time and save working presets under clear names.
