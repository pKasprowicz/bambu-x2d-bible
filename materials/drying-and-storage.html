Drying & storage

Wet filament is the most common hidden cause of bad prints. Learn why plastics drink water from the air, how to spot it, how to dry each material safely, and how to keep it dry afterward.

Beginner⏱ 40 min read
What you will learn
  • Why polymers absorb moisture and which ones are the worst.
  • The symptoms of wet filament and three tests to confirm it.
  • Drying temperatures and times for each material, and the traps (spool softening, oven errors).
  • Dryer types, storage methods and a simple moisture budget calculation.

🔬 Hygroscopy: why plastic drinks water

A hygroscopic material pulls water vapor out of the air. In polymers the cause is chemistry: chains that contain polar groups (amide –CONH– in nylon, ester –COO– in PLA, PET and PETG, urethane in TPU, hydroxyl –OH in PVA and BVOH) form hydrogen bonds with water molecules and let them diffuse into the amorphous regions. Non-polar polymers such as polypropylene and polystyrene hardly absorb any. The amount depends on relative humidity (RH), temperature and time. At equilibrium, the water content is a fixed percentage of the filament’s weight for a given RH; to reach it takes hours to weeks, depending on diameter and how tightly the spool is wound.

What the water does

  • In the hotend: water flashes to steam at 200–300 °C, makes bubbles inside the molten plastic, and bursts out of the nozzle (popping, hissing, tiny craters).
  • Hydrolysis: at high temperature, water breaks ester and amide bonds. Chains shorten, so PETG, PET, PC, PLA, PA and TPU print weaker, and the damage is permanent. This is why wet PC or PET makes brittle parts even when they look fine.
  • Lower viscosity: hydrolyzed polymer flows too easily, which creates stringing and blobs.
  • Post-print: parts keep absorbing moisture: nylon swells and softens; PVA turns sticky.

🌡️ Drying temperature and time

Heat increases the vapor pressure of the absorbed water and speeds its escape. The limit is the filament’s softening point: above it the spool deforms, filaments fuse together, and the loops cross-weld. Stay at least 10–15 °C below the glass transition (Tg) of the polymer, or follow the maker’s spool label. Times assume a standard 1 kg spool, ventilated dryer and mild moisture; very wet nylon or PVA can take twice as long.

Why drying temperature has a ceiling

Heating PLA to 70 °C on a spool is a classic mistake: the polymer is above its Tg, the spool relaxes, loops fuse and the filament can no longer unwind. Nylon is the opposite: it benefits from high temperatures but a standard plastic spool can warp, so move the filament to a heat-resistant spool or use a dryer designed for it. Also, a hot oven set to 50 °C usually swings 10–20 °C, which is why food ovens are risky.

🧰 Dryer types

Dedicated filament dryers

Heated, insulated boxes with a fan or a ventilated vent and a thermostat, usually 35–70 °C and sometimes up to 90 °C. Most hold 1–2 spools and have a feed port for printing from them. The most reliable and safe option for everyday use.

Food dehydrators

Cheap, high capacity, fans give good airflow. Many only reach 70 °C, and the temperature display can be off by 5–10 °C. Verify with a thermometer. Do not use the same unit for food afterward.

Ovens

A convection oven can work but cycles ±10–20 °C around a low setpoint, and many do not go below 80–100 °C. Dangerous for PLA and TPU. If you must, use an oven thermometer and a heat-safe tray; never leave it unattended with plastics.

Heated AMS-type enclosures

Multi-material systems such as the AMS 2 Pro and AMS HT include heaters and desiccant to dry and store spools in place. They dry slower than a dedicated unit but keep filament dry during long prints.

Drying inside the AMS 2 Pro and similar units

Bambu’s AMS 2 Pro can heat and dry spools in place, to a maximum of 65 °C. That is enough for PLA, PETG, TPU, and borderline for ABS or nylon (most nylons need a higher-temperature dryer for deep drying). Important details from the official documentation:

  • Drying needs a 6-pin cable power connection. Depending on the printer, the power comes from the printer itself or from an included adapter; it does not dry as a standalone unit without that connection.
  • Drying power is reduced if the AMS is feeding or the printer is printing at the same time, so the unit dries more slowly while a job runs.
  • The AMS contains a color-changing desiccant; when the indicator shows saturation, or after roughly 4–6 weeks of use, replace or regenerate it.

Treat this as keeping filament from getting worse during a job and an extra drying step for lightly wet spools. For heavily wet nylon or PC, use a dedicated dryer first. Details for the unit are on the AMS 2 Pro page, with model and printer compatibility in the compatibility notes.

“Active drying” while printing

A long, large-capacity job lasts 10–30 hours, during which the spool in a printer or on a shelf may reabsorb moisture. To prevent that, feed filament from a heated dry box, or keep the spool in a sealed container with desiccant plus a short PTFE tube. For nylon, PC, PVA and BVOH, this is not a luxury: it determines whether the second half of the print is as good as the first.

Desiccants: what they are and how to recharge them

  • Silica gel (colour-changing orange-to-green or blue-to-pink) absorbs about 20–30 % of its weight at moderate humidity and releases it when heated. Recharge in an oven at ≈ 120 °C for 1–2 h (use only products rated for it; some indicator dyes cannot take heat).
  • Molecular sieve (zeolite 3A/4A) reaches lower humidity (down to a few percent) but is slower to regenerate, needing 200–300 °C.
  • Clay (montmorillonite) desiccant is cheap, but has a lower capacity and doesn’t reach low humidity.
  • Always put desiccant in a breathable pouch so dust cannot reach the filament path.

🧮 Moisture budget: why a tiny leak matters

A sealed box has two numbers that matter: the water in the air and the water the filament and desiccant can hold. Air carries remarkably little: at 25 °C and 50 % RH, 1 m³ of air holds ≈ 11.5 g of water (saturated air is ≈ 23 g/m³). A 20 L box contains therefore ≈ 0.23 g of water vapor. A 1 kg nylon spool at equilibrium (≈ 2.5–3 % at 50 % RH) holds 25–30 g, over a hundred times more than the box air.

  • Desiccant capacity: 50 g of silica gel at ~25 % capacity can take about 12 g of water.
  • Ingress: a poorly sealed box lets humid air in continuously: each air exchange adds ≈ 0.23 g, so 50 exchanges over a month deliver ≈ 11 g, about what the desiccant can hold. That is why a good gasket matters.
  • Takeaway: the box air dries within minutes of adding desiccant. What the desiccant must really offset is the slow leak plus the spool’s own stored water. A spool that was never dried will keep releasing water into the box for days.

Budget by hand: box water per refill = (volume m³ × 11.5 g × RH/50 %) × exchanges; desiccant needed ≈ total water ÷ (0.2 to 0.25). For a 20 L box opened twice a week at 50 % RH, 20–50 g of silica per month is enough. Spreadsheet help lives in the calculators.

🧪 How to test: is my filament wet?

  1. Sound testExtrude 100 mm of hot filament in the air at the right temperature, listening and watching. Crackling, popping or a steam wisp says wet; a silent, glossy, bubble-free strand says dry enough.
  2. Extrude testLook at the extruded strand: bubbles, a rough or foamy surface, and thin, curly threads show moisture. Compare to a fresh spool of the same material if you have one.
  3. Weigh-in testWeigh the spool on a 0.1 g scale (kitchen scales are often enough for 1 kg spools if they resolve 1 g). Dry it, weigh it again. A loss of more than 0.3–0.5 % of the filament’s weight for PLA/PETG, or more than 1 % for nylon, means it was wet. Subtract the spool weight from your measurement for accuracy.
  4. Print testPrint a small stringing test or a 10-mm tower; compare before and after drying.
  5. Brittleness testBend a 20 cm piece. PLA snapping like a dry twig is a sign of hydrolysis; nylon that bends but looks hazy is probably wet rather than damaged.

🚚 Printing from the dry box, shipping, and vacuum bags

  • Printing from a box: use a PTFE tube from the box port to the printer and a smooth spool path; make sure the spool can rotate with low friction and that the tube cannot kink as the toolhead moves. Seal the gap around the tube with foam or tape.
  • Vacuum bags: re-bagging a dried spool with a fresh desiccant pouch extends its life from days to months. Do not pull a hard vacuum on soft spools; just remove most of the air.
  • Shipping: keep spools in a sealed bag and use desiccant in the box. Do not ship the dried spool in an uncontrolled, hot truck without a seal, as moisture can reenter within days for nylon.
  • Humidity indicator cards that turn from blue to pink are cheap and handy inside bags and boxes.

📝 A step-by-step drying protocol

  1. Identify the material and its limitRead the spool label or datasheet. If it lists a drying temperature, use it. If not, use the table above and stay 10–15 °C under the base polymer’s Tg.
  2. Check the spoolCardboard spools tolerate low temperatures only; thin plastic spools soften above about 60 °C. For nylon at 80 °C or more, rewind onto a heat-resistant spool, or hang the loose coil on a rack.
  3. Open the lid or ventA fully closed hot box simply recirculates the humid air. Crack the vent, or use a dryer with a built-in exhaust, so vapor can leave.
  4. Verify the real temperaturePut a cheap probe thermometer in the dryer on the first run. Inexpensive units are often 5–10 °C off the setting.
  5. Dry for the table time, then testDo a sound or extrude test. If the strand still pops, give it another 2–4 h.
  6. Seal or feed immediatelyMove the spool straight to a dry box or feed from the dryer. Spools left in room air start picking water up the moment they cool.
  7. Label and logWrite the date on the spool. It saves you from re-drying a spool you dried yesterday, and it shows which brands need regular treatment.

How many times can I dry a spool?

As many times as needed, as long as you stay below Tg. Drying is not what damages filament; printing while wet (hydrolysis in the hotend) and long storage at high temperature are. Repeated drying cycles can make some dyed filaments slightly yellower or the spool slightly looser, but these are cosmetic. If a spool is brittle and snaps when bent after drying, the polymer chains have probably been damaged by earlier moisture exposure and drying cannot repair it.

🧩 Material-by-material moisture notes

PLA

Absorbs slowly. Mild stringing and a dull surface are the first signs, but a spool may sit open for weeks in dry climates. In very humid regions, dry it too before critical prints. Never dry above 55 °C on the spool. Wet PLA often looks fine but is more brittle.

PETG and PCTG

Picks up water in a few days and shows it quickly: pops, strings and blobs. Cheap to fix: 60–65 °C for 4–8 h. Wet PETG tends to string long, glassy hairs and the layers look wet.

Nylon

The extreme case. It reaches a few percent water within a day and prints as a foam when wet. It is hygroscopic after printing, so a dried part’s stiffness and size change over days. Always dry just before printing and feed from a dry box.

TPU

Moderately hygroscopic but with a nasty sensitivity: wet TPU prints very stringy and bubbly because its soft melt traps steam. Dry it gently and watch for spool swelling.

PC and PET-CF

Hydrolysis is the key risk: wet PC loses toughness permanently and can even turn a hazy yellow. Dry thoroughly and keep dry during the print.

PVA and BVOH

Absorb so much water that they can swell and stick to themselves in hours of humid air. Store sealed, dry hot, and print from a heated box. Once wet, they clog nozzles by bubbling and degrading.

🚫 Common drying myths

  • “Vacuum-sealed filament is guaranteed dry.” Only if it was dry when sealed and the bag has desiccant. Factory seals can fail, and cheap bags leak slowly.
  • “A hygrometer reading of 20 % means the spool is dry.” It reports the air. A spool can hold water from past exposure and slowly release it into the box.
  • “PLA never needs drying.” Not true in humid climates or after long storage, though it is far more forgiving than nylon.
  • “Hotter is always better.” Above the Tg the spool deforms and the filament fuses; below the limit, longer is safer.
  • “The microwave is a fast way to dry filament.” It heats unevenly, melts spools, and can start fires. Do not do this.
  • “Dried filament will remain dry.” Nylon can pick up significant water in hours; PLA in days to weeks. Sealing immediately matters.

🌍 Your climate matters

Humidity swings with the seasons and location. A dry winter apartment might sit at 25–35 % RH and need no special measures for PLA or PETG, while a tropical summer at 80 % RH can ruin a PETG spool in a weekend. Measure it: a US$10 hygrometer in the print room tells you the baseline. If your room is typically below 40 % RH, a dry box for sensitive materials will be enough. Above 60 % RH, store everything sealed. Coastal and basement workshops need dehumidifiers or air-conditioning to protect more than just filament, since electronics and linear rails also suffer from condensation.

Quick triage

Printing a new material? Dry it first, then print a test. If you hear pops, mark the spool. If a spool you haven’t used for a month acts up, assume it is wet until proven otherwise; an hour on the dryer costs less than an hour of troubleshooting.

💰 Cost and benefit

A dedicated dryer costs about US$40–90 and a good box with desiccant US$15–40. A failed 12-hour nylon print wastes 100–300 g of filament (US$5–15) plus your time and machine wear. If you print PLA exclusively in a dry climate, you can often skip drying. The moment you print nylon, PC, PET-CF, TPU or PVA, a dryer is cheaper than the failures it prevents.

Only PLA

Seal opened spools in a bag with desiccant and dry only if symptoms appear (≈ US$10–20).

PETG, ABS, TPU

Buy a dryer and a few airtight boxes (≈ US$50–120). Dry new spools before first use in humid climates.

Nylon, PC, PVA, CF blends

A 90 °C-capable dryer with feed port plus a heated enclosure or AMS-type drying; always print from the dry box (≈ US$100–250).

✅ Key takeaways

  • Polar polymers absorb water: PVA/BVOH ≫ nylon ≫ TPU, PC, PETG, PLA ≫ PP.
  • Wet filament pops, steams, strings, looks rough and prints weak; hydrolysis damage is permanent.
  • Dry below Tg: PLA 45–55 °C, PETG 60–65 °C, TPU 50–65 °C, ABS/ASA 70–80 °C, PA 75–90 °C (on a heat-safe spool).
  • The AMS 2 Pro dries up to 65 °C and loses power while printing; use a dedicated dryer for nylon and PC.
  • Seals and fresh desiccant keep the filament dry; test with sound, extrusion and weigh-in.
