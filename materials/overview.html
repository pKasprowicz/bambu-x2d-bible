🔰 What filament actually is

Filament is thermoplastic that has been melted, extruded through a precise die, cooled, and wound onto a spool as a long, thin, round “wire”. An FDM (fused deposition modeling, also called FFF) printer pulls it off the spool, pushes it into a heated nozzle, melts it, and lays it down line by line and layer by layer. Because the plastic is a thermoplastic, it softens when heated and hardens when cooled, again and again. That is the only reason the process works, and it is also why a printed part can soften in a hot car.

Almost all consumer printers, including the Bambu Lab X2D, use filament with a nominal diameter of 1.75 mm. A second standard, 2.85 mm (often called “3 mm”), exists for a few older or industrial machines. The two are not interchangeable, so always check what your printer accepts.

Diameter and tolerance

The extruder meters plastic by length, not by volume. The slicer assumes a diameter (usually 1.75 mm) and computes how many millimeters to feed. If the real wire is 1.80 mm, the cross-sectional area is about 6% too large, and so is the amount of plastic extruded. That shows up as slightly over-extruded, bulgy surfaces. A thin spot at 1.70 mm gives the opposite: gaps and weak walls. Good filament holds ±0.02 mm; budget filament is often ±0.05 mm; anything wider is a risk. A calibrated flow ratio per material (see Calibration playbook) absorbs the average error but not diameter swings along the spool.

Length per kilogram

Density sets the length: PLA (1.24 g/cm³) gives roughly 330 m per kg, PETG (1.27) about 320 m, ABS (1.04) about 400 m, and carbon-fiber nylon (around 1.1–1.2) 340–370 m. Light plastics like ABS and PP go further per spool, which is why price per kg is not the same as price per part.

The spool

The spool is not just packaging: it is part of the system. Its core diameter determines how tightly the filament is bent. Stiff materials like PC, PA-CF and PLA-CF dislike tight cores and can snap while being unrolled. The spool’s flange, hole size and width decide whether it fits an AMS, a dryer or a side-mounted holder. Refill spools without a reel exist for the Bambu ecosystem; you reuse a reel and save plastic and money. A spool’s weight label includes only the plastic. The empty reel weighs 150–250 g, which matters when you weigh a spool to estimate what is left.

Color, pigments and additives

A “pure” polymer is typically translucent or milky. Color comes from pigments or dyes compounded into the plastic before extrusion. Additives change behavior as well:

Property modifiers

Impact modifiers (rubber particles) make “Tough PLA”; plasticizers soften; nucleating agents speed crystallization (sharper detail, less warp in some PLA); flame retardants make V-0 grades.

Fillers and fibers

Chopped carbon or glass fiber (stiffness, low warp, abrasive), wood flour, ground metal, stone powder, glow pigment (strontium aluminate, abrasive), magnetic or conductive particles.

Process aids

Lubricants and flow improvers for high-speed PLA, anti-oxidants and stabilizers against heat degradation, UV stabilizers in ASA.

Visual effects

Silk (shiny, usually PLA with a modifier), matte (mineral fillers), sparkle, galaxy, marble, dual-color and gradient wraps. They may print at different settings than the base polymer.

Two spools of “PLA” from different brands can therefore differ more than two different polymers from the same brand. Pigment matters too: black and white are often noticeably different in flow and strength from natural or other colors, and carbon-black pigment can change heat absorption. Always treat a new brand, and even a new color, as a new material to tune (see calibration).

🔧 Polymer science in plain language

A plastic is a polymer: a very long molecular chain built from thousands of repeating units. How those chains are arranged and how they move when heated explains stiffness, heat resistance, shrinkage, clarity, chemical resistance and even how hard the material is to print.

Amorphous vs semi-crystalline

Chains either stay in a random tangle (amorphous) or partly fold into tidy, tightly packed regions called crystallites (semi-crystalline). Real plastics are a spectrum, but the two families behave differently when cooled.

Amorphous

Softens gradually over a range of temperature. Shrinks little (0.3–0.8%), warps moderately, bonds nicely between layers. Often transparent-capable. Typical: ABS, ASA, PC, PETG, PCTG, HIPS, PEI, PVA (partly).

Semi-crystalline

Stays stiff above its glass transition until close to the melting point, then turns thin and watery. Shrinks strongly as crystals form (1–2% and more), so warping is common. Better chemical, fatigue and wear resistance. Typical: PA6/PA12/PA66, PP, PET, PPS, PEEK, POM, PLA (when annealed or nucleated).

Glass transition (Tg) and melting point (Tm)

The glass transition temperature (Tg) is not melting. It is the temperature range where the chains in the amorphous regions start to wiggle freely, and a hard glassy solid turns rubbery. A part made of an amorphous plastic loses most of its stiffness near Tg, which is why a PLA part (Tg about 55–65 °C) sags in a sun-baked car even though PLA does not melt until about 150–180 °C. Semi-crystalline plastics also have a Tg but their crystals keep them partly stiff until the melting temperature (Tm), the point where crystals dissolve.

HDT: the practical number

Datasheets often quote HDT (heat deflection temperature): the temperature at which a standard bar under a small load bends by a fixed amount. It is a handy comparison, but it depends heavily on load (0.45 vs 1.8 MPa), print orientation, infill and annealing. Treat HDT as a ranking, not a guarantee, and test your own part at the real temperature with the real load.

Crystallization, shrinkage and warping

Every plastic shrinks as it cools. Semi-crystalline plastics shrink more because the chains pack closer when they crystallize. In a print, the top layer is hot while the layer beneath is already cold and rigid. As the top layer cools it wants to contract but is held by the cold layer. The result is internal stress that pulls the part’s edges upward. That curl is warping. Adhesion to the build plate fights it; heat (a warm bed, a warm chamber) reduces the temperature difference that drives it.

Hygroscopy: plastics that drink water

Many polymers attract water vapor from the air (hygroscopic). Nylon, PVA, BVOH and TPU absorb it fast and in large amounts; PETG, PC, and PLA absorb less but still enough to matter. When wet filament enters the hot zone, the water flashes to steam, which makes hissing, popping, tiny bubbles, stringing and rough surfaces, and in the worst case hydrolysis, which permanently cuts the polymer chains (PLA, PETG, PET and PA are all vulnerable). The details, humidity targets and drying charts are in Drying & storage.

Layer adhesion and anisotropy

A printed part is made of lines welded to lines. Inside a layer the plastic is continuous and molecules are oriented along the path, so the part is strong in X and Y. Between layers, the weld depends on how hot the surfaces still were when they touched. This makes parts anisotropic: strength depends on direction. Pulling across layers (the Z direction) usually yields only a fraction of the in-plane strength, commonly 30–70%, and less for cold, fast prints. Materials that need a hot chamber (PA, PC, ABS) get much better inter-layer welds when printed warm, which is a major reason enclosed, actively heated chambers exist. See Strength & orientation for design rules.

⚙️ When you need a hardened nozzle

Brass is soft. Carbon fiber, glass fiber, glow pigment (strontium aluminate), metal powders, and some stone or ceramic fills act like fine sandpaper and widen a brass orifice within a few hundred grams. A wider hole means over-extrusion, dull detail, and eventually inconsistent flow. The cure is a nozzle made of hardened steel, tungsten carbide, ruby-tipped or coated alloys. Hardened steel conducts heat less well than brass, so you typically raise the nozzle temperature by 5–10 °C and keep flow in check.

Required

All carbon-fiber and glass-fiber filaments (PLA-CF, PETG-CF, PA-CF, PAHT-CF, PET-CF, PPS-CF, ASA-CF), glow-in-the-dark, metal-filled, many “marble” or ceramic-style fills.

Recommended

Heavy glitter or sparkle, “galaxy”, some stone fills, high-percentage wood filaments (also use ≥ 0.6 mm to avoid clogs), recycled filaments with unknown fillers.

Not needed

Plain PLA, PETG, ABS, ASA, PC, TPU, PA without fiber, silk and standard colors, and most dual-color or gradient filaments.

Not every printer ships with a hardened nozzle; some official hotends and nozzle kits are compatibility-limited by material, so check the printer’s filament table before you load a fiber-filled spool (X2D filament compatibility).

🚀 Strategy: choosing like an engineer

  1. Write the requirement listMaximum temperature in service, load type (static, impact, cyclic), environment (UV, water, oil, fuel, cleaning chemicals), contact (skin, food), and appearance. A part that “just needs to be strong” is not a requirement.
  2. Find the dominant constraintOften one property decides: heat points to ABS/PC/PA-CF, UV to ASA, flex to TPU, wear to PA, stiffness to CF-composites. Decide on that first.
  3. Check your hardwareHotend rating, enclosure, chamber heating, nozzle hardness, dryers and AMS compatibility. Step down if the printer cannot support the first choice.
  4. Pick the cheapest material that passesPLA and PETG are cheaper, faster and more forgiving. Use harder materials only where the requirement forces you.
  5. Prototype in PLA, then test the real materialUse a throwaway PLA to prove fit and geometry. Then print the final one and test it under real load and heat before releasing it.

Mini case studies

Dashboard phone mount

Summer cabin temperature can pass 70 °C. PLA would sag, PETG is marginal, so ASA (or ABS) is chosen. ASA is also UV-stable. Printed enclosed at 100 °C bed.

Garden hose connector

Outdoors, wet, UV, and some pressure: PETG works for light duty; ASA is better; nylon would absorb water and swell. Chosen: ASA with 5 walls.

Drive gear

Sliding wear and impact: PA6 or PA12 (low friction, tough); PA-CF if stiffness matters more than toughness. Needs dry filament and a warm chamber.

Common mistakes

Choosing the “strongest” filament

“Strong” depends on how a part fails. PLA has a high tensile strength but cracks under impact; PETG or nylon bends and survives. CF composites are very stiff but brittle across layers.

Ignoring creep and heat

Plastics under a steady load slowly deform (creep), and much faster when warm. A PLA hook that carries a plant pot at 22 °C can fail in sunlight months later even if it never broke on day one.

Printing fiber-filled filament in a brass nozzle

It will “work” for the first hundred grams and then slowly ruin print quality. Fit a hardened nozzle first.

Pro trick: material swap test

Print the same small functional part in two materials, then abuse them: bend, drop, heat in an oven at 60, 80, 100 °C, immerse in the real chemical. An hour of testing beats days of forum reading.

Key takeaways

  • Filament is 1.75 mm thermoplastic wire; consistent diameter and dryness matter as much as the polymer.
  • Amorphous plastics soften at Tg; semi-crystalline plastics hold up to Tm but shrink and warp more.
  • Heat is the limit for PLA (about 55–60 °C); step up to PETG, ABS/ASA, PC or fiber-filled nylon only when the requirement forces it.
  • Moisture ruins prints and parts; dry hygroscopic materials (nylon, PVA, TPU, PETG, PC) before use.
  • Fiber, glow and metal fills need hardened nozzles.
  • Choose by the dominant constraint, then check your hardware. Practice: Level 2 exercises.
