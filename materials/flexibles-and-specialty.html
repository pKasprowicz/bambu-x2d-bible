🧽 Flexible filaments: TPU, TPE and friends

TPU (thermoplastic polyurethane) and TPE (thermoplastic elastomer, a broader family that includes TPU, TPC and SEBS-based blends) are rubbery at room temperature but melt like any thermoplastic. They consist of hard, crystalline segments that act as physical crosslinks and soft, stretchy segments that give the elasticity. A higher proportion of soft segments gives a softer, more stretchy material and a harder print job.

  • Strengths: abrasion resistance, impact and fatigue resistance, grip, vibration damping, good chemical resistance to oils and greases, hydrolysis resistance (for polyether grades).
  • Limits: low stiffness, creep, difficult to print fast, strongly hygroscopic, low heat resistance (softens around 80–100 °C, depending on grade), sticky on some build plates.
  • Typical uses: phone cases, gaskets, bumpers, wheels and tires for RC cars, belts, vibration feet, grips, hinges, protective covers.

Shore hardness

Flexible plastics are rated by Shore durometer: a spring-loaded indenter is pushed into the material and the depth is read as 0–100. Shore A covers soft to medium rubbers; Shore D covers harder plastics. The scales overlap loosely and are not exactly convertible: 95A is roughly 45D.

🛠️ Printing flexibles

The difficulty is not melting TPU but pushing it. A soft filament behaves like a noodle: it compresses and buckles between the extruder gear and the nozzle whenever there is a gap or high resistance. The fix is a short, fully constrained filament path.

Core settings

  • Speed: limit volumetric flow to about 2–6 mm³/s for 85–95A TPU (a 0.4 mm nozzle with 0.2 mm layers at 0.45 mm line width is roughly 35–65 mm/s at 3–6 mm³/s). Bambu’s high-flow TPU 95A HF is the exception and will reach higher flow rates.
  • Retraction: keep it small (0.5–1.5 mm) and slow, or disable it for very soft grades. Large, fast retractions pull the soft filament into a wad (“tangle” inside the extruder).
  • Temperature: 210–240 °C. Hotter lowers viscosity and reduces stringing but can degrade the polymer.
  • Cooling: 30–100 % fan works fine for TPU: it solidifies quickly and sheds heat poorly. Use more cooling for overhangs.
  • Bed: 30–60 °C; TPU can stick extremely well to PEI. Use a release layer on smooth plates, or a textured plate without glue (see below).
  • Infill: gyroid at 10–30 % for flexible parts gives a springy, isotropic feel; rectilinear gives directional stiffness. More walls = stiffer part.

Bed adhesion, drying, and storage

TPU welds to PEI. Do not add glue on textured PEI (it over-bonds and may tear the plate), but apply a thin layer of glue or a release agent on smooth PEI, engineering or high-temperature plates. Dry TPU before use (typical 50–70 °C for 4–8 h; Bambu’s own guidance for its TPU is 70 °C for 8 h in a forced-air oven, with warnings that spools expand and may deform) and store below ≈ 20 % RH in a sealed box with desiccant. TPU swells with moisture and prints foamy and stringy. Read the drying chapter.

Bambu X2D and TPU

On the X2D, print TPU on the main nozzle only: the right auxiliary extruder has higher feed resistance and does not support TPU. Hardness and hotend rules (for example TPU 85A needs a 0.6 mm or larger hotend; 90A can use 0.4 mm; high-flow 95A HF and “TPU for AMS” run on 0.4–0.8 mm) are in the official guide summarized in TPU & flexibles on the X2D. Only the stiffer grades labeled for the AMS belong in it; feed everything softer from the external spool.

AMS and flexibles

The AMS pushes and pulls filament along a PTFE tube with a defined friction budget. TPU 95A and “TPU for AMS” grades are formulated for this and have a stiffer, slicker body. Softer grades (85A–90A) can be used with the AMS only when the vendor specifically says so; otherwise they kink and jam in the buffer. Keep spools dry and keep tubing curves gentle. Dual-material interface mechanics

When two polymers touch, three things decide whether they bond: chemical compatibility (similar polarity and solubility parameters mix and entangle), temperature at the interface (hot melt diffuses across a boundary only above Tg), and surface contact (gap, flow, and layer time). Designers use that:

  • Want to bond: two chemically similar materials printed at similar temperatures bond strongly, such as PLA+PLA of different colors, or TPU on TPU. TPU on PETG often bonds surprisingly well; TPU on PLA is weaker.
  • Want to release: pair incompatible polymers (PLA with PETG, PETG with PA) and add a small gap and low interface adhesion. PETG support under a PLA overhang releases cleanly; PLA support under PETG fuses.
  • Mechanical locks: where chemistry will not bond, design a dovetail, hole or undercut so the materials physically interlock. This is how multi-material gaskets and over-molded grips stay put.

Multi-material setup, purge volumes and prime towers are described in Multi-color & multi-material, and the support settings in Supports, bridges & overhangs. The X2D’s dual nozzles (see dual-nozzle printing) reduce the purge needed to switch materials, which is especially helpful for expensive soluble supports.

Handling soluble supports

  • PVA and BVOH are the most moisture-hungry filaments. Keep them in a sealed box with desiccant, ideally dried during printing (a heated dry box).
  • Dissolve them in room-temperature to warm (≤ 40 °C) water, stirring occasionally. Change the water as it saturates. Do not pour dissolved PVA down a drain in large amounts.
  • Use a separate nozzle or purge thoroughly; PVA burnt in a hotend carbonizes and blocks it.
  • Print support interface layers 0.1–0.2 mm away from the model to keep surface quality.

🎨 Specialty and effect filaments

Most “effect” filaments are a base polymer (usually PLA, sometimes PETG or ABS) plus a pigment, filler or additive. They print nearly like the base polymer, with some predictable side effects: abrasion from hard fillers, clogging from large particles, brittleness from high filler loading, and heat sensitivity from organic fillers. A good rule is: use the base polymer’s profile, then slow down, enlarge the nozzle, and test.

Silk, gradient and dual-color

Silk PLA has a polymer blend and additives that give a pearly, metallic-like sheen. It prints slightly hotter (215–235 °C) and slower, strings more, is weaker between layers than plain PLA and shows overhang defects clearly. Gradient and dual-color (“co-extruded”) filaments are made of two or more colors co-extruded side by side so that the color changes along the spool or with the viewing angle. They print like silk. The color transition occurs along the filament, so the pattern depends on the model’s path, not on its geometry; expect surprises and wasted color on prime towers. Use them for single-color-looking prints where a decorative effect is welcome. Multi-material printing with real AMS color changes gives you control instead (see Multi-color).

Use a hardened nozzle for metal and stone

Brass wears fast with metal and ceramic powders, and wood fill will clog a 0.2–0.3 mm nozzle. Choose a 0.6 mm hardened nozzle and print slowly with a low retraction. Do not leave a nozzle full of wood-fill hot, because it will char. Purge with PLA at the end of the job.

Glow-in-the-dark, photochromic and thermochromic

  • Glow (phosphorescent): strontium aluminate pigment charges under light and releases it slowly. It is very abrasive (hardened nozzle). Brightness depends on wall thickness: thin parts glow weakly. Charge with a UV or strong white lamp for a few minutes. Glow filaments can jam some filament switches and tracks; Bambu’s documentation lists glow among the filaments that have trouble on certain filament-track mechanisms of the X2D, so check compatibility.
  • Photochromic: pigment that changes color under UV (outdoor sunlight) and returns indoors. Colors fade after many cycles. Works best on thin, light-colored walls.
  • Thermochromic: changes color at a set temperature (often 28–31 °C, “body heat”, or ~45 °C). It is sensitive to nozzle temperature: prints that are too hot permanently damage the pigment. Use the lowest recommended nozzle temperature.

Conductive, ESD-safe and magnetic

  • Conductive PLA / TPU (carbon black): volume resistivity of roughly 10²–10⁴ Ω·cm. It works for touch sensors, pressure pads and simple capacitive buttons, not for carrying current: expect hundreds to thousands of ohms per centimeter of trace. Layer-to-layer contact varies, so measure results.
  • ESD-safe (static-dissipative): carbon-filled PETG, ABS, PC or PA with surface resistivity of 10⁴–10⁹ Ω. Use it for electronics trays, fixtures and tool holders to bleed static. It is abrasive and brittle at higher loading.
  • Magnetic (iron-filled PLA): attracted by magnets but not magnetic on its own. It is heavy, abrasive, and often rusts if polished, which some people use deliberately.

Foaming and lightweight filaments

These filaments contain microspheres or a chemical foaming agent that expands in the hotend. The result is a part up to 50 % lighter than solid, with a matte texture and reduced layer lines. They are popular for RC aircraft. Key points: tune flow ratio and temperature, since the expansion depends on temperature (at e.g. 230 °C vs 200 °C you get different densities); expect weak parts compared to ordinary PLA. Bambu’s documentation lists PLA Aero as one of the materials that can cause trouble on the X2D filament-track switch, so confirm in compatibility notes before loading it in the AMS.

Transparent and translucent

Clear PETG, PC, PMMA and some PLA grades produce translucent parts, but 3D printing leaves tiny air gaps between lines that scatter light, so “clear” prints look frosted. Tricks: print with a high flow (105–110 %), thin layers (0.05–0.1 mm), a hot nozzle, slow speed and no infill gaps (100 % solid), then sand and clear-coat or flame-polish. Natural, undyed filaments are typically the clearest but lose color. Expect translucent, not transparent.

Recycled and bio-based filaments

Recycled PETG (rPETG), recycled PLA, ocean-plastic filaments and bio-based PA (e.g. PA11 from castor oil) are chemically similar to their virgin counterparts but can vary batch to batch in color, impurities and moisture. They print fine at the base polymer’s settings; test a new batch with a temperature tower. “Bio-based” means feedstock, not biodegradable. Wood-fill and coffee-fill are filler composites, not compostable plastics. See also biodegradability myths and the economics and ethics reference.

Wax and casting filaments

Special casting filaments (PMMA-like, wax-like, and burnout PLA) vaporize or melt out cleanly of an investment mold, leaving a cavity that a jeweler or foundry fills with metal. They print slowly, with a low temperature, at fine layer heights. Expect needed supports to be small. Use the filament maker’s burnout schedule, and test with a sacrificial piece. Regular PLA leaves ash and is not suitable for fine jewelry casting.

Specialty “engineering” lines

Makers sell proprietary grades: flame-retardant ABS/PC (UL94 V-0), food-contact-grade PP, medical-grade PA, electro-conductive PEEK, and radiation-shielding fills. These behave like the base polymer but are tuned to a certification or datasheet. Buy them for the property you need, check the paperwork (and what the certification covers, such as the filament but not the printed part), and follow the vendor’s print profile. 📐 Designing for flexible materials

A good flexible part is designed, not just printed in a soft material. Elastomers deform under load, so the geometry controls how they behave:

  • Wall count sets stiffness. Two perimeters with 10 % gyroscopic infill feel squishy; four perimeters with 40 % feel nearly solid. Change the walls first, then the infill, when tuning “feel”.
  • Infill pattern matters. Gyroid and cubic give even compression in all directions; grid and rectilinear are springier in one direction. Lightning infill is too fragile for TPU unless the part is mostly shell.
  • Avoid thin, tall unsupported posts. Soft TPU columns wobble while printing, ruin accuracy and deflect under the nozzle. Print them thicker than you would in PLA or support them with a rib.
  • Use generous fillets. Elastomers concentrate stress in sharp corners and tear from them; a 1–2 mm radius dramatically improves life.
  • Design tight press fits and interference. A seal or gasket should be 3–10 % undersized relative to what it grips; TPU tolerates a lot of stretch without cracking.
  • Print orientation: layer lines are weakest in tension perpendicular to the layers, but TPU’s intrinsic toughness hides this far better than in rigid plastics. Orient for best surface finish and fewest supports.
  • Overhangs and bridging: TPU bridges surprisingly well when cooled aggressively, but overhangs droop; limit them to about 45° or use thin supports.

A reliable TPU workflow

  1. Dry the spoolFollow the maker’s temperature (many people use 55–70 °C for 6–8 h), then print from a dry box or sealed feed.
  2. Check the pathRemove slack and long PTFE runs; use an external spool holder for very soft grades and make sure the filament unspools freely.
  3. Pick the nozzle0.4 mm for 90A–95A; 0.6 mm for 85A. Make sure the hotend is clean (cold-pull with PLA or PETG first).
  4. Print a 20 mm test cube slowlyStart at 20–30 mm/s and 220 °C, then increase speed until under-extrusion appears and back off 20 %.
  5. Tune retraction lastRaise it from zero in 0.2 mm steps until stringing is acceptable, no further.
  6. Remove carefullyCool the plate and peel gently from an edge so that the elastic part does not tear.

🧭 Choosing between flexible options

Need a gasket or pad

TPU 90A–95A, 3–4 perimeters, gyroid infill. If it has to seal against oil, check the datasheet for the chemical resistance of the base polymer (polyether grades resist hydrolysis better).

Need a grippy shell

TPU 95A over a rigid PETG or PLA core using multi-material printing, with a dovetail or interlocking pattern for security.

Need something stretchy

TPU 85A or softer, slow on the main nozzle with direct feed. If the part must survive millions of flexes, consider TPC (copolyester elastomer) for fatigue life.

Flexible part over rigid core

If you want a rubbery surface on a firm part, print the core in PETG or PLA and the skin in TPU as a second material. Prime tower and purge volumes for TPU are small. Avoid putting TPU in the AMS with soft grades and use the matching profile. A plain overlap of 0.4–0.8 mm with a few mechanical ribs holds better than a flat interface.

✅ Key takeaways

  • Hardness decides difficulty: 95A TPU is easy; 85A and softer need slow direct-drive printing and, on the X2D, the main nozzle only.
  • Short, constrained filament path, small slow retraction, dry filament: the three basics of flexibles.
  • Soluble supports give clean cavities but demand dryness and clean nozzles. Pair incompatible polymers (PLA vs PETG) for easy release.
  • Specialty fills inherit the base polymer’s settings, plus abrasion, brittleness and clog risks. Use hardened nozzles for glow, metal, stone and fiber.
  • Check the vendor’s datasheet and the compatibility list before loading anything unusual into the AMS.
  • Compare options in the material picker, then try the Level 2 exercises.
