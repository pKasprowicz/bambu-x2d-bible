PLA, PETG & everyday plastics

The three plastics behind most of the world’s 3D prints: how each one works, how to tune it, where it fails, and how to fix the usual problems.

Beginner⏱ 35 min read
What you will learn

  • The whole PLA family (plus, tough, high-speed, matte, silk, marble, wood, CF, aero, glow, color-change) and the quirks of each.
  • Starting settings for temperature, cooling, retraction, speed and bed, and how to adjust them.
  • How to anneal PLA, fix material-specific faults, store spools and see through common myths.

🔰 Why these three

If you print for a year, roughly nine parts in ten will probably be PLA or PETG. They print at moderate temperatures on almost any machine, do not need an enclosure, and are inexpensive. PCTG, a close cousin of PETG, is increasingly popular for tougher parts. Before engineering materials make sense (Engineering plastics), you should be comfortable with these three. A good rule: start with PLA, move to PETG when you need heat resistance up to about 70 °C, toughness or outdoor tolerance, and only then think about anything harder. 🟢 PLA: the friendly one

PLA (polylactic acid) is a polyester made from fermented plant sugars (corn, sugarcane, cassava). It melts at low temperature, shrinks very little, and adheres to almost any clean bed, so it is the easiest filament to print. It is rigid and takes sharp detail, and it comes in more colors and finishes than any other material. Its two weaknesses are low heat resistance (it softens at about 55–60 °C) and brittleness: it snaps rather than bends.

What PLA is good at, and what it is not

Strengths

  • Easiest to print; low nozzle and bed temperature
  • Very low warp; no enclosure required
  • Stiff, crisp detail and sharp corners
  • Huge range of colors and finishes
  • Inexpensive; plant-derived
  • Low odor while printing

Limits

  • Softens around 55–60 °C: cars, kettles, radiators, direct sun
  • Brittle: snaps under impact or flexing
  • Creeps under constant load, even below Tg
  • Poor UV and outdoor lifetime (months, not years)
  • Weak to many solvents; hydrolyzes when wet and warm
  • Not suitable for sliding or wear

The creep problem (🔧 intermediate)

Plastics under steady load deform slowly. That is creep. It speeds up dramatically as the temperature nears the glass transition. A PLA clip that holds a weight happily at 20 °C may stretch noticeably at 40 °C within days and give way at 55 °C within hours. Any part that carries a continuous load (springs, hooks, belt clamps, shelves, bike mounts) needs a material with a margin: PETG for modest load, ABS/ASA, nylon or CF-composites for more.

X2D note: PLA on the auxiliary nozzle

Bambu’s compatibility notes recommend official PLA, especially for the auxiliary (right) nozzle, which is fed through a longer path and prints at slightly lower quality than the main nozzle. Some PLA flavors (Aero, Glow, CF) are flagged as problematic with the filament track switch. Always check Filament compatibility before loading a new special PLA, and prefer the main nozzle for fine detail.

🔧 Tuning: temperature, cooling, retraction, speed

Each setting has one purpose. Think of them as five dials: how hot, how cool, how far you pull back, how fast, and how much sticks to the bed. Start from your slicer’s official or vendor profile for your spool; change one dial at a time.

Temperature

Hotter plastic flows better, bonds between layers more strongly and shines more. But it also strings more, sags on bridges, and degrades if left sitting. The sweet spot is the lowest temperature that still gives a strong bond at your speed. Higher speed needs higher temperature because the plastic has less time in the hot zone. Use a temperature tower for each new spool: a quick print with the nozzle temperature stepping down every few millimeters.

  • PLA: start at 210 °C; high-speed grades 220–240 °C; silk 215–230 °C; wood 195–210 °C.
  • PETG: start at 240–250 °C; many brands print well at 235–255 °C.
  • PCTG: start at 255 °C; range 245–275 °C.

Cooling

PLA needs lots of cooling: it stays soft and sags if it is not chilled quickly. Use 80–100 % fan after the first layers, 100 % on bridges and overhangs, and minimum layer time of about 6–10 seconds on tiny parts. PETG wants less cooling: too much weakens layer bonds, but too little lets overhangs droop. Try 30–60 % with higher values on overhangs and bridges. PETG on tall thin features needs a short minimum layer time to stay stable.

Retraction and stringing

Retraction pulls the filament back a short distance before the nozzle travels across a gap, so that molten plastic does not ooze out and leave strings. Too little leaves hairs; too much grinds filament, causes clogs, or pulls air into the melt. On a direct-drive extruder (extruder gear on the toolhead), 0.4–1.0 mm is typical. On Bowden setups (the filament pushed through a long tube), 3–6 mm. PETG is stringier than PLA because it is stickier, so it benefits from a slightly longer retraction, slower travel acceleration and a cooler nozzle.

Speed and volumetric flow

The real speed limit is not mm/s but volumetric flow, the volume of plastic the hotend can melt per second (mm³/s). It equals line width × layer height × print speed. A standard 0.4 mm hotend melts about 12–20 mm³/s of PLA; a high-flow hotend can reach 30 mm³/s or more. PETG melts more slowly and needs a lower limit, about 8–14 mm³/s. If you exceed your hotend’s limit, you get under-extrusion: gaps, rough walls, weak layers, sometimes extruder clicking. See Speed, cooling & quality.

Bed and first layer

  • PLA sticks well to textured or smooth PEI at 55–60 °C; on glass use a glue stick or hairspray. Lower the bed to 45–50 °C if you see elephant foot (a flared first layer).
  • PETG bonds very strongly to PEI at 70–85 °C: sometimes too strongly. Use a thin layer of glue stick as a release agent, or a textured plate, and let the bed cool before removal. Never print PETG directly on a smooth PEI sheet without a separator, as it can pull off bits of the coating.
  • PCTG sticks like PETG; same release-layer trick.

🔵 PETG: the all-rounder

PETG is PET (the plastic of drink bottles) with glycol added during polymerization. The “G” stops it from crystallizing, which makes it clear-capable, tough, easier to print than pure PET, and gives it a Tg of roughly 70–80 °C. It is more flexible than PLA, much more impact resistant, more resistant to water, many chemicals and UV, and its layers bond very well. The cost: it is stickier, so it strings and blobs, scratches easily, and it can bond so strongly to the bed that it damages it.

Where PETG shines

Mechanical brackets, water-exposed parts, outdoor-light use, bottles and containers (non-food-contact, see myths), protective covers, tools, drone parts and anything that has to survive a drop.

Where PETG disappoints

Very fine detail and sharp overhangs, bridging, cosmetic surfaces that must not scratch, sliding bearings, sustained temperatures above 70 °C, and prints that need to be sanded to a polish (it gums up).

PETG tuning checklist

  1. Dry itWet PETG gives cloudy, bubbly, stringy prints. Dry at 60–65 °C for 6–8 h if it hisses.
  2. Nozzle 240–250 °C, bed 70–80 °CGo hotter only for speed. Cooler reduces strings but weakens layers.
  3. Fan 30–60 %Higher on overhangs. For maximum strength, run 20–30 %.
  4. Z-offset slightly looserPETG likes a bit more room: squish it too hard and it forms blobs, ridges and nozzle-dragging “zits”. A first layer a hair higher than for PLA is usually best.
  5. Slow outer walls and keep flow stablePrint outer walls at 60–100 mm/s, with coasting or pressure advance tuned (Calibration playbook).
  6. Wipe, don’t dragUse “wipe on retract” and avoid crossing walls so any blob does not collect on the nozzle and show up later as a lump.
  7. Use a glue-stick separatorParticularly on smooth PEI.
The nozzle blob problem

Sticky PETG can build up a blob on the nozzle and later drag it across the part. Heat the nozzle to 250 °C and wipe it with a brass brush (hot!) before printing. In a dedicated case, a silicone nozzle sock and a long, slow final wipe at the end of each layer reduce it. Bambu’s own guide has more on hotend blob.

X2D: PETG on which nozzle?

PETG is fully usable on the main hotend. On the auxiliary hotend, Bambu’s table lists PETG as printable but not a primary recommendation, because the auxiliary head moves up and down between jobs and its remote extrusion path gives slightly less precise response. Keep PETG on the main nozzle when quality matters, and use the auxiliary nozzle for supports or simple dual-material parts. Details: Dual-nozzle printing.

🔷 PCTG: PETG’s tougher cousin

PCTG is a copolyester similar to PETG, but with a different monomer balance (more cyclohexanedimethanol, “CHDM”, in the polymer). It is tougher, more shatter-resistant, optically clearer, and often prints at a slightly higher temperature. It has a bit lower stiffness than PETG, and handles chemical and impact stress well, which makes it a favorite for protective covers, tool handles and parts that get dropped. Its settings sit close to PETG’s, usually with a hotter nozzle (250–270 °C), and a hotter bed (70–90 °C); it dislikes drafts and also needs thorough drying.

PCTG strings a little more than PETG and gums on the nozzle more readily. If a PETG profile gives you blobs, expect them with PCTG too and raise the nozzle by 5 °C only when layer adhesion suffers.

Ask: “Will it sit in a warm place, take a drop, get wet, or live outside?” If yes to any, take PETG or PCTG. If it is for looks, fit tests, jigs on your desk, or highly detailed models, PLA wins. If you simply cannot decide, print a small test in each.

X2D: keep PLA’s chamber cool

The X2D can heat its chamber up to 65 °C, which is fantastic for ABS, PC and nylon but harmful to PLA: heat creeping up the filament path can soften the filament before the nozzle and clog it. Leave chamber heating off for PLA and PETG (the printer’s intakes can flush the chamber with cool air for PLA-class materials), and see Bambu Studio settings & profiles for chamber-temperature options.

🔥 Annealing PLA for heat resistance (🚀 advanced)

Printed PLA is mostly amorphous because it cools too fast to crystallize. If you heat it above its glass transition (≈60 °C) and hold it, the chains rearrange into crystals. Crystalline PLA keeps its stiffness to much higher temperatures: parts that softened at 60 °C may hold shape at 90–110 °C. The price is shrinkage and distortion, typically 1–5 % in some or all directions, and the result is more brittle. Best results come from PLA grades made for annealing (often called HT-PLA or crystallizing PLA); ordinary PLA anneals less predictably.

  1. Test on a spare print firstMeasure it before and after to learn the shrinkage in X, Y and Z for your brand.
  2. Pack itPut the part in a metal or glass tray, buried in fine salt or sand so thin features are supported.
  3. Heat graduallyPreheat the oven to 90–100 °C (check it with a separate thermometer: kitchen ovens are inaccurate) and hold 30–60 minutes. Never exceed 110 °C; PLA softens badly.
  4. Cool slowlyTurn the oven off and let it fall to room temperature with the door closed.
  5. Plan for shrinkageScale critical dimensions in the model, or accept small distortion for non-fitted parts.
Not for food

Do not use a kitchen oven for anything that touches food afterwards without cleaning it thoroughly; ventilate during heating, since hot PLA can emit a faint sweet smell. Better: use a dedicated filament dryer or small hobby oven.

📦 Storage and drying in brief

PLA absorbs water slowly (often under 0.5 % by weight after weeks), but when it is wet enough to print badly it also becomes brittle. PETG and PCTG absorb more, and show it as haze and stringing. Keep opened spools in a sealed bag or box with desiccant and a hygrometer, aiming for below about 20 % relative humidity for PETG and below about 30–40 % for PLA. If a spool misbehaves, dry it: PLA at 45–55 °C for 4–8 hours, PETG at 60–65 °C for 6–8 hours. Do not dry PLA above about 55 °C or it can soften and fuse on the spool. The full guide, including dryer types and the AMS 2 Pro’s 65 °C limit, is in Drying & storage.

🧠 Myths worth busting

“PLA is food safe”

The raw polymer is a food-contact grade plastic, but a printed object is a different thing. Layer lines trap food and bacteria, the filament contains pigments and additives that are not food-tested, brass nozzles can contain traces of lead, and washing at high temperature deforms the part. Treat 3D-printed items as single-use or short-contact at most, never for hot liquids, acidic food or long storage. Coatings that are explicitly food-safe can seal a surface, but they do not make an unknown filament safe. PETG is not magically food safe either; the same cautions apply.

“PLA is biodegradable, so I can bury it”

PLA is bio-based and industrially compostable: at about 58 °C or above, with moisture and microbes, it breaks down in weeks to months. In a garden bin, landfill or the sea, it persists for many years. Printed parts are dense, which slows degradation. Do not throw prints in the compost; instead, reuse, regrind, or take them to a facility that accepts PLA. See Economics, licensing & sustainability.

“PETG is recycled like water bottles”

PETG contains glycol and melts differently from bottle PET, so it can contaminate PET recycling streams. It is technically recyclable, but print waste is rarely collected. Reuse spools, use refills and keep failed prints for regrinding or test pieces.

“PLA+ is a different plastic”

Not usually. It is PLA with additives, and each brand’s mix is secret. Two “PLA+” spools can differ in temperature, strength and stringiness.

“Higher temperature always means stronger parts”

It improves layer bonding up to a point, but too hot causes degradation, oozing, and poor detail. The right temperature is the lowest one that gives a good bond at your speed.

Key takeaways

  • PLA is the easiest and most detailed; its limits are 55–60 °C, brittleness, creep and weather.
  • PETG and PCTG trade easy printing for toughness, temperature (70–85 °C), water and chemical resistance.
  • Cooling: PLA 80–100 %, PETG 30–60 %. Retraction and temperature fix stringing once the filament is dry.
  • PETG needs a release layer on PEI; PLA needs a cool chamber on heated-chamber printers like the X2D.
  • Annealing raises PLA’s heat resistance at the cost of shrinkage; special crystallizing PLA works best.
  • “Food safe” and “biodegradable” are context-dependent claims; treat both with skepticism.
