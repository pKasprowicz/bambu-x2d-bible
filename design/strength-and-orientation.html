Strength & orientation

A printed part is a stack of welded lines, strong along the line and weaker between layers. Learn to orient, shape and reinforce parts so the loads follow the strong direction, and learn how to find out (cheaply) whether a design is strong enough.

Intermediate⏱ 50 min read
What you will learn

What anisotropy means for FDM, how to choose a build orientation from the load path, why walls matter more than infill, how stiffness depends on I = b·h³/12, how creep, heat and fatigue limit printed parts, how to test a design at home, which safety factors to use and how to read a broken part. Numbers marked indicative are typical ranges, not test data: always test your own material and printer.

🔰 Anisotropy: the Z direction is weak

An FDM part is made of lines (extruded beads) laid side by side in layers. Inside one line the plastic is continuous and strong. Between two neighbouring lines in the same layer, and between two layers, the plastic is only welded while still warm. The weld is good but not perfect: it has the strength of the polymer minus whatever the cooling, speed and temperature took away. This gives a part direction-dependent strength, called anisotropy. The tensile strength across layers (Z) is typically only 40–80 % of the strength along the lines (XY), and impact and fatigue strength across layers are worse than that.

Two practical points follow. First, layer adhesion is improvable: a hotter nozzle, a slower print, less part cooling and a warm chamber (up to 65 °C in the X2D) all increase the weld. Second, the weakness is a design question: you do not fix it by turning knobs, you avoid loading the part across the layers. The next sections show how.

X2D note

An actively heated chamber improves layer bonding in ABS, ASA, PC and nylon, which is the main reason enclosed printers produce stronger engineering parts. For the temperatures and materials, see Engineering materials & chamber and Engineering plastics.

🔰 Load paths

A load travels through a part from where it is applied to where it is supported, along the stiffest available path, a bit like water flowing downhill. Draw that path (a pencil line on the sketch is enough). Wherever the path turns a sharp corner, passes through a thin neck or crosses a layer interface, that spot will be the first to fail. Your goals are to keep the path short and smooth, put material along it and make sure the plastic is strong in the direction the path pulls it.

🔧 Orientation strategy

Orientation is the cheapest strength upgrade: it costs nothing. The rule: orient the part so the highest tensile stress runs along the lines and in the layer plane, never across layers. Typically that means laying the part so its longest or most loaded dimension is flat on the bed. The price is support material or a worse surface on the faces that now overhang; for many parts that is a fair trade.

Hook, clip, snap armLayers run along the armBending stress is along the arm

🔧 Walls vs infill

Beginners increase infill to make a part stronger. It helps, but not much. For most loads (bending, torsion) the stress is highest at the outer surface, so the perimeters do the work, while the infill acts as a lightweight filling that keeps the walls apart and supports the top layers. Doubling the walls from 2 to 4 adds far more strength than doubling the infill from 15 to 30 %, and costs less time.

Perimeters first

Spend your print time in this order: (1) orientation, (2) wall count (at least 3 for functional parts, 4–6 for loaded ones), (3) top and bottom layers (4–6 at 0.2 mm), then (4) infill (15–30 % gyroid or cubic is enough for most parts). Raise infill above 50 % only for threaded or insert regions and where a screw must bite.

🔧 Material choice vs load type

The right material depends on how the part is loaded and for how long, not on a single "strength" number. A stiff, brittle material (PLA) is superb for a static desk stand and terrible for a clip that is dropped. A tough one (PETG, ABS, PC, nylon) forgives overloads. 🔧 Impact vs stiffness

Strength is a family of properties. Stiffness (Young's modulus E) tells you how little the part bends; toughness tells you how much energy it absorbs before breaking; strength tells you the load at which it yields or breaks. A material that is strong and stiff, like PLA, often has low toughness: it breaks suddenly. Nylon and PC combine good strength with high toughness, but are less stiff than PLA.

🔧 Creep, fatigue and heat

Creep is slow deformation under a constant load: a PLA shelf that holds a book today sags over weeks. It gets rapidly worse as the temperature approaches the glass transition. Fatigue is cracking from repeated loading far below the static strength: a snap-fit that works 5 times fails on the 50th. Heat reduces stiffness and strength long before the part melts. Three simple rules cover most designs: keep long-term stress under about 20–25 % of the short-term strength for PLA/PETG, use material with a higher glass transition for hot places, and give every repeatedly loaded feature a generous radius.

🔧 Stress concentrations

Stress is not uniform. Wherever the geometry changes abruptly, such as a hole, a sharp inside corner, a thread root or a layer notch, the local stress is a multiple (Kt) of the average. A round hole in a plate under tension has Kt ≈ 3: the plastic at the edge sees three times the load that the rest of the plate sees. A sharp 90° inside corner is worse. The fix is cheap: a fillet radius of at least 0.25 × the wall thickness cuts Kt to about 1.5, and a radius of half the thickness to about 1.25.

Printed notches

Layer lines are tiny notches that print into every surface. A 0.2 mm layer line already imposes some stress concentration at the surface, so printed parts are more notch-sensitive than the same plastic injection moulded. That is why generous fillets matter more in print than in the datasheet.

🔧 Beam stiffness: why height matters

When a beam bends, stiffness depends on its cross-section through the second moment of area I. For a rectangle of width b and height h (measured in the direction of the bending load) I = b·h³/12. The cube on h is the key: doubling the height makes the beam 8 times stiffer, while doubling the width only doubles it. The tip deflection of a cantilever of length L under force F is δ = F·L³ / (3·E·I) and the peak surface stress is σ = 6·F·L / (b·h²).

This also explains orientation for stiffness: a shelf bracket printed with its 10 mm dimension vertical bends 4 times less than one printed with the 20 mm dimension vertical... but remember that the strong direction for strength is separate from the tall direction for stiffness. In the model, make the section tall and orient the layers along the load.

🔧 Ribs, hollow vs solid

Ribs are the practical application of I = bh³/12: a rib adds height at a tiny cost in material. In the comparison below a 20 mm wide, 2 mm thick plate becomes 29 times stiffer with a 2 × 8 mm rib, using 40 % more material, and it is 3.6 times stiffer than a plate twice as thick that uses 43 % more material than the ribbed one.

Hollow vs solid: in printing, an infill-less shell is already "hollow", and the infill is the filling that stops the walls from buckling. A hollow section made of 3–4 walls has most of the stiffness of the solid one (Figure 8: 67 % of I at 42 % of the area). For loads that bend the part, thicker walls and lower infill beat thin walls and high infill, at the same weight.

🔧 Infill patterns by application

The infill pattern changes how the load is shared and how quickly the print goes. Gyroid and cubic are the default recommendations: they have no long straight weak planes. Pick a different pattern only for a reason.

🔧 Five kinds of load

Every force on a part is a combination of five basic loads. Identify which one dominates each feature before you choose the orientation.

🚀 Testing strength at home

You can test a printed part without a lab. All you need is a bucket of water (or a hanging weight), a luggage scale and a repeatable procedure. The goal is not an exact number but a comparison: is revision B stronger than revision A, and how much margin does the design have against the load it needs to carry?

Safety factors

A safety factor divides the failure load you measure by the load you allow in service. Printed parts have large scatter and unknowns (layer quality, humidity, temperature), so factors are larger than for machined metals.

🔧 Annealing

Annealing is controlled heating of a printed part to let the polymer chains relax and, for semi-crystalline polymers, crystallise. The result is a higher heat-deflection temperature and often stronger layer bonds. The price is dimensional change (0.5–3 % shrinkage, sometimes warp), so do it only on parts you can afford to scale in CAD, and keep tolerances in mind. Use a kitchen oven checked with a separate thermometer. Do it in a filament maker's instructions window when they specify one.

🔧 Fibre-reinforced designs and continuous fibre

Chopped-fibre filaments (carbon or glass, typically 5–25 % by weight) are stiffer and creep less than their base polymer, but they are less tough, strongly anisotropic (fibres align with the line) and abrasive: they need a hardened or abrasion-resistant nozzle. Check X2D filament compatibility before using them. Their effect on design: make arms and ribs longer, bigger fillets, and never use them for snap-fits or living hinges.

🚀 Topology optimisation and lattices

Topology optimisation software starts from a block of material, applies your loads and supports and removes everything that carries little stress. The result looks organic: struts and arches along the load paths. It saves 30–60 % of the mass at the same stiffness. It suits 3D printing well because complex shapes cost nothing extra, but remember the usual rules (overhang ≤ 45°, minimum feature 0.8–1.2 mm, orientation along the load). Lattices (gyroid, octet, honeycomb) can replace infill or lighten a part intentionally, and graded lattices put denser cells where stress is high.

🔧 Designing printed threads and bosses

Threads and bosses are where layer direction bites hardest. A vertical boss (axis along Z) has thread teeth in rings that hold well in shear, and hoop stress runs along the layers. But if the boss is pulled out along Z, it breaks off at its base across layers: add a fillet and gussets. A horizontal boss (axis in XY) has its layers parallel to the screw axis, so the teeth shear along layer interfaces: it strips easily. Use heat-set inserts if a horizontal boss is unavoidable. Detailed dimensions are in Joints & fasteners.

🔧 Printed vs injection-moulded parts

A printed part is never a drop-in copy of a moulded one. Injection moulding gives isotropic properties, a smooth surface and tight tolerance, but needs a mould that costs thousands. Printing gives complete freedom of shape for no tooling. The break-even point depends on size and complexity, usually a few hundred to a few thousand parts.

Key takeaways

  • FDM is anisotropic: the Z direction has 40–80 % of the XY strength. Never put the main tensile load across layers.
  • Orient first, add walls second (≥ 3, loaded parts 4–6), infill last (15–30 % gyroid or cubic).
  • Stiffness depends on I = bh³/12: double the height, get 8× the stiffness. Use ribs, tall sections and hollow shapes.
  • Round every inside corner (r ≥ 0.25 × thickness) and give holes edge distance ≥ 2 × Ø.
  • Use tough materials for impact, high-Tg ones for heat and constant load; PLA creeps and softens near 55–60 °C.
  • Test your design: failure load ÷ working load ≥ 2 for static, 4–6 for fatigue; use metal for anything safety-critical.
  • Read failures: delamination = orientation, brittle snap = corner, necking = overload, beach marks = fatigue.
