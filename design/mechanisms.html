Mechanism catalog

Hinges, latches, mounts, slides, rotary drives, clamps and springs that print well, side by side. Each entry shows how the mechanism works, the dimensions to start from, how to orient it on the plate, which material to use and when to pick something else.

What you will learn

How to pick the right mechanism for a job (a lid that stays open, a box that locks, a panel that slides, a part that hangs on a rail) and size it for printing. The catalog complements two other pages: Joints & fasteners explains the underlying rules (snap-fit strain, pins, print-in-place gaps) and Design patterns shows complete parts built from them. Here the focus is the mechanism itself and its alternatives.

🔰 How to use this catalog

A mechanism is an arrangement of parts that guides, locks, holds or converts motion. Most printed products need only a handful of them, and almost every one has several variants that trade hardware, durability, play and print difficulty against each other. The entries are grouped by job: hinges, latches and catches, mounts, slides and guides, rotary and indexing, clamps and adjusters and springs and flexures.

Every card has the same parts: a diagram, a one-line use, the starting dimensions, the print orientation and material, and choose / avoid. Dimensions assume a 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layers and a calibrated printer. They are starting values: print a one-joint test piece before you commit, as described in testing a mechanism.

🔰 Hinges

A hinge lets one part rotate about an axis fixed to another. Printed hinges differ in where the axle comes from (a separate pin, a printed cone, the plastic itself) and therefore in how much play they have, how many cycles they survive and whether they need assembly. All of them want the hinge axis parallel to the build plate: the knuckles are then stacks of rings whose layers wrap around the pin instead of splitting along it.

Pin-and-knuckle hinge

Use: box lids, doors, flaps, anything opened daily.

  • Pin: steel rod or 1.75 mm filament; hole = pin + 0.3–0.4 mm in the turning knuckles, + 0.1–0.2 mm where the pin is held
  • Knuckle length ≥ 3 × pin Ø; outer Ø ≈ 2.5–3 × pin Ø; 0.3–0.5 mm gap between knuckles
  • Retain the pin: mushroom a filament end with a hot tip, or add a cap
  • Print with the axis horizontal; PETG or PLA

Choose for durability and low play. Avoid when assembly is not possible. Rules: pins & hinges.

Print-in-place cone hinge

Use: one-piece cases, toys, folding stands, articulated models.

  • Cone half-angle 45° or steeper; gap 0.3–0.5 mm per side, never less than two layers vertically
  • Two opposed cones (one at each end) keep the parts from separating
  • Break it free gently after cooling; a little play is the price of no assembly
  • Axis horizontal; PLA or PETG; slow outer walls for round cones

Choose when the product must come off the plate finished. Avoid for precise or heavily loaded hinges. Rules: print-in-place joints.

Snap-on (lift-off) hinge

Use: lids you remove for cleaning or filling, battery doors, detachable covers.

  • Boss Ø 5–8 mm; clip bore = boss + 0.3 mm; clip wall 1.6–2.4 mm
  • Mouth 85–90 % of the boss Ø in PETG, 90–95 % in PLA; check the arm strain
  • Best printed with the hinge axis vertical, so the C lies in the layer plane and opens without bending layers apart; if the lid must print flat, make the clip thicker and use PETG or nylon
  • PETG, nylon; PLA cracks after a few removals

Choose for parts that separate. Avoid in PLA or under shock loads. Rules: snap-fits.

Living hinge

Use: flip-top caps, cable clips, folding boxes, one-piece clamshells.

  • PP: 0.4–0.5 mm web, thousands of cycles; PETG: 0.4–0.6 mm, 10–200 cycles; TPU 95A: 0.8–1.2 mm, practically unlimited
  • Web 2–3 mm long (3–5 mm in TPU), with generous fillets into both plates
  • PLA: do not use

Choose for zero-part-count products in PP or TPU. Avoid in PLA and for daily use in PETG. Rules: living hinges.

Detent (click-stop) hinge

Use: lids that stay open, adjustable stands, folding legs.

  • Bumps 0.3–0.5 mm high with 30–45° flanks; dimples 0.1 mm larger
  • The spring: a slit behind the outer knuckle, or one knuckle on a thin flexure
  • For smooth friction instead of clicks, run the pin with a snug fit (+0.1 mm) or add an O-ring between knuckles
  • PETG or nylon; PLA bumps wear flat

Choose to hold an angle. Avoid when the holding torque must be large: add a stay or prop instead.

Piano (continuous) hinge

Use: long lids, cases, folding panels, display covers.

  • Knuckles 8–15 mm long, gap 0.3–0.5 mm; one continuous steel rod as the pin
  • Print in place with cone ends per knuckle, or as two halves and slide the rod in
  • Leaf thickness ≥ 2 mm so the lid does not twist

Choose for lids longer than about 150 mm. Avoid on short parts: two or three knuckles are easier to print.

Flexure pivot (cross-spring)

Use: precision tilts, measuring arms, grippers, instrument mounts.

  • Blade strain ≈ t · θ / (2L): a 1 mm × 15 mm blade at ±10° (0.17 rad) sees about 0.6 %
  • Blades printed flat so their layers bend; PETG, nylon or PP
  • Add hard stops so it cannot be bent past its range

Choose for small, precise motions. Avoid for more than about ±15°. Background: compliant mechanisms.

Pro trick

Add a stop to every hinge: a step on the leaf that touches the other leaf at the end of travel. Without it, a lid that is opened too far levers on the knuckles and cracks them, and print-in-place hinges are pulled apart.

🔧 Latches and catches

A latch locks two parts together until someone releases it; a catch only holds them lightly, so a pull opens it. The difference is the shape of the locking face: a face square to the pull locks, a sloped face lets go. Pick the weakest closure that does the job: it is easier to use, and it fails less often than a strong latch that has to be forced.

Cantilever snap hook

Use: enclosure lids, battery doors, clip-in panels.

  • Size the beam with the strain formula; arms of 10–20 mm are typical for 1.2–2 mm thickness
  • Taper the arm to h/2 at the tip to spread the strain; 0.5–1 mm fillet at the root
  • Print so the beam lies in the layer plane
  • PETG, ABS, nylon; PLA only for one-off assembly

Choose as the default closure. Rules and strain table: snap-fits.

Push-button release latch

Use: cases that must stay shut but open by hand, buckles, module ejectors.

  • Return face 90° (locks under pull); release only by pressing the pad
  • The press travel must exceed the undercut by 0.5 mm, and the arm must survive that deflection: check its strain
  • Two opposed latches make a squeeze-to-release buckle
  • PETG or nylon

Choose when a pull must never open it. Avoid if the pad cannot be reached through the housing.

Bayonet (twist-lock)

Use: lids, lens caps, filter housings, quick-change tool heads.

  • 2–3 lugs, equally spaced; lug 3–6 mm wide, 1.5–2.5 mm deep
  • Slot width = lug + 0.4–0.6 mm; a 1–2° ramp in the run pulls the cap tight
  • Print the cap upright (lugs as short overhangs, chamfer their undersides 45°)
  • PETG, ABS; add a TPU or O-ring seal for watertight caps

Choose over a thread for a fast, short twist. Avoid when the cap must be pulled very tight; use a thread.

Over-center (draw) latch

Use: toolboxes, cases that travel, clamping lids onto a seal.

  • Go a few degrees past center and land on a stop; too far and it opens itself
  • Steel pins (Ø 2–3 mm) at the pivots; the link can be a printed spring or a wire loop
  • The pull-in comes from the link stretching 0.5–1.5 mm: make it a flexible loop or add a TPU pad on the keeper
  • PETG, nylon; lever printed flat

Choose when the lid must be pulled tight. Avoid on small, light lids: a snap is simpler.

Sliding bolt

Use: cabinet doors, gates of small enclosures, locking pins for jigs.

  • Bolt ≥ 4 × 4 mm; guide length ≥ 2 × the bolt's travel
  • Print the bolt flat (layers along its length), the guide with its channel horizontal
  • Hole in the knob for a padlock shackle if needed

Choose for a positive lock that is obvious to the user. Avoid where the door must open with one hand.

Magnetic catch

Use: cabinet doors, lids that should close softly, removable covers.

  • Pocket Ø = magnet + 0.1–0.2 mm, depth = thickness + 0.2 mm; skin 0.4–0.6 mm toward the mating face
  • Embed by pausing the print, or press in and glue from the back
  • Pull force falls quickly with distance: every extra layer of skin costs force
  • One magnet and a steel disc avoid polarity mistakes

Choose for wear-free, soft closing. Avoid near magnetic cards and where a fall must not open it. Rules: magnets.

Bump (ball) catch

Use: cabinet doors, drawer stops, sliding lids, hatch covers.

  • Arm 15–25 mm long, 1.2–1.6 mm thick; bump 0.6–1.0 mm with 30–45° flanks so it opens with a pull
  • Check the arm strain for the bump height (deflection = bump height)
  • PETG, nylon, POM-like filaments; PLA arms fatigue

Choose for a light hold that opens with a tug. Avoid where it must not open on its own under shock.

Turn button and quarter-turn cam

Use: access panels, picture backs, enclosure covers, quarter-turn fasteners.

  • Tab 6–10 mm wide, 3–4 mm thick; pivot on a screw with a washer, or a printed snap pin
  • Ramp 5–10° over the last 30° of travel; a small bump at 90° holds it
  • Quarter-turn version: a cam on a shaft through the panel, turned with a slot or knob

Choose for panels opened occasionally. Avoid in sight on cosmetic faces.

Push-push latch

Use: hidden drawer and door releases, pen mechanisms, flush lids without handles.

  • Track 1.5–2 mm deep, 0.4 mm wider than the pin; steps in the track floor force one-way travel
  • Needs a return spring (printed or steel) and a flexible pin arm
  • Print the track facing up; nylon or POM-like filament for low wear

Choose for a clean, handle-free face. Avoid unless you can test several iterations: it is the hardest latch here.

Common mistake

Making every latch as strong as possible. A 90° hook in PLA that is meant to be opened will break the first time someone pulls on it. Decide first whether the closure is permanent, releasable by a pull, or released on purpose by a button, and pick the return face and the mechanism from that.

🔧 Mounts

A mount fixes a part to something else: a wall, a rail, a tripod, a tube, a pegboard. The main question is which way the load goes. Weight pulls down along the wall, leverage pulls the top of the part away from it, and vibration slowly undoes anything held by friction alone. Good mounts let gravity push the part into its seat rather than out of it.

Keyhole slot

Use: signs, small boxes, panels, anything you hang and lift off.

  • Entry Ø = head Ø + 1 mm; slot = shank Ø + 1 mm; slot ≥ 8 mm long
  • A recess behind the slot as deep as the head plus 1 mm; rear wall 2 mm around it
  • Use two keyholes at the same height for anything wider than a hand

Choose for a hidden, tool-free hang. More: keyhole pattern.

French cleat

Use: tool walls, shelves, holders that you rearrange often.

  • Bevel 45°; strip 12–20 mm thick in print, as long as the object is wide
  • Print the strips on their back so the bevel is a 45° slope, not an overhang
  • Screw the wall strip into studs or anchors; the cleat only transfers the load

Choose for modular walls. Avoid for items that get pushed upward in use.

DIN-rail clip

Use: electronics enclosures, terminal holders and cable guides in control cabinets.

  • Common rail: 35 mm wide, 7.5 mm (or 15 mm) deep, about 1 mm thick steel flanges
  • Latch: a slider on a printed spring with a screwdriver slot; travel ≥ 2 mm
  • PETG or ABS/ASA for warm cabinets; PLA softens near hot power supplies

Choose for anything that lives in a cabinet. Avoid for heavy modules without an extra screw.

Tripod screw mount

Use: camera brackets, phone and light holders, anything for a tripod or arm.

  • 1/4-20 UNC (6.35 mm, 20 threads per inch) is the camera standard; 3/8-16 for heavier heads
  • Heat-set insert or a captive hex nut; a flat 20 mm face around it so the head can clamp
  • Add a small anti-twist pin or rubber pad if the mount must not rotate

Choose whenever photo gear is involved. Rules: heat-set inserts.

Prong (finger) mount

Use: camera and light arms, adjustable brackets, sensor mounts.

  • Match an existing system by measuring it: finger thickness and gap must fit within 0.1–0.2 mm
  • Fine radial ridges on the finger faces add grip; an M5 or similar thumbscrew with a captive nut
  • Print the fingers flat so the clamping load is across the layers, not peeling them

Choose for adjustable angles. Avoid for heavy cantilevered loads; friction will slip.

Tube and edge clip

Use: handlebar mounts, pipe and rod holders, clips on shelf edges and desk tops.

  • Mouth 85–90 % of the tube Ø in PETG, 90–95 % in PLA; arm 1.6–2.4 mm thick; check the strain
  • Bore = tube + 0.2 mm, or a TPU liner for grip and damping
  • Print the C flat (axis vertical) so it opens within the layers
  • Edge clips (on a shelf or desk) work the same way with a U-shaped jaw and a ridge

Choose for tool-free mounting on round stock. Avoid when it must not turn: add a screw clamp.

Grid baseplate

Use: drawer organizers, workshop walls, desk systems.

  • One pitch for everything (several open systems exist; follow one for compatibility)
  • 45° chamfered feet print without support; optional magnets in the feet

Choose for organizers you will extend. More: modular bins.

Strap and cable-tie anchor

Use: bike and frame mounts, cable routing, temporary fixtures.

  • Passage = tie width + 0.8 mm by tie thickness + 0.8 mm; for straps, width + 1 mm
  • The bridge spans ≤ 10 mm so it prints flat; 1 mm fillets where the tie bends
  • A V-groove or TPU pad under the part stops it rotating on a tube

Choose for quick, adjustable mounts. Avoid for permanent or cosmetic fixings.

Pegboard hook

Use: tool holders, bins and shelves on pegboard.

  • US hardboard pegboard: 1/4 in (6.35 mm) holes on 1 in (25.4 mm) centers; other boards differ, so measure
  • Hook and peg 0.3–0.5 mm under the hole size; hook depth = board thickness + 1 mm
  • Print the holder on its side so hook and peg are not layer-split

Choose for workshop walls. Avoid without the lower peg: the holder rotates out.

Magnetic mount

Use: tool holders on steel, fridge and machine mounts, removable covers.

  • Rate the mount for weight using about 25–30 % of the magnets' pull force
  • A TPU skin or rubber pad raises the friction and protects the paint
  • Skin 0.4–0.6 mm over the magnets

Choose for tool-free placement on steel. More: magnetic mount pattern.

🔧 Slides and guides

A slide constrains a part to move along one line. Printed slides fail in two ways: they bind (jam when pushed off-center) or they rattle (too much clearance). The first is a geometry problem, solved by the length of the guide; the second is a tolerance problem, solved by a test print and, where it matters, by steel rods or ball bearings doing the guiding.

Dovetail slide

Use: drawers, sliding lids, battery covers, adjustable fences.

  • Clearance 0.3 mm per side; a 0.4 mm detent bump at the end positions
  • A 1–2° taper along the length turns it into a wedge lock that tightens as it slides home
  • Run the slide axis along the printed lines and wax or dry-lube it

Choose for all-plastic slides. Rules: dovetails, drawer rails.

T-slot slide on extrusion

Use: printer and jig accessories, adjustable stops, camera sliders on aluminum extrusion.

  • Common 20 mm profile: 6 mm slot opening, M5 hardware; measure your brand
  • Tongue = slot − 0.3–0.4 mm, 5–10 mm long at each end of the carriage
  • For rolling motion use V-wheels or ball bearings on the profile instead

Choose when an extrusion frame is already there. Avoid for long-stroke smooth motion without wheels.

Rod and bushing

Use: camera and tool sliders, plotters, adjustable jigs, sliding doors.

  • Ground rods or drill rod (6, 8 or 10 mm); bushings printed with the bore vertical for roundness
  • Two rods: make one bushing a close fit and give the other 0.5 mm side play, so the carriage does not jam if the rods are not perfectly parallel
  • PETG or nylon bushings; or press in linear ball bearings

Choose for smooth, accurate linear motion. Avoid in dusty places without wipers.

Telescoping tubes

Use: adjustable legs and arms, extendable handles, height stops.

  • Walls ≥ 1.2 mm; diametral clearance 0.4–0.6 mm; overlap ≥ 1.5 × the tube width
  • Square or keyed sections stop rotation; round ones need a key
  • Print tubes upright for round sections; the button arm lies in the layer plane

Choose for stepped lengths. Avoid for continuous adjustment: use a clamp collar.

Rack and pinion

Use: focusing stages, adjustable heights, sliding doors, linear actuators.

  • Module 1.5–2 for a 0.4 mm nozzle; ≥ 17 pinion teeth (or profile shift)
  • Guide the rack in a slot so the mesh distance cannot open up
  • Print the rack on its side so the teeth are not stacked layers

Choose to turn rotation into straight travel. Gear rules: gears.

Roller carriage

Use: long-stroke sliders, doors, carriages on extrusion or flat bar.

  • Small ball bearings (for example 623, 625 or 608) on shoulder screws or pins
  • Eccentric 0.5–1 mm offset on one wheel to adjust preload
  • Wheels on a V-groove or with a printed TPU tire for grip

Choose for long travel with low friction. Avoid when space is tight: rods are slimmer.

Why sliders jam: the binding ratio

Why it works

The off-center force F at distance d tilts the slider; two corners, a distance L apart, take the moment: N = F · d / L. Their friction is 2 μ N. The slider moves only while 2 μ F d / L < F, that is while d < L / (2 μ). With printed plastic on plastic (μ ≈ 0.3) that means d < about 1.7 L. Engineers round this to the "2:1 rule": keep the guided length at least half of the distance from the guide to where you push. The fix is never a looser fit; it is a longer guide, a push point closer to the guide, or two guides far apart.

🚀 Rotary and indexing mechanisms

Rotary mechanisms carry a shaft, stop it in steps, let it turn one way only, or convert rotation into another motion. Gears are covered in Joints & fasteners; this section collects the other building blocks. Use metal shafts wherever you can: printed shafts bend and wear long before printed hubs do.

Ball-bearing seat

Use: wheels, turntables, spool holders, pulleys, anything that spins under load.

  • Pocket Ø = outer race + 0.1 mm (+0.0 for a press); depth = width + 0.2 mm; 1 mm shoulder; shaft hole 0.4 mm over the bore
  • Print the bore vertical; retain with a lip, a cover or a drop of CA glue

Choose for anything that spins fast or long. Pocket table: bearings.

Plain bushing on a pin

Use: slow pivots: levers, wheels of toys, arms, idlers.

  • Running fit +0.3–0.4 mm; length 1–1.5 × pin Ø; washers at both faces
  • PETG, nylon or PTFE-filled filaments wear far less than PLA; a dab of grease helps
  • Bore printed vertical, or as a teardrop with the axis horizontal

Choose for slow, occasional motion. Avoid for high speed or load: use a ball bearing.

Ratchet and pawl

Use: winches, tensioners, cable reels, adjustable straps, one-way clutches.

  • 12–24 teeth, 1–2 mm deep; the locking face slightly undercut (5–10°) so the pawl is pulled in under load
  • Pawl on a printed flexure arm, or a pin and a small spring
  • Print the wheel flat; PETG or nylon

Choose to allow motion one way. Avoid for silent operation: it clicks.

Detent wheel (indexing)

Use: selector knobs, rotary switches, indexing tables, adjustable angles.

  • Notches as arcs 0.5–1 mm deep; the notch flank angle sets the holding torque
  • Size the arm by its strain for the notch depth (deflection = depth)
  • Same idea as a detent hinge, but radial

Choose for positive steps both ways. Avoid where any position must be possible.

Geneva drive

Use: indexing tables, film-advance style steps, counters, kinetic models.

  • n slots give a 360°/n step per driver turn
  • Center distance C = r / sin(180°/n), where r is the pin radius on the driver (C = 1.41 r for 4 slots)
  • Steel pin; slots 0.3–0.4 mm wider than the pin

Choose for intermittent motion from steady rotation. Avoid at high speed (impact at the slot entry).

Worm drive

Use: winches, tilt and pan heads, valves, anything that must hold its position.

  • Ratio = wheel teeth ÷ worm starts (30 teeth, one start = 30 : 1)
  • Self-locking when the lead angle is below the friction angle: single-start worms usually are
  • High sliding friction: PETG or nylon worm, grease, thrust washers at both ends

Choose for big reductions that hold. Avoid where efficiency matters.

Cam and follower

Use: valves, timed pushers, kinetic sculptures, clamps.

  • Keep the pressure angle under 30° or the follower binds in its guide
  • Follower guide clearance 0.3 mm; a roller tip (small bearing) cuts wear

Choose for a set motion profile. More: cam pattern.

Crank and slider

Use: pumps, shakers, walking toys, reciprocating cutters.

  • Rod ≥ 3 × crank radius keeps the side load on the slider low
  • Steel pins at both rod ends; slider guide at least as long as it is wide (see the binding ratio)

Choose for steady reciprocation. Avoid at high speed without balancing.

Ball-and-socket joint

Use: articulated arms, phone and camera holders, figures, adjustable lamps.

  • Ball Ø 10–20 mm; socket bore 0.1–0.2 mm under the ball for friction, or +0.3 mm for free movement
  • A clamp ring or screw on the socket gives adjustable friction
  • Print-in-place version: 0.3–0.4 mm gap, socket printed around the ball

Choose for adjustment in every direction. Avoid for heavy loads far from the joint.

Shaft-to-hub connections

Use: knobs, pulleys, gears and wheels on motor or steel shafts.

  • D-flat: copy the shaft's flat, add 0.1–0.2 mm clearance and a set screw against the flat
  • Hex: printed across-flats + 0.2 mm; strong and easy to print
  • Split clamp: bore + 0.2 mm, 1–2 mm slit, M3 screw with a nut or insert
  • Hub wall ≥ 3 mm; never rely on a press fit alone for torque

Choose the clamp for round shafts, the flat or hex when the shaft has one.

🔧 Clamps and adjusters

Clamps hold something by squeezing; adjusters let the user set a position and lock it. They all convert a small, comfortable hand force into a large clamping force through a lever, a wedge, a cam or a thread, so their parts see high local stress. Put metal (pins, screws, inserts) where the force concentrates and keep the plastic in compression.

Cam-lever clamp

Use: quick-release clamps, jig hold-downs, axle locks, bike-style quick releases.

  • Offset e 2–4 mm on a 15–20 mm cam radius R; with plastic (μ ≈ 0.3) it self-locks while e < 0.3 R
  • Steel pin Ø 4–6 mm; cam printed flat with ≥ 4 walls
  • Adjust the clamp height with a thread or shims; the cam only gives the last few mm

Choose for fast, repeatable clamping. Avoid on parts that vibrate a lot: friction alone holds it.

Split (pinch) clamp

Use: collars, arm joints on tubes, knob hubs, sensor holders on rods.

  • Bore = rod + 0.2 mm; slit 1–2 mm; screw as close to the bore as the wall allows
  • Thin the material opposite the slit (a relief cut) for even clamping
  • Print with the bore axis vertical; screw in a nut pocket or insert

Choose for adjustable grip on round stock. Avoid in PLA under heat: it creeps loose.

Thumbscrew and knob

Use: tool-free adjusters, lids, clamps, the screw of a prong mount.

  • Hex pocket = across-flats + 0.2–0.3 mm; glue or press the head in
  • Knob Ø about 4–6 × the thread size (M5: 20–30 mm); flutes 2–3 mm
  • Print the knob flat, pocket facing up

Choose for anything adjusted by hand. Hardware sizes: bolted joints.

Wedge and taper lock

Use: leveling feet, drawer and door wedges, tool holders that grip by taper, shaft locks.

  • Self-locking when the taper angle is below the friction angle (about 17° for μ ≈ 0.3); 3–8° is safe
  • Shallower tapers multiply force more but need more travel
  • Print the wedge faces as side walls, not as stair-stepped top surfaces

Choose for simple, strong, tool-free locking. Avoid where frequent release is needed: tapers stick.

Spring clamp

Use: glue-up clamps, bag clips, cable and hose holders, spool clips.

  • Steel pin as the pivot; the spring printed flat, 1.2–2 mm thick, sized by its strain
  • PLA springs relax within hours under load (creep): use PETG, nylon or a rubber band
  • TPU pads on the jaws for grip

Choose for light, quick clamping. Avoid for sustained high force: use a screw.

🚀 Springs and flexures

A printed spring is a beam that bends within its strain limit. It gives you return motion, preload and click feel without extra parts, but it creeps: a printed spring held deflected for days loses part of its force, PLA most and nylon or PETG less. Use printed springs for motions that return to rest, and metal springs for anything held loaded.

Creep

A printed spring that is held deflected loses force over hours and days, faster when warm. A PLA clip on a shelf may be loose a week later; the same clip in PETG or nylon holds much longer. If a spring must stay loaded, use a metal spring and print only its seats.

🚀 Testing a mechanism before you commit

  1. Print only the joint.Cut the hinge, latch or slide out of the model with a few millimeters of surrounding material. It takes minutes instead of hours and shows whether the fit is right.
  2. Print variants side by side.Three copies with the key clearance at −0.1, ±0 and +0.1 mm, or the snap undercut at three depths. Label them on the part.
  3. Cycle it.Open and close a hinge, snap a latch or slide a drawer 50–100 times. Wear, whitening at bend roots and loosening show up quickly.
  4. Load it the wrong way.Pull a lid sideways, push a slider off-center, drop the part. Mechanisms fail through the load you did not design for.
  5. Leave it loaded and warm.A day in a warm car or near a window shows creep in clips and springs before your users do.
  6. Record the winner.Write the working clearance or undercut into the CAD parameters, per material and nozzle (see Parametric CAD workflow).

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Hinge axis vertical. Knuckles printed as stacked discs split along the layers the first time the lid is levered. Keep hinge axes parallel to the plate.
  • Flexing parts that bend across layers. Snap arms, clips, springs and living hinges must bend within the layer plane.
  • Printed pins and shafts under load. A 3 mm steel rod is many times stiffer and stronger than a printed pin of the same size, and costs almost nothing.
  • Loosening a fit to cure binding. A slider that jams needs a longer guide, not more play.
  • No end stops. Hinges, slides and levers without stops are overloaded at the end of travel.
  • PLA in warm or long-loaded mechanisms. It creeps and softens near 55–60 °C; use PETG, ABS/ASA or nylon.

Key takeaways

  • Pick the mechanism from the motion and the closing behavior you need, then size it with the shared rules: fit classes, print-in-place gaps and strain limits.
  • Orientation decides more than dimensions: hinge axes parallel to the plate, anything that flexes bends within the layers.
  • Let metal do what plastic does badly: pins, shafts, threads and long-loaded springs.
  • Sliders jam from geometry (d < L / 2μ), not from tight fits.
  • Test the joint alone, in variants, and cycle it before printing the whole part.
