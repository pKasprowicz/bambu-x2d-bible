Typical, approximate values

Every number in this tool is a typical, approximate value for a printed part, rounded to a 0–10 scale or a round temperature. Brands differ widely, printed parts are weaker than datasheets, and the scores are a guide for shortlisting, not an engineering certificate. Always check the manufacturer's datasheet for the exact product.

    How the scoring works

    Understanding the method protects you from over-trusting a single number. The scoring has three stages and each is deliberately simple so you can check it by hand.

    Reading the properties

    Each slider is a requirement level. Here is what the scale means so that you can set it with confidence.

    Strength (0–10)

    Resistance to breaking or yielding under load, in the printed orientation. 3 means hobby trinkets, 6 a bracket that carries a few kilograms, 9 a part that replaces aluminum. Layer adhesion limits real values, so a carbon-fiber filament scores high in-plane but is not magic across layers.

    Stiffness (0–10)

    How little the part bends. High stiffness helps rails, frames and drones; low stiffness helps snap fits and shock absorption. Fillers such as carbon fiber raise stiffness far more than strength. Strong is not the same as stiff.

    Heat resistance (°C)

    The temperature at which a loaded part begins to soften (a heat-deflection style figure, not the melting point). A car dashboard in the sun reaches 70–85 °C, a motor mount 100 °C. Heat resistance is treated as a hard target: materials below it are punished steeply.

    Flexibility (0–10)

    Elastic stretch and bend. 0 is glass-like, 5 is a living hinge in nylon or PP, 10 is a rubber-like TPU 85A. A high flexibility need naturally conflicts with a high stiffness need; the picker lets you ask for both and shows you the damage.

    Outdoor / UV (0–10)

    Resistance to sunlight, weather and moisture over months. ASA is the benchmark; PLA gets brittle and warps in a hot car; PETG tolerates rain and light better than PLA but still yellows.

    Chemical resistance (0–10)

    Resistance to oils, fuels, cleaners and solvents. Polyolefins such as PP and polyamides score high; PLA and ABS fail against many solvents (acetone dissolves ABS, which is why it is used for smoothing).

    Food-adjacent (0–10)

    Suitability for items near food, such as a cookie cutter. This is a very cautious score: no filament is food safe by default, because layer lines trap bacteria, nozzles can contain lead in older brass, and dyes or additives are rarely declared. Use food-contact claims from the manufacturer only, for dry or short contact.

    Surface finish and ease (0–10)

    Finish is how good the part looks straight from the printer (gloss, layer-line visibility, sandability). Ease is how forgiving the material is on warping, stringing, adhesion and settings. They pull in different directions: silk PLA looks superb but demands care; the easiest plastics rarely win beauty contests.

    Equipment warnings explained

    • Dryer. Hygroscopic plastics (nylons, PC, PVA, TPU, PETG-CF) absorb water within hours. Wet filament makes bubbles, strings and brittle parts. Materials marked essential are not worth printing without a dryer. See Drying and storage for temperatures and times.
    • Hardened nozzle. Carbon-fiber and glass-fiber fills abrade brass in a few hundred grams. The X2D ships with a hardened-steel 0.4 mm hotend, so the picker treats the option as available when you choose the X2D; read the materials overview for the general rule.
    • Enclosure or heated chamber. ABS, ASA, PC and nylons shrink as they cool; a closed, warm space prevents warping and layer splits. The X2D chamber heats to 65 °C, which helps the entire engineering family. See Engineering plastics.
    • Dryer temperature limit. The AMS 2 Pro dries up to 65 °C. Nylons, PC and fiber-filled polyamides want 80 °C or more, so they appear with a yellow "needs a hotter dryer" chip.

    Methodology and limits

    1. Sources of the numbers. Values summarize widely published datasheet ranges and community experience, rounded to a 0–10 scale. They describe a competently printed part in its strongest orientation. Where sources disagree, the cautious value is used.
    2. Printed versus molded. Printed parts lose 20–60 % of datasheet strength across layers. The scale therefore ranks materials against each other more reliably than it predicts absolute loads. See Strength and orientation for the design side.
    3. Heat resistance. Values are practical service temperatures near the heat-deflection point under light load, not melting points. A part that holds a spring load for months should stay at least 10–15 °C below the number.
    4. Penalties are conservative. A penalty is a warning with a price, not a ban. If you have a clever workaround (a cardboard tent over an open-frame printer for ABS), you can ignore the corresponding chip, but you should know that you are doing so.
    5. What is not modeled. Fatigue, creep under constant load, wear and friction, radiation, electrical properties, certification (flame retardancy, food contact) and print settings are outside the model. Chemical resistance is a single score per material and cannot replace a compatibility chart for the exact chemical.
    6. Brands vary. A PLA+ from one maker can behave like PETG from another. Treat the picker as the start of a conversation with a datasheet.
    A good workflow

    Shortlist with the picker, read the matching material guide, print a small test (one calibration tower and one stress coupon), and only then commit to a long job. Materials overview, Everyday plastics, Engineering plastics and Flexibles and specialty carry the detail. For numbers while designing, jump to the calculators; for intuition about how heat and layers behave, open the visual lab.

    Key takeaways

    • Choose by requirements first, brand second. Most parts need only PLA or PETG.
    • Strength, stiffness and heat resistance rarely come cheap: each step up costs ease, equipment or money.
    • Warnings (dryer, hardened nozzle, chamber) matter more than a few score points: a perfect material that you cannot print is a bad choice.
    • On the X2D the main nozzle is the most capable; check TPU, PVA and fiber-filled filaments for the AMS path.
    • All numbers here are typical and approximate; trust the manufacturer's datasheet and your own test prints.
