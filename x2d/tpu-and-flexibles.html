TPU & flexible filaments on the X2D

Flexible filament is the one material family where the X2D's dual-nozzle design has a hard rule: TPU runs on the left (main) nozzle only. This page shows the feed paths Bambu supports, how to load and set up each hardness, and how to combine soft and rigid plastics into gaskets, grips and overmolded parts.

1. The rules of TPU on the X2D

TPU (thermoplastic polyurethane) is rubbery; it compresses and buckles when pushed, which is why feed path and speed matter more than any slicer trick. Bambu's X2D guidance boils down to five rules:

  1. Left (main) nozzle only.The auxiliary (right) extruder is a stepper on the rear panel with a 525 mm PTFE feed path; its feed resistance is too high for TPU, so Bambu does not support it. In the compatibility chart every TPU is marked not available on the right nozzle, and "TPU for AMS" is marked not recommended.
  2. Match the hotend to the hardness.See the table below. A 0.2 mm hotend is not recommended for any TPU.
  3. Keep the volumetric speed limit at the default.Do not raise speed to "make it fast".
  4. No chamber heating.Use the Strong Cooling air mode (the same as PLA).
  5. Place the model at the middle-front of the plate to reduce friction in the PTFE tube.

TPU and the Filament Track Switch

The track switch (sold separately) is not suited to TPU. Bambu states that TPU, PA-CF and similar are not supported through it, and "TPU for AMS" is marked as highly not recommended with it fitted. If you own a switch, disable it for a TPU job or use the direct external path.

2. Feed paths in detail

A soft filament needs a short, straight, low-friction path to the extruder gears. The X2D guide describes the following options.

TPU 95A HF · external spool

Recommended for TPU 95A HF. The spool sits on the external holder and loads through the left extruder inlet via a short (about 175 mm) PTFE tube. Move the spool base to the right side and do not install the holder reversed.

TPU 90A / 85A · top mount

Use a top-mounted spool holder (the same size as the P2S/P1S top holder, available as a MakerWorld model) or the TPU Feed Assist Module. Softer TPU needs less friction than the standard path allows.

TPU Feed Assist Module

Works inside an enclosed chamber; uses short (about 160 mm) PTFE pieces and a 4-pin to 6-pin adapter. It can be paired with the AMS HT TPU bypass port, in which case the AMS HT acts only as a dry box for the spool.

TPU for AMS

This specific filament type can feed from the AMS to the main nozzle. Do not use standard AMS routing for softer TPU 85A/90A; Bambu advises avoiding TPU, TPE and PVA in a standard AMS because of the bending path.

Loading TPU from the top holder (documented sequence)

  1. Open the toolhead coverand disconnect the main PTFE tube from the extruder.
  2. Heat the main hotend to 250 °Cand insert the filament into the extruder inlet.
  3. Tap load slowly.Do not tap repeatedly: soft TPU wraps around the gears if it is pushed too fast.
  4. Unload the same way at 250 °C; do not pull cold.
Before you start a TPU job

If the hotend has printed other materials, run Nozzle Cold Pull Maintenance from the Toolbox (cold pull with PLA or PETG 2 to 3 times; never with a fiber-reinforced filament), and check the extruder gears and filament cutter for wear. Dirt and residue are the most common reason soft TPU grinds.

Why TPU speed is limited by flow, not motion

TPU is compressible: when the extruder pushes, some of the push compresses the filament and the PTFE guide tube instead of reaching the nozzle. The faster you go, the more of it is lost, until the filament buckles or the gear slips. A volumetric speed limit caps the plastic volume per second to a level the path can deliver. Raising it is the single most common cause of failed TPU prints; Bambu's own advice is to leave it at its default.

Why slower is also better visually

Flexible filaments hold pressure in the nozzle like a squeezed sponge. Slow, constant flow keeps pressure stable, which is also why Bambu advises Auto or Off for flow-dynamics (pressure compensation) on TPU rather than aggressive tuning.

4. Drying and storage

TPU absorbs moisture; wet TPU pops, strings and prints with a rough, bubbly surface. Bambu's documented guidance:

  • Dry: oven at 70 °C for 8 hours, or heatbed at 75–85 °C for 8 hours.
  • The spool swells while drying (the guide gives an example growing from 65.4 to about 69.4 mm), so check that it still fits a holder.
  • Store: sealed with desiccant, below 20 % relative humidity.
  • AMS drying: do not use static drying mode with low-softening materials such as TPU; and follow the manual's temperature rules and keep TPU away from high-temperature spools when drying.

6. TPU with rigid plastics: interfaces and multi-material tricks

The X2D's two nozzles make soft-plus-rigid prints practical, but the TPU-left-only rule shapes every plan. A few patterns work well.

🔧 TPU as a support interface (and rigid as TPU's support)

A support interface is the thin layer between support and model. A flexible interface can peel off a rigid part cleanly because it stretches rather than snaps; a rigid support under a TPU part gives a stable, flat base that TPU cannot provide by itself. In both cases the support and the model are different materials that do not bond strongly (typical expectation; test your pair). The slicer needs the interface and body materials assigned to different filaments, and the nozzle assignment must respect the TPU rule.

Adhesion between TPU and other plastics is material-dependent

TPU bonds strongly to some plastics and barely to others; Bambu's TPU guide does not publish a bond chart. Run a small peel test with your exact pair before designing around either outcome (see exercise X-6 and X-9).

Multi-material tricks that usually pay off

(1) Print the rigid part of the assembly first, so the TPU prints on top of a stable shell (typical). (2) Add a 0.2–0.4 mm design gap or interlocking lip between TPU and rigid regions instead of relying on chemical adhesion (typical). (3) Keep TPU layers away from long travel moves; combine TPU regions into one tool change per layer. (4) Use a tall, wide prime tower when switching to or from TPU, because TPU leaves pressure in the nozzle. (5) Avoid rapid alternations: each tool change adds time and risk.

7. Gaskets, seals and overmolding-style printing

Gaskets

A printed gasket works by being compressed between two faces. Design for a modest compression (typical 10–30 % of the gasket thickness, depending on hardness) and for a groove that holds the gasket in place. Softer TPU (85A) seals more easily but is harder to print; 90A or 95A is more forgiving (typical). Print gaskets flat on the plate so the layer lines run along the seal, not across it, and use 100 % infill or a high wall count for a continuous, non-porous seal.

Overmolding-style printing

Printing a soft grip, bumper or seal directly onto a rigid part imitates overmolding. It works best when the two materials are mechanically locked (undercuts, holes through which the TPU flows, dovetails) and not only chemically bonded. On the X2D, TPU on the left nozzle and the rigid core on the right nozzle in one job is the more advanced route; the safer route is to print the rigid core first, pause or re-load, then print the TPU region. Reduce travel across the TPU regions, and avoid TPU on the very first layer of a plate containing other materials if the TPU is only a small portion (typical).

8. Test models worth printing first

  • Retraction tower or stringing test: reveals if retraction should be shorter or off.
  • Flow cube or 20 mm cube with 2 walls: check wall thickness and layer bonding by pulling it apart.
  • Bridge and overhang sample: soft plastics bridge poorly at speed.
  • Compression gasket ring set (three thicknesses): measure seal quality against a mating lid.
  • Rigid + TPU peel strip: two strips of rigid plastic with a TPU layer in between, for adhesion tests.
  • Hinge/flexure test: a living-hinge pad, bent 100 times, to check layer strength.

Choosing a hardness

Shore hardness (the "A" number) measures how deep a standard probe sinks into the material: lower means softer. The X2D guide covers three grades plus a special AMS-friendly type, and the choice is a trade between feel and printability:

95A (HF)

Firmest. Easiest to push through a path and the only one Bambu recommends from a plain external spool. Good for belts-like parts, bumpers, protective feet and anything that must keep its shape under load.

90A

A middle ground. More cushion than 95A, still workable on 0.4 mm. Needs the top-mounted holder or the Feed Assist Module for a gentle path.

85A

Softest in the guide and hardest to feed. Needs a 0.6 or 0.8 mm hotend and the top holder or Feed Assist Module. Choose it for soft seals and comfort grips only when 90A is not soft enough.

Start firm, go softer later

Learn the geometry, retraction and plate behavior on 95A HF. Once your design works, swap to a softer grade, which usually needs a slower print, a bigger nozzle and a better feed path. A design that fails at 95A will not succeed at 85A.

Softer by design: geometry before grade

On the X2D, moving to a softer grade is not just a filament swap. TPU 85A needs a 0.6 or 0.8 mm hotend, and for a dual-nozzle job both sides must match; 90A and 85A also need the top holder or the Feed Assist Module instead of a plain external spool. Before paying that cost, make the firm, easy grade feel softer with geometry. Walls, infill and shape change how a part squashes far more than a few points of Shore hardness.

Slicer workflow for a TPU job

  1. Select the hotend sizein the printer preset that matches what is installed (0.6 or 0.8 for 85A).
  2. Pick the TPU filament presetand confirm it is assigned to the left nozzle in the filament list. If Studio proposes the right nozzle, use Custom grouping and move it.
  3. Choose the plateto match the surface (textured PEI without glue, or engineering/smooth plate with glue).
  4. Position the modelat the middle-front, then set flow dynamics to Auto or Off.
  5. Check the speedshown for outer walls; do not raise volumetric speed.
  6. Sendand watch the first layers on the camera: a clean, slightly squished first layer is the sign you are on track.

Design tips for flexible parts

  • Wall thickness in multiples of the line width: thin flexible walls print as one solid line, which is more flexible but less strong (typical).
  • Fillets everywhere: sharp inside corners tear; add a generous radius at stress points.
  • Orient flex axes along layers so bending happens across the layer bonds that are strongest in the XY plane, not between layers.
  • Use thicker, sturdier clamping areas at holes and screws: TPU creeps under a screw head, so use a washer or a rigid insert.
  • Accept some elephant's-foot and surface texture; plan post-processing such as trimming with a sharp blade rather than sanding.

Key takeaways

  • TPU lives on the left nozzle; the right nozzle is for rigid supports and companions.
  • 95A HF: external spool. 90A and 85A: top-mounted holder or Feed Assist Module; 85A needs 0.6 or 0.8 mm.
  • Nozzle 225 °C and bed 30–35 °C for 85A/90A; no chamber heating; keep the speed limit at default.
  • Dry for 8 hours; store under 20 % humidity; expect the spool to grow while drying.
  • Textured PEI without glue, or engineering/smooth plate with glue; never cool plate.
  • Lock soft and rigid parts mechanically and test the combination on a small piece first.
  • Need it softer? Try fewer walls, less infill and a compliant shape on 95A HF before moving to 90A or 85A and new hotends.
