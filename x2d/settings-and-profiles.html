Bambu Studio settings & profiles for the X2D

The X2D is two printers in one toolhead, and Bambu Studio is where you tell them what to do. This page walks through presets, nozzle assignment, flush volumes, calibrations, chamber and drying controls, per-object tricks and sharing, then ends with starting profiles for every major material.

What you will learn

How the three preset layers fit together, how filaments get grouped onto the left (main) and right (auxiliary) nozzle, what the flush-volume matrix really controls, which calibrations matter and when, where the chamber and AMS drying controls live, how to share a project, and which numbers to start from for PLA, PETG, ABS/ASA, PC, nylon, carbon-fiber blends and TPU.

1. The big picture: three layers of presets

Every slice in Bambu Studio is the product of three independent presets (named bundles of settings) plus whatever you override on the plate:

  • Printer preset: the machine itself. For the X2D this includes the build volume (256 × 256 × 260 mm), the two nozzle diameters, retraction defaults for each extruder, the start and end G-code and the printable area rules for the auxiliary nozzle.
  • Filament preset: everything that belongs to a material: nozzle temperature range, heatbed temperature per plate, flow ratio, flow-dynamics (pressure-advance style) K value, cooling curve, volumetric speed limit, chamber temperature request and the drying recommendation.
  • Process preset: how the part is built: layer height, walls, infill, supports, speeds, accelerations, seam, prime tower and so on.

🔰 System presets, user presets and the "modified" dot

Bambu Studio ships system presets that are read-only. The moment you change a value, a small marker appears next to the preset name; you can then Save as a user preset, which inherits from the system preset (inheritance means that when Bambu updates a system value you did not touch, your preset picks up the change). A few habits keep libraries healthy:

  • Name user presets by purpose, not by date: "PETG bracket 0.6 strong" beats "test 7".
  • Change one thing at a time when tuning, so you can tell what helped.
  • Use the compare function (the small compare icon next to the preset drop-down) to see exactly which values differ from the system preset before sharing a profile.
  • Cloud sync of user presets is a convenience, not a backup. Export important presets from the File menu.

🔧 What is special about X2D presets

Because there are two extruders, many values exist twice. You will see paired entries for the left (main) and right (auxiliary) extruder in the printer preset (retraction length, retraction speed, nozzle diameter) and in filament presets (flow-dynamics K, temperature). Three consequences:

  1. The auxiliary extruder has its own retraction.It pushes filament through a PTFE tube from the printer's rear panel, so it needs a much longer retraction than the direct-drive main extruder to take up slack in the tube. Keep the system value unless you have a reason to change it.
  2. The K value differs per nozzle.Bambu documents that the auxiliary extruder's flow-dynamics K is much larger than the main extruder's, because the long feed path adds elastic hysteresis, friction and pressure lag.
  3. Limits are enforced for the auxiliary side.Maximum speed 200 mm/s and acceleration about 1000 mm/s² when printing with the auxiliary nozzle. Do not raise them in a custom profile.
Both nozzles must be the same size

Dual-nozzle printing currently requires nozzles of the same diameter (Bambu's FAQ says mixed diameters may be announced in a future firmware release). The two hotends are identical in structure and can be swapped left to right. The pre-installed nozzle is hardened steel (0.4 mm). High-flow hotends and a 0.2 mm + 0.6 mm multi-size kit are optional extras; check the filament-compatibility table on the filament compatibility page before choosing a size.

3. Nozzle assignment and filament grouping

This is the single most X2D-specific concept in the slicer. Each filament used in a project is assigned to the left (main) nozzle, the right (auxiliary) nozzle, or, with a Filament Track Switch installed, to both dynamically. The assignment decides how many filament changes must happen inside one nozzle (slow, purge-heavy) versus nozzle switches (fast, little purge).

🔧 How the AMS binds to nozzles

On the X2D, an AMS is wired to the filament buffer, which has two filament channels. The upper channel leads to the left (main) hotend, the lower channel to the right (auxiliary) hotend through the auxiliary extruder. Bambu's first-print guide recommends connecting the AMS 2 Pro to the upper inlet so it feeds the left hotend, which has the widest filament compatibility. After first connection you run AMS Setup on the screen (Filament page): Auto mode feeds a filament and uses Hall sensors to learn which side the AMS is on; Manual mode lets you assign it when the AMS is empty. Once an AMS has fed filament to the extruder its icon turns gray and the pairing is locked.

Without a track switch the AMS-to-nozzle relationship is therefore fixed. With a single AMS on the main nozzle, the auxiliary nozzle can only use a spool fed through its own path. The Filament Track Switch (sold separately) breaks that binding: it has two inlets and two outlets and lets any AMS reach either nozzle. Two restrictions come with it: external spools are not supported while it is installed, and high-precision nozzle offset calibration currently cannot run with it fitted (remove it first).

The three grouping modes

  • Filament-saving: Studio searches for the grouping that minimizes flush waste and time. It may ask you to physically move spools to a different AMS or slot. Best for long, many-color jobs when you can rearrange before starting.
  • Convenience: respects the current AMS-to-nozzle wiring so nobody has to touch spools. Slightly more purge waste. Best for unattended prints.
  • Custom: you decide, drag by drag. Use this for support-material workflows where the grouping logic is obvious to you but not to the algorithm.
Dynamic mapping (with a track switch)

If a model uses different color pairs in different height ranges, static grouping can force two colors into the same nozzle in some range, so the other nozzle idles. Dynamic mapping lets a filament "borrow" the other nozzle for only the layers that need it. Studio then suggests which spools to put in which AMS. Bambu notes that actual print time approaches the slicer estimate when your placement follows the recommendation, because most color changes then happen across the two track-switch inlets, which is the fast path. It pays off for gradient or segmented-color models and long multi-color jobs; for simple two- or three-color models the normal dual-nozzle mode is enough.

4. Flush volumes and the prime tower

🔰 Why purge at all?

When a nozzle changes from one filament to another, the old color is still inside the melt zone. The printer pushes new filament through until the old one is gone; this purged plastic is called flush (or purge) and ends up in the waste chute or inside the prime tower. The amount depends on how dark and how different the colors are: going dark to light needs far more flushing than light to dark.

Studio fills the matrix automatically from the colors of the loaded filaments (it computes how different two colors look), and you can edit any cell. A multiplier scales the whole table. The X2D changes the economics in two ways:

  • Nozzle switches replace much purging. Switching from the left to the right nozzle swaps which hot melt is in front, so there is no color change inside either nozzle. Bambu cites faster color changes and less waste for multi-color and support-heavy models.
  • Flush is per nozzle. The matrix applies to changes inside the same nozzle. A good grouping puts colors that change often on different nozzles.
Do not slash flush volumes blindly

Reducing the multiplier saves plastic but can leave a ghost of the previous color. Reduce it in small steps (for example 10 percent at a time) with a test print that goes from dark to light, and keep a note of the value that still looks clean. Exercise X-3 turns this into a structured experiment.

Prime tower on a dual-nozzle printer

People often ask why the X2D needs a prime tower at all if it has two nozzles. Bambu's FAQ gives the reason: during travel or a nozzle switch a small amount of filament may remain in the nozzle, and the prime tower cleans that residue and stabilizes pressure inside the nozzle so the next surface does not show a defect. Keep it on for multi-material jobs, and consider these tweaks:

  • Place the tower where the toolhead will not have to travel over tall parts. Closer to the objects means shorter, faster visits.
  • Use fewer, thicker tower layers for a faster tower, but keep it wide enough to be stable (a wobbling tower is a common cause of a failed multi-color print).
  • For the same filament across many objects, print one plate at a time rather than piling distinct colors onto one plate.
  • Supports printed on the other nozzle in a different material often need no flushing against the model at all: the transition happens through a nozzle switch.

5. Calibrations: what runs, when, and why

The X2D automates a lot, but "automatic" does not mean "never think about it". These are the calibrations in the printer's Calibration menu and the send dialog, and what each one protects you from.

Auto bed leveling and high-temperature leveling

Auto bed leveling (ABL) probes the plate and builds a height map that the firmware uses to compensate small tilt or bow. You can switch it on or off per print in the send dialog and in the on-screen advanced options. For ABS, ASA, PC and PA Bambu recommends running high-temperature bed leveling first, because the heatbed and the frame expand when hot and a cold map is then slightly wrong. Run it again after moving the printer, after long storage, or after any work on the heatbed.

If the first layer is still too thin in one area, the support documentation's escalation path is: run first-layer calibration, then heat the bed to about 55 °C for 5 minutes and re-check, check the four bed screws, and only then consider manual leveling through G-code with an A4 sheet of paper. Patterns that appear in a fixed spot usually point to oil on the plate rather than a leveling problem.

Vibration compensation and motor noise cancellation

Vibration compensation measures the resonance of the toolhead and plans input shaping against it so fast moves do not leave ringing (ghost ripples behind corners). Motor noise cancellation tunes the stepper drive to reduce motor sound. Both are quick and belong in the "full calibration" set that you run after lubricating the rods or screws. Because the speed modes scale both print speed and acceleration (Silent 50 %, Standard 100 %, Sport 124 %, Ludicrous 166 %), a clean vibration calibration is what keeps Sport and Ludicrous presentable.

Flow dynamics calibration (auto and manual)

Flow dynamics calibration finds the K value that tells the firmware how to anticipate pressure in the nozzle: it sharpens corners and avoids bulges at starts and ends of lines. In the filament preset you will see the choice Auto or Off:

  • Auto: the printer runs the calibration for the main extruder before the print and caches the result per filament. This is the right default for the left nozzle.
  • Off: uses the stored K from the preset. Bambu advises this for TPU, along with leaving the volumetric speed alone.
  • Auxiliary extruder: not automatic; calibrate manually from Studio's calibration page. Plan to do this once per filament type you intend to run on the right nozzle, and save the result in a user filament preset.
Why the aux K is so different

The auxiliary extruder sits on the rear panel and pushes filament through a 525 mm PTFE tube. The tube stretches, rubs and stores pressure, so a successful correction needs a much larger K and a much longer retraction. This is exactly why Bambu markets the right nozzle for supports and lower-priority surfaces.

6. Chamber temperature, airflow and AMS drying

Three controls influence the heat around your part: the heatbed, the chamber setpoint (active heating up to 65 °C using a PTC heater and a circulation fan) and the air-condition mode (Strong Cooling or Heating). In the filament preset there is a field for chamber temperature; on the printer's screen the Controls tab exposes the same setting live.

How the chamber behaves

  • The heater runs at full power with the circulation fan at maximum until the setpoint is reached; then the fan drops to about 40 % and heater power is reduced to hold temperature.
  • After homing, the toolhead raises to a Z position of about 160 mm (below the vent) so the heated air is not pushed straight out.
  • Heatbed pre-heat strategy. If the bed temperature is 90 °C or below, the printer pre-heats about 5 minutes; if it is 90 °C or above, about 30 minutes, because active chamber control is not yet triggered at the first layer and the bed slowly lifts the chamber above 50 °C. Do not skip that soak for ABS/ASA/PC/PA.
  • In Heating mode the right-hand fan becomes a filtration fan that recirculates air through the filter; in Strong Cooling mode it draws fresh outside air and the exhaust fan runs, which suits PLA and TPU.

The full material strategy lives on the Engineering and chamber page. In Studio the typical rule is: put the chamber temperature in the filament preset, not in the process, so the value follows the material.

AMS drying controls

The AMS 2 Pro has two heating modules (maximum 65 °C, reached only when the ambient temperature is about 25 °C or higher), active vents, a sealed lid and a humidity/temperature sensor between slots 2 and 3. Drying can start from the printer, from Studio's AMS panel, or remotely from Handy. Choose between two modes:

RotatingFilament is pulled back onto the spool and the spool turns about 30° every 5 minutes for even drying.Most spools; the safer, more uniform mode.

Drying rules from the documentation

Do not dry high-temperature and low-temperature filaments in the same AMS at the same time (the cooler spool deforms). Drying needs the 6-pin connection to the printer; there is no offline mode. Print-while-drying exists since firmware 01.01.00.00, but drying power is reduced while the printer is printing and feeding unless the AMS has an additional power supply (an official 24 V / 4 A adapter is recommended). Replace the desiccant when it turns from yellow to purple, or after about 4 to 6 weeks of continuous use.

Filament sync and AMS options

Click the sync button next to the filament list to import the AMS slot contents. Studio reads the RFID tag of Bambu spools (type, color, remaining estimate); third-party spools appear as generic entries that you set by hand. On the printer, AMS options cover Read on insertion, Read on startup, Auto-refill (switches to another spool when one runs out; works only with identical brand, type and color on the same side, left-left or right-right) and Arrange AMS order. If you re-assign a slot, use Re-read so the remaining estimate resets.

Third-party spools

For non-RFID filament set material, color and a user filament preset by hand, then save it. A named preset per brand and material (for example "Brand-X PETG 0.4") is what makes a reproducible workflow possible. Third-party carbon- or glass-fiber filament can wear PTFE tubes faster than the tuned official ones; see the maintenance page.

8. Timelapse, AI detection and monitoring

The X2D has a 1080p live-view camera and a 1600 × 1200 toolhead camera. Timelapse can be recorded in two ways: stored to a USB drive, or, since firmware 01.01.00.00, stored locally inside the printer. Without a USB drive there is no video recording and no log export on older workflows, so keep a drive formatted FAT32 or exFAT (USB-A 2.0; 32 to 64 GB recommended) plugged in if you rely on them.

  • Traditional timelapse pauses the toolhead at the same position each layer; it gives a smooth film but adds time. Smooth style recording typically moves less; treat "adds time" as an expectation to check on your slice preview.
  • Print options on the screen include spaghetti detection, purge-chute pile-up detection and nozzle-clumping detection, each with sensitivity High / Medium / Low (Low is meant for overnight runs where false stops are costly). Spaghetti detection needs the chamber light on, and dark filaments reduce reliability.
  • Other checks: plate type and alignment detection, foreign-object detection, printed-part displacement detection, auto-recovery from step loss, motion accuracy enhancement and "purify air at print end" (needs the external exhaust fan).

9. Per-object settings, modifiers and supports

Right-click an object and choose Add settings to override process values for just that object, or add a modifier (a box, cylinder or sphere that carries its own settings for the region it covers). Combined with the second nozzle this unlocks workflows that no single-nozzle printer offers.

Per-object tricks worth knowing

(1) Give functional parts more walls than decorative ones on the same plate. (2) Assign the support and support-interface filament to the right nozzle; because that is a nozzle switch rather than a filament swap, little purging is needed. (3) For a mixed TPU and rigid assembly, assign TPU to the left nozzle only, at TPU speeds. (4) Use "avoid crossing walls" for objects that sit next to each other so the nozzle does not drag a blob across a finished surface. See the recipes for complete examples.

10. Quality and speed profiles

System process presets are named for layer height and goal (for example "0.20 mm Standard", "0.12 mm Fine", "0.28 mm Extra Draft"). Treat the names as starting points. Four independent levers control speed and quality:

  1. Layer height.Thinner layers look better and print slower. Taller layers on a larger nozzle (a 0.6 mm or 0.8 mm hotend) are the biggest time saver for functional parts.
  2. Print speeds and accelerations in the process.Outer wall slower than inner wall and infill gives better surfaces.
  3. Volumetric speed limit in the filament preset.This caps mm³/s and therefore the real ceiling for each material. Bambu advises against raising it for TPU. Standard hotends have a lower ceiling than the optional high-flow hotends, which Bambu claims can cut print time by up to 30 %.
  4. Printer speed mode.Silent 50 %, Standard 100 %, Sport 124 %, Ludicrous 166 % of both speed and acceleration. Higher modes are only as good as the filament's volumetric limit and the cooling.

11. Sharing projects: .3mf and .gcode.3mf

Bambu Studio saves two flavors of project file, and knowing the difference prevents many "it printed differently on your machine" mysteries:

  • .3mf (project): models, plate layout, all presets and per-object settings. Anyone with Bambu Studio can open it, change anything and re-slice. Share this for collaboration or MakerWorld uploads.
  • .gcode.3mf (sliced plate): the same container plus the toolpaths for a particular printer, nozzle assignment and filament set. It can be printed directly from internal storage, USB or the cloud, with no slicing step. The X2D's built-in and USB models are pre-sliced for the main hotend: choosing a hotend or per-nozzle filaments is only possible by slicing in Bambu Studio.
Checklist before sharing a sliced file

Name the filament types and nozzle size in the file name (bracket_PETG_0.4_X2D.gcode.3mf), say which AMS slots the recipient must fill, and mention the plate type. A sliced file for a 0.4 mm nozzle will not be accepted on a printer with a different nozzle size and the plate-type check will pause a mismatch, so include those facts as plain text.

12. Firmware, Studio and Handy updates

Bambu ships major X2D firmware roughly every three months with smaller fixes in between. The first public releases already show how much the workflow evolves:

  • 01.01.00.00 (April 2026): Filament Track Switch support, print-while-drying, remote drying, high-flow hotend support, local timelapse, AI popups with snapshots, low-power heatbed, under-bed foreign-object check, first-layer quality calibration, print-loosening detection, PIN binding and heating-base maintenance mode. Requires Studio 2.5.3.60+ and Handy 3.18.0+.
  • 01.01.01.00 (May 2026): Z-motor noise reduction, abnormal toolhead-change detection, auxiliary loading fixes, AMS 2 Pro drying power-loss fix.
  • 01.02.00.00 (July 2026): motors may be powered off while drying in the chamber, better spaghetti detection, live-view camera calibration no longer required.
Update etiquette

Update between jobs, never during one; keep Studio, Handy and printer firmware in step (a feature may need all three); and read the release notes for changes to calibrations before an important print. After an update that touches motion or leveling, run the full calibration once.

Key takeaways

  • Three presets plus per-object overrides define every slice; save changes as named user presets.
  • The X2D has two nozzles with different limits: the right one is capped at 200 mm/s and about 1000 mm/s² and its flow dynamics are calibrated manually.
  • Check the filament-to-nozzle assignment every time; choose Filament-saving, Convenience or Custom grouping deliberately.
  • Nozzle switches replace many color changes; flush volumes still matter inside one nozzle, and the prime tower still cleans and stabilizes pressure.
  • Run high-temperature bed leveling and a proper heat soak for ABS/ASA/PC/PA; put chamber setpoints in the filament preset.
  • Dry spools in the right mode and never mix high- and low-temperature filaments in one AMS during drying.
  • Share .3mf for collaboration, .gcode.3mf for ready-to-print results, and state nozzle size, plate and AMS slots.
