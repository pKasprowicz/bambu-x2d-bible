Dual-Nozzle Printing in Practice

Two hotends, one toolhead, and a slicer that decides who prints what. This page explains how filaments are assigned to the left and right nozzle, what happens during a switch, where each nozzle can reach, why the right nozzle is slower, and how to build smart multi-material jobs.

Bambu X2DIntermediate⏱ 22 min read
What you need to know first

The numbers on this page (200 mm/s and about 1000 mm/s² for the right nozzle, the 235.5 × 256 × 256 mm right-only area, the 256 × 256 × 260 mm main area) come from Bambu’s documentation. Anything about how much time or material you save depends on the model, so this page explains the mechanism and avoids promising a figure. Check the Bambu wiki for current limits after firmware updates.

🔰 The idea in one minute

A normal single-nozzle printer that prints several colors must purge: after every filament change it pushes plastic through the nozzle until the old color is gone. That wasted plastic and time grows with the number of changes. The X2D has two hotends. The left one is the main nozzle (with the direct-drive servo extruder) and the right one is the auxiliary nozzle (fed by a separate extruder at the back of the printer). Only one nozzle is lowered to the plate at a time; a lever-driven flow blocker (the gear-and-trigger mechanism described in the hardware tour) moves the idle one out of the way. If a model needs two colors, each color can live in its own nozzle, so the printer swaps nozzles instead of flushing plastic.

The prime tower does not go away

Bambu notes that two-color printing still needs a prime tower. The tower is used to stabilize the nozzle after a swap (a little ooze and pressure change when the idle nozzle comes into play). What changes is that far less flushing is required between colors. Bambu also states that, compared with earlier machines that top out at 16 colors, the X2D can handle up to 25 colors in one job when all AMS units sit on one nozzle and one external spool sits on the other.

🔰 Who is who: left, right, main, aux

⬅️

Left = main nozzle

Direct-drive servo extruder in the toolhead. Auto flow-dynamics calibration. Full build area (256 × 256 × 260 mm). Handles the widest range of filaments, including TPU. This is the default for single-material prints.

➡️

Right = auxiliary nozzle

Fed by a stepper extruder on the rear panel through a PTFE tube. Slower limits (200 mm/s, about 1000 mm/s²), longer retraction, manual flow-dynamics calibration. Reaches 235.5 × 256 × 256 mm. No TPU.

The two hotends are physically identical (300 °C maximum, same quick-release design) and can be swapped; what differs is the extruder that feeds them and the path the filament takes. The difference matters because it decides the speed and the filament list for each side.

🔰 Print-area geometry: who can reach where

Because the two nozzles sit side by side, each one cannot reach the entire plate edge. Bambu documents three regions, which every dual-nozzle project must respect:

  • Left (main) only: 256 × 256 × 260 mm — the whole plate, and the full height.
  • Right (aux) only: 235.5 × 256 × 256 mm — 20.5 mm less across X and 4 mm less in height (the flow blocker moves under the main hotend and the auxiliary hotend lowers).
  • Common area for both: 235.5 × 256 × 256 mm — anything the two nozzles must print together has to fit here.
Why a model can be “out of bounds”

A tall two-color part that fits the 260 mm main limit may still exceed the 256 mm limit of the common area. If Studio flags the model, either lower it, rotate it, reduce its footprint or print it single-material on the left nozzle.

🔧 How filaments get assigned to nozzles

In Bambu Studio each plate filament is linked to a physical slot (an AMS slot or the external spool). The slot lives on one nozzle’s feed path: AMS units connected to the buffer’s upper inlet feed the left nozzle and units connected to the lower inlet feed the right nozzle. Assignment therefore has two layers: the hardware layer (which AMS feeds which nozzle, set in AMS Setup) and the slicer layer (which object, color or support uses which filament).

With the optional Filament Track Switch (sold separately) Studio can map filaments dynamically if you enable Fila Saving and Enable Smart Filament Assign. The track switch needs firmware 01.01.00.00 or later and Studio 2.5.3 or later (Bambu Handy 3.18.0 or later for the app side). It imposes tube-length limits (AMS to switch at most 700 mm, switch to buffer at most 115 mm) and does not allow an external spool.

Manual beats automatic when…

…you need a specific material on a specific side. Typical case: a flexible filament must be on the left (main) nozzle, a support interface material may be placed on the right nozzle, and a filament that Bambu marks “not recommended” on the aux nozzle must stay left. In such cases set the grouping to Custom and verify the result in the preview.

🔧 The nozzle switching sequence

A swap is mechanical and uses no extra motor. A lever on the underside of the toolhead meets a push block on the printer’s lining as the toolhead moves; the gear and connecting rod then swing the flow blocker so that one hotend is lowered and the other raised. Bambu rates the mechanism for more than one million cycles in its brief. The printer also has two filament cutters (left and right), a purge wiper and a nozzle wiper that are used around swaps and filament changes.

You can also trigger the switch manually from the screen (Nozzle & Extruder page), which is useful for loading filament to the second nozzle or for maintenance. After you reinstall or swap a hotend, run nozzle-offset calibration again.

🔧 Right-nozzle limits you must design around

The auxiliary extruder is a stepper-driven unit located at the back of the printer, and its filament travels through a long PTFE tube. Bambu documents these consequences:

  • Speed and acceleration: limited to about 200 mm/s and about 1000 mm/s², versus up to 1000 mm/s and 20,000 mm/s² for the printer’s motion system overall.
  • Retraction: much longer than on the main nozzle, with a larger pressure-advance (K) value.
  • Detail: weaker fine detail and sharper corners; Bambu suggests using it for supports and lower-demand features.
  • Flow dynamics: automatic for the main nozzle, manual for the auxiliary one in Studio.
  • Filaments: no TPU. Some materials are listed as not recommended; see the compatibility page.

🔧 Flow dynamics and pressure advance per nozzle

Flow dynamics (the X2D’s name for pressure-advance style calibration) compensates for the elasticity of molten plastic in the nozzle so corners and line ends stay crisp. On the main nozzle the printer calibrates it automatically. On the auxiliary nozzle you run the calibration manually from Bambu Studio, per filament, because the long PTFE path and different extruder behave differently. Bambu says the auxiliary nozzle has a larger K value and much longer retraction. Practical consequences:

  • If a filament looks fine on the left but blobby or stringy on the right, calibrate it for the right nozzle before changing anything else.
  • Keep separate filament presets for “left” and “right” use of the same spool so that the calibrated values do not get mixed up.
  • When you swap the two hotends physically (for example to diagnose a clog), the calibrations belong to the extruder path, not to the hotend body. Recalibrate nozzle offset after any reinstall.

🔧 Multi-material strategies that use the second nozzle well

1 · Rigid body + flexible parts (TPU on the left)

TPU is only supported on the main (left) nozzle and, in Bambu’s guidance, is best fed from the external spool or the TPU Feed Assist Module rather than the AMS. The rigid material (PLA, PETG, ABS and similar) can then occupy the right nozzle for the structural part. Keep the model inside the common area and place it mid-front on the plate, as Bambu suggests for TPU. Do not heat the chamber for TPU. See TPU & flexibles on the X2D.

2 · Support interface in a different material

A dissolvable or easy-release support material on the right nozzle, with the structural filament on the left, gives clean surfaces under overhangs. Interface layers are slow and low-flow, so the aux nozzle’s speed ceiling matters less. Check the compatibility matrix: the support filaments that Bambu lists as available for the aux nozzle include supports for ABS, PLA/PETG, PLA and PVA, while the support for PA/PET is highly not recommended there.

3 · Model + support split between nozzles

The classic use: the model in a “good” filament on the main nozzle and the bulk of support in a cheap or special material on the aux nozzle. The long supports do not need fine detail, which suits the slower nozzle. In the slicer, give supports their own filament (support base and support interface may be different).

4 · Two colors with minimal flush

Put color A on the left and color B on the right. Neither nozzle needs to flush for the other. The prime tower remains but can be small. Prefer designs where the colors do not alternate every layer; a swap still costs time.

5 · Many colors: groups of two

For more than two colors, groups of colors are assigned to a nozzle, and the swaps inside a group still involve normal filament changes through the AMS (with flushing). The optimizer tries to keep colors that alternate often on different nozzles. “Filament-Saving” grouping gives the least purge.

Spread the abrasive and the delicate

If one filament is abrasive (for example a carbon-fiber blend), prefer to place it on the nozzle you swap most easily and keep a spare hardened-steel hotend. Because the hotends are identical and quick-release, you can later move the worn one to the other side or replace it. See the hardware tour for the swap procedure.

🚀 Ideas for using two nozzles smartly

  • Hardware tolerance split. Keep a 0.4 mm nozzle on both sides for general use. Dual-nozzle jobs need the same diameter, so plan a swap of both when you move to 0.6 mm.
  • “Sacrificial” right nozzle. Run abrasive support or test materials on the nozzle whose wear you accept; you have a spare hotend anyway.
  • Color-per-nozzle for production. Printing a series of two-color parts means the same two filaments stay loaded for the whole run: nothing to change between plates.
  • Multi-plate jobs. The AMS 2 Pro’s drying can keep reserve spools dry while a job runs (print-while-drying; extra AMS units need the switching adapter).
  • Up to 25 colors. Place all AMS units on one nozzle and an external spool on the other (Bambu’s description of the maximum).
“Parallel” printing?

The X2D has one toolhead and one nozzle lowered at a time, so the two nozzles do not print simultaneously. Any idea of printing two copies at once does not apply to this machine; the gain comes from fewer purges, not from twice the deposition.

🚀 Failure modes and tips

Don’t mix up “aux” and “weak”

The right nozzle is a full hotend that can print most materials. It is the extruder path that gives it lower speed limits. Use it for what it does well rather than avoiding it. And remember: some filaments are marked highly not recommended there (for example PETG-CF, ASA-CF and PA6-CF); do not override that unless you accept the risk.

Tips

  • Start with a small two-color test plate before a long job; check the boundary between colors.
  • Use the preview’s color/tool view to confirm each feature’s nozzle before printing.
  • Write on each spool which nozzle side it lives on.
  • Run nozzle-offset calibration after any hotend removal.
  • Read the HMS page when the screen warns of a swap or filament issue.
  • See also multi-color and multi-material for general technique.

🚀 Worked examples

The following three examples show how the rules combine. They are illustrative; the filament names are generic, and the final word always belongs to the wiki entry for the exact product.

  1. A two-color logo in PLA. Both colors are basic-type PLA, so either nozzle is allowed. Put color A on the left and B on the right, enable the prime tower, keep the part inside the common area (235.5 × 256 mm) and choose Filament-Saving grouping. The gain is that neither nozzle flushes for the other.
  2. A functional bracket with soluble-style support. The structural filament goes left (full area, precise motion). The support goes right, at modest speed. Check that the support type is rated for the model material; verify in the preview that interface layers use the support filament.
  3. A flexible seal on a rigid housing. TPU is limited to the left nozzle and an external or feed-assisted path, so the housing goes right in a permitted rigid material. Print the model mid-front, keep the chamber cool and expect a slower job; the speed limit on the right nozzle is not the bottleneck here, because the TPU on the left is slower still.

✅ Key takeaways

  • Two hotends mean color changes can become nozzle swaps instead of flushes; savings depend on the model.
  • Left = main (full plate, auto flow dynamics, TPU allowed); right = aux (200 mm/s, about 1000 mm/s², manual flow dynamics, no TPU).
  • Both-nozzle work must fit the 235.5 × 256 × 256 mm common area; main-only work can use 256 × 256 × 260 mm.
  • The prime tower stays; the same nozzle diameter is required on both sides.
  • Use Filament-Saving, Convenience or Custom grouping; use Custom when a material must be on a specific side.
  • Fine detail on the left, supports and bulk on the right, and always verify in the preview.
  • Check the compatibility page and the Bambu wiki before assigning an unusual filament to the aux nozzle.
