Engineering materials & the heated chamber

The X2D can actively heat its chamber to 65 °C. That one number changes what is realistic at home: large ABS and ASA parts without cracks, polycarbonate without layer splits, and nylon or carbon-fiber blends that behave. This page explains the physics, then turns it into material-by-material strategy.

What you will learn

1. Why a hot chamber matters: shrink, stress and warp

🔰 The simple version

Hot plastic is bigger than cold plastic. As each new layer is laid down at nozzle temperature and cools toward room temperature, it tries to shrink. The layer underneath is already cooler and has already shrunk, so the two fight. The result is internal stress that pulls the part up from the bed (warping) or splits it between layers (delamination). Amorphous plastics such as ABS, ASA and PC mostly shrink by contracting as they pass through their glass-transition temperature; semi-crystalline plastics such as nylon (PA) shrink more, because the crystals pack tighter as they form.

🔧 The physics, a little deeper

Think of each layer as a stretched rubber band glued down while hot. How hard it pulls depends on three things: the shrinkage coefficient of the material, the temperature drop from the moment it solidifies to the moment the whole part is at ambient, and the stiffness of the part. A warm chamber attacks the second factor. If the air around the part is already, say, 50 °C instead of 22 °C, the layer that solidifies at roughly the glass-transition region has far less to cool, and the temperature difference between neighboring layers stays small throughout the print. Part adhesion matters too: a layer that stays warm bonds better to the one below, so layer lines are stronger.

Why PLA and TPU do not want a hot chamber

PLA softens at relatively low temperatures; at elevated chamber temperatures the part above the heated bed can become soft, jam in the heatbreak zone or sag. TPU is soft by nature and needs controlled, steady feeding. Bambu's X2D therefore uses the cooling air mode (heater fan off, outside air drawn in, exhaust fan running) for PLA and TPU, and the heating mode for ABS, ASA, PC and PA. Choose the mode that suits the filament rather than "hotter is better".

2. How the X2D heats and ventilates the chamber

The X2D has an active chamber heater: a PTC heating element with a circulation fan on the left side. Together with the heatbed (up to 120 °C) it raises the chamber to a setpoint of up to 65 °C. Behavior documented by Bambu:

  • Until the setpoint is reached, the heater runs at full power and the circulation fan at maximum speed. At the setpoint the fan slows to about 40 % and heater power is reduced.
  • After homing, the toolhead is parked at about 160 mm Z (below the chamber vent), so the vent does not leak the hot air out during preheating.
  • In the Heating air mode the right-hand fan acts as a filtration fan and recirculates the air through the filter stack; in Strong Cooling the right fan pulls cool air in from outside and the exhaust fan removes warm air.

Pre-heat and heat soak

The chamber is not hot the instant you press Print. Bambu documents a heatbed-assisted pre-heat: if the bed setpoint is 90 °C or below, the printer pre-heats about 5 minutes; if it is above 90 °C (ABS, ASA, PC and many PA grades), pre-heat lasts about 30 minutes because active chamber control is not yet triggered for the first layer and the chamber is slowly lifted above 50 °C by the bed. Treat the pre-heat as part of the print:

  1. Close everything.Door shut, lid on (see section 5). Open air paths lose heat faster than the heater can add it.
  2. Start high-temperature bed leveling first for ABS, ASA, PC, PA. Bambu recommends it for these materials, because the cold map is slightly wrong once the frame is hot.
  3. Set the chamber target in the filament preset (or on the screen) rather than hoping the bed alone will do it.
  4. Let the soak complete.If you scale to large parts, consider adding a few extra minutes of soak before the first layer. That is a typical practice, not a documented rule.

Filtration and fumes

ABS, ASA, PC and PA release volatile compounds as they melt, and ABS and ASA in particular smell strongly. The X2D integrates a filter stack: a G3 pre-filter for dust, an H12 HEPA element for fine particles, and a coconut-shell activated carbon layer for odors and some gases. In Heating mode the air passes through this stack repeatedly. Notes:

  • Purify Air at Print End (a print option) needs the external exhaust fan, and an exhaust pipe can vent outdoors. The external exhaust fan bundle plugs into a 6-pin port on the filament buffer.
  • An exhaust-kit filter is also supported by the documentation if you want filtered exhaust rather than room venting.
  • Replace the carbon filter when it is heavily contaminated. The cover may be rinsed but must be completely dry before it goes back.
  • HEPA and carbon do not make high-temperature printing "safe" on their own; ventilate the room, and see the engineering plastics page for health notes.

3. Door and lid handling

With a heated chamber, the door and lid are part of the thermal system. Rules of thumb:

ABS, ASA, PC, PA

Keep door closed and lid on. Do not open the door until the chamber has cooled to a handling temperature; this prevents a sudden temperature drop that can crack tall parts. Use the screen's chamber reading, not a guess.

PLA, TPU

Use Strong Cooling. For heat-sensitive filaments, a documented air mode is enough; opening the door is a community trick for PLA in hot rooms, but follow Bambu's recommended mode first. Check the printer's open-door detection setting before you do it.

The chamber is hot and so are the surfaces

At 65 °C chamber and a bed up to 120 °C, a quick reach-in for a pause or filament change can burn. The X2D has open-door detection and idle-heating protection (heating is turned off after 5 minutes of idle), but treat both as backup, not as procedure. Wear heat-resistant gloves for hotend work.

Auxiliary nozzle on engineering plastics

The right nozzle is rated for ABS, ASA, PC, PA, PET, PAHT-CF and PA6-GF, and with caution for PETG and some others, but it is capped at 200 mm/s and about 1000 mm/s² and needs a manually calibrated K. PA6-CF is rated "not recommended", PPA-CF and PPS-CF are not supported there. Use it for supports or a secondary material, not for the visible surface of an engineering part.

5. Drying before printing

Nylon, PC, PET and PETG are hygroscopic: they absorb moisture from the air. Wet filament steams in the nozzle and prints with bubbles, weak layers, stringing and a rough surface. Dry spools before they go into the AMS or the printer. The AMS 2 Pro can dry spools itself, with up to 65 °C from its two heating modules (when ambient is about 25 °C or higher), but remember the documented rules:

  • Do not mix high-temperature and low-temperature spools in one AMS during a drying run.
  • Use rotating mode for most spools; static mode is not for low-softening materials.
  • The X2D chamber can also act as a drying space. Firmware 01.02.00.00 allows motors to power off while drying in the chamber.
  • Drying power is reduced during printing unless the AMS has the optional 24 V / 4 A adapter.
  • Replace the AMS desiccant when it is purple, or after roughly 4 to 6 weeks of continuous use.
Typical drying guidance

Follow the spool label first. As a typical cross-check, PA and PET family materials need the longest and hottest drying; PETG and PC are in the middle; ABS and ASA are less hygroscopic. Details and storage are on the drying and storage page.

6. Material-by-material strategy

For every material below the common backbone is the same: dry spool → correct plate and glue → high-temperature bed leveling → heating air mode → full pre-heat soak → closed door → cool down in the chamber. The differences are in the details. Temperatures without a "documented" tag are typical starting points; your spool label wins.

ABS

Strengths: cheap, tough, machinable and smoothable with vapor (see post-processing). Challenge: high shrinkage, so warping and layer cracks without a warm chamber.

  • Air mode: Heating. Close the exhaust path; use the filter.
  • Bed: 100–110 °C is the documented typical range for ABS and PC. High-temperature bed leveling first.
  • Soak: do the full pre-heat; add a few minutes for large parts (typical).
  • Cooling fan: little or none for large parts; more fan for small overhang-heavy parts (typical). Part-cooling fans cool layers, which is the opposite of what you want for adhesion.
  • Plate: textured PEI usually works without glue (typical); use a glue stick on smooth surfaces if the first layer lifts.
  • Brim: use a brim for any large, flat footprint.

ASA

ASA behaves like ABS but resists UV and weather, which makes it the default outdoor engineering plastic. Treat it exactly as ABS, with two additions: ASA fumes are also unpleasant (keep the filter in and ventilate), and its best use is exterior, visible parts where UV resistance counts. Bambu lists ASA as available on all main hotends including 0.2 mm. For ASA with carbon fiber (ASA-CF) the chart needs caution on the HF 0.4 hotend, and the auxiliary nozzle is rated "not recommended".

PC (polycarbonate) and PC blends

PC is tough, heat-resistant and clear-ish when unfilled, and the hardest of the group to print: it shrinks strongly, absorbs moisture and needs high nozzle temperatures. The X2D's 300 °C nozzle limit and 65 °C chamber are precisely what PC needs.

  • Hotend: 0.4 mm or larger; the 0.2 mm hotend is not recommended for PC in the chart.
  • Dry thoroughly and keep it dry: PC bubbles quickly when wet.
  • Soak long; do not open the door early. Tall PC parts cool slowly in the chamber.
  • Wide, thick brim; avoid sharp corners (round them in CAD, see design patterns).
  • Auxiliary nozzle: rated as available for PC, but speed limits still apply.

PA (nylon), PAHT-CF, PA6-GF, PA6-CF

Nylons offer wear resistance, toughness and good fatigue life. They are the most moisture-sensitive materials in this guide and the most important to dry; stored open they can absorb enough water to ruin a print within hours to days (typical).

  • Hotend: hardened steel (standard). Carbon- and glass-filled grades need 0.4 mm or larger. HF 0.4 is rated with caution for fiber blends; HF 0.6 and 0.8 are fine.
  • Air mode: Heating, filter on. High-temperature bed leveling first.
  • Plate: engineering plate is the typical choice for nylon; textured PEI works for some blends. Add glue if a plate recommends it.
  • Run the filament out of a dry source. AMS 2 Pro drying (rotating mode) is the intended route; avoid long idle periods with the spool in a humid room.
  • Aux nozzle: PAHT-CF and PA6-GF are rated available; PA6-CF is "not recommended"; PPA-CF is not available.

PET-CF and other carbon-fiber blends

PET-CF, PETG-CF, PLA-CF and ASA-CF trade some toughness for stiffness and a dry, matte surface. Their abrasiveness is handled by the hardened-steel extruder gears and nozzle; the AMS tubes can still wear faster with third-party fiber filament, whereas Bambu's own are tuned for the AMS. Cold pull a nozzle with plain PLA or PETG only; never cold pull with a fiber-reinforced filament.

PPS-CF

Not a supported X2D material in the standard kit

In Bambu's X2D compatibility chart, PPS-CF is marked as not available on every standard-kit hotend (0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 mm and the HF hotends) on the main side, and not available on the auxiliary side. This guide therefore does not give a PPS-CF recipe. PPS-CF needs very high nozzle temperatures that the X2D's 300 °C limit and 65 °C chamber were not stated to support. If you want to print it, check Bambu's current compatibility page before buying any filament.

First-layer troubleshooting reminders

If prints refuse to stick: wash the plate with warm water and dish soap, do not touch it with fingers, run first-layer calibration (adjustment steps of 0.02 mm, up to 0.05 mm), and re-run (high-temperature) bed leveling. A pattern in a fixed place usually means oil on the plate. More on first layer and adhesion.

8. Avoiding warping on large parts

  1. Reduce the flat footprint.Chamfer the base edge, add rounded corners, and cut tall slabs into joined pieces if the design allows.
  2. Add a brim or mouse ears.The wider the first-layer contact, the more pull it can resist.
  3. Choose the right plate and keep it clean.Skipping the soap-and-water wash costs more reprints than any other single habit.
  4. Hold the chamber stable.Do not open the door; do not change fan targets mid-print; avoid drafts from open windows.
  5. Reduce internal stress.Fewer, thicker walls and moderate infill beat dense, thin infill: dense infill adds mass that contracts. Use a gyroid or similar pattern (typical).
  6. Orient for strength and stress.Align the longest axis with the bed's short side; print on edge only if the design survives it. See strength and orientation.
  7. Cool down slowly.Leave the part in the closed chamber until it is comfortably cool.

9. Annealing in the chamber: an idea, with caveats

Annealing means holding a finished part at an elevated temperature below its softening point for a period, then cooling slowly, to relieve internal stress and (for semi-crystalline plastics) raise crystallinity and heat resistance. The X2D's chamber and heatbed can reach 65 °C and 120 °C, and its documentation shows the chamber being used for drying.

Caveats: this is an idea, not a Bambu procedure

Bambu does not document annealing in the X2D chamber. Parts can shrink, deform or sag; unsupported spans and thin walls are most at risk; the result depends on material and geometry. If you try it, work on a sacrificial copy, support the part on a flat, heat-tolerant surface, stay below the heat-deflection temperature of the material (typical practice), and monitor from the live camera. Do not exceed the printer's limits and do not leave it unattended. Many parts do not need it.

Key takeaways

  • A warm chamber cuts the temperature gap between layers, so less stress locks into ABS, ASA, PC and nylon.
  • The X2D heats the chamber actively up to 65 °C; plan a pre-heat (about 30 minutes when the bed is above 90 °C).
  • Use Heating mode for ABS/ASA/PC/PA and Strong Cooling for PLA/TPU; keep the door and lid shut for the former.
  • High-temperature bed leveling first; clean plate; brim; rounded corners.
  • Dry nylon, PC and PET before printing; the AMS 2 Pro can do it, but follow the mixing rules.
  • PPS-CF is not available on any standard-kit hotend according to Bambu's chart; the 0.2 mm hotend is not for PC or fiber-filled filament.
  • Annealing in the chamber is an unofficial idea; test on sacrificial prints.
