First layer & bed adhesion

Most failed prints die in the first five minutes. Learn what a perfect first layer looks like, why plastic shrinks and lifts, and how to make any material stay put and still come off cleanly.

Beginner → Intermediate⏱ 35 min read
What you will learn

How to read a first layer at a glance, set Z-offset by sight, choose and clean a build surface, pick the right adhesion aid for each material, use brims, rafts and mouse ears, stop elephant foot and warping, level the bed, and diagnose every common first-layer failure from a picture.

Why the first layer matters so much

A 3D print is a stack of thin plastic lines. Every line is glued to the one below it by heat. The very first line has nothing below it except the build plate, so it has to stick to a different material, at a different temperature, while the rest of the part tries to pull it loose. Three jobs fall on that one layer:

  • Hold the part down against nozzle drag, travel moves and, most importantly, the internal stress created when hot plastic cools and shrinks.
  • Provide a flat reference: every following layer copies the first one. A first layer that is 0.1 mm thicker on one side makes a tilted part, and small errors in the base get magnified into visible lean at the top.
  • Create the visible bottom surface of your part. Whatever the bed looks like, the underside of the print looks like it too.

The good news: the first layer is easy to judge by eye, and a few minutes of tuning per printer and per plate pay back on every print you make afterwards.

Z-offset and squish: the core idea

A typical first layer is set to a height of about 0.2 mm even when the rest of the print uses 0.12 mm or 0.16 mm layers, and the line is deliberately extruded a little wider than the nozzle (for example 0.5 mm wide from a 0.4 mm nozzle). The plastic leaves the nozzle as a round "noodle" and gets pressed flat by the nozzle tip as it moves. The gap between the tip and the plate determines the final shape.

How to set Z-offset by eye

  1. Print a thin test patternUse a single-layer full-bed or large-square pattern (many slicers and printers include one; you can also print a 150 × 150 mm square, one layer thick). Clean the plate first.
  2. Watch the first lines being laidLines should look slightly flattened and touch their neighbors. If they look like round tubes with visible gaps, lower the nozzle (more negative offset or "closer").
  3. Adjust live in small stepsMove 0.02 mm at a time. Wait one or two lines for the change to show. Larger jumps overshoot and gouge the plate.
  4. Inspect the finished sheetHold it at an angle under light. It should be a uniform, slightly glossy skin without grooves. Run a fingernail across it: you should feel almost no ridges.
  5. Try the paper test only as a rough startOn printers without auto Z, a sheet of paper that drags slightly is a starting point. Always refine by looking at real extrusion.
  6. Save itStore the value per plate (smooth PEI and textured PEI need different offsets) and note it in your calibration log.
Why squish works

Imagine squeezing toothpaste onto a table while dragging the tube. With the tube lifted high the paste is a fat worm that barely touches. Pressing the tip close forces the worm to spread wide and flat, increasing the contact area between plastic and plate, and pushing each line into its neighbor so they weld together. The pressure also drives molten plastic into the micro-texture of the surface, which is a big part of why adhesion improves.

Common mistake

Trying to cure poor adhesion with temperature or glue when the real problem is Z-offset that is 0.05–0.1 mm too high. If lines look round, fix squish first, then everything else.

Squish, line width and flow: a small bit of math

The slicer calculates how much plastic to extrude from the cross-section of a line. A common model treats the line as a rectangle with rounded ends: the area is roughly (width − height) × height + π × (height / 2)². For a 0.5 mm wide, 0.2 mm high line this is about 0.30 × 0.2 + 0.031 = 0.091 mm². At 50 mm/s that is 4.6 mm³/s, easy for any hotend. If the real gap is smaller than the slicer assumes, the plastic still has to go somewhere, which is why too-low Z creates ridges and may even stop extrusion (the nozzle tip is blocked by the plate and the extruder clicks or skips).

Cleaning: the most underrated adhesion upgrade

Skin oil, finger grease and old glue are what most "my plate lost its stickiness" complaints really are. Make cleaning a routine, not a rescue.

🧼

Dish soap + warm water

Best for textured PEI and oily films. Scrub with a soft brush or sponge, rinse well, dry with a lint-free cloth. Do this every few weeks, or when IPA no longer helps.

🧴

Isopropyl alcohol (IPA, 90 %+)

Quick wipe between prints. Evaporates fast and removes light oil. Use a clean paper towel and wipe in one direction; do not keep rubbing the same dirty spot.

🚫

Do not touch

Fingerprints are the number one cause of random adhesion failures. Hold the plate by its edges and flip it only with clean hands or gloves.

Pro trick

If a smooth plate has lost its grip after many glue-stick prints, a warm dish-soap wash followed by IPA often brings it back to "new" behavior. A few drops of dish soap on a wet sponge, ten seconds of scrubbing, and a good rinse beat any chemical cocktail.

Avoid

Acetone on PEI plates (it attacks the coating), abrasive pads on smooth plates, scraping with metal tools on coated spring steel, and sanding. Never print directly into the bare steel of a coated sheet when the nozzle could gouge it.

Plate care calendar

  • Every print — Wipe with IPA only if the surface looks dull or you have touched it. Never wipe a hot plate: it can streak.
  • Weekly (heavy use) — Dish soap wash, rinse, dry.
  • Monthly — Inspect for gouges, bubbles in the coating, bent steel.
  • When sticking changes suddenly — Check Z-offset first, then clean, then reconsider glue and temperature.
  • Replace — When coating peels, deep scratches appear in the print area, or the plate no longer sits flat.

Adhesion aids: glue stick, hairspray and friends

Sometimes the plate alone is not enough, or it is too good and the part will not release. An adhesion aid works in one of two ways: it adds a tacky layer that holds plastic (hairspray, PVA-glue solution, ABS slurry) or it adds a thin sacrificial layer that stops plastic bonding to the sheet so you can release it (glue stick on PEI with PETG).

Glue stick (PVA type)

The most common aid. Apply a thin, even layer in a crosshatch to the area under the part, let it dry, and print. Washes off with water. Works as adhesion for PLA/ABS/PC on glass and as a release agent for PETG and nylon on PEI.

Hairspray (strong hold, unscented)

Spray lightly from 25–30 cm onto a removed plate away from the printer. Good for glass and for ABS. Leaves a sticky film; wash off with warm water.

ABS slurry (ABS juice)

Dissolve scrap ABS in acetone (about 1 g per 10 mL) and brush a thin layer onto cold glass or plate. Dries to a very grippy ABS film for ABS/ASA. Use outdoors or with strong ventilation; acetone is flammable and the fumes are irritating.

Special adhesives

Branded liquid glues for nylon, PC and carbon-fiber filaments. They are formulated for high bed temperatures and for easy release. Useful where glue stick fails at 90–110 °C.

Application rule

Less is more. A thick lumpy layer makes a rough, uneven bottom surface and can raise the effective Z height. Apply thin, spread with a damp finger or a cloth, let it dry, and wipe off high spots before printing.

Bed temperatures and the glass-transition idea

Plastics soften at their glass-transition temperature (Tg). Above Tg the material is rubbery and sticky; below it, it is rigid. A bed set near (but not far above) the material's Tg keeps the lowest layers soft enough to stay flat and gripping, while the higher layers (which are further from the bed) cool and set. If the bed is far below Tg, the bottom layers harden instantly, shrink, and peel. If it is far above Tg for too long, the bottom layers sag and flare out as elephant foot.

Brim, skirt, raft, mouse ears

When the bottom face of the part is small, tall, or made of a warp-prone material, extra helper structures give the first layer more area to grab or hide the ugly bits.

🔧 Tuning brim settings

  • Width: start with 5 mm; use 8–10 mm for large ABS parts or tall thin pieces. The aim is to put the brim where warp forces are strongest: the corners.
  • Brim ears vs full brim: some slicers (including Bambu Studio and OrcaSlicer) offer "auto" brim that adds width only at sharp corners.
  • Brim-to-object gap: 0.05–0.1 mm makes the brim peel off in one piece. 0 gives maximum hold but is harder to remove.
  • Inner brim: adds a brim inside holes and gaps; useful for large, thin-walled parts.
  • Brim speed: follows first-layer speed; do not set it too high.
Trick

A brim is also great as an elephant-foot hider and as a first-layer quality check: if the brim lines are not perfect, the part's lines will not be either, and you can cancel after two minutes.

🔧 Rafts in detail

A raft is a printed mat: a thick, widely spaced base layer (often 0.3 mm), a few interface layers, and a small gap before the part starts. Because the raft's underside is printed on the plate and the part's underside is printed on the raft's top, small plate irregularities and bed temperature gradients matter less. The cost is speed and surface quality of the bottom.

  • Raft layers: 2–3 base layers and 1–3 top (interface) layers are typical.
  • Raft expansion / margin: 3–5 mm beyond the part outline.
  • Air gap (Z): 0.1–0.2 mm. A smaller gap bonds better but is harder to peel.
  • Best use: PETG or nylon on a poor plate, ABS on glass, tiny pieces on textured plates when you want a flat bottom, and any case where the model's bottom is not flat.

Elephant foot

The bottom few layers of the part may bulge outward and look like an elephant's foot. Hot bed + slow cooling + heavy squish = soft plastic being squashed by the weight of the layers above. It is purely cosmetic in most cases but ruins press fits and tight tolerances.

Remedies, from simple to strong:

  1. Raise Z-offset by 0.02–0.05 mmLess squish means less outward flow. Do this first.
  2. Lower bed temperature5–10 °C lower for PLA often removes it with no loss of hold.
  3. Use "elephant foot compensation"In most slicers, 0.1–0.3 mm. It insets the first layer's outline without changing the design.
  4. Chamfer the model's bottom edgeA 0.4–0.6 mm 45° chamfer on the bottom perimeter eliminates the problem in CAD and also hides it where parts need to seat.
  5. Reduce first-layer flowOnly a few percent, only when squish is already right.

Warping: the physics of curling corners

Warping is the biggest real adhesion problem in FDM. The physics is simple: almost every plastic shrinks as it cools from its printing temperature to room temperature. In an unconstrained block this is harmless, but a 3D print is not unconstrained: the lowest layers are held by the bed, while upper layers cool later and shrink on top of them. The top contracts and tries to drag the part into a bowl. At the corners the stress concentrates and the plastic peels from the plate.

The math, simplified

Shrink strain ≈ α × ΔT, where α is the material's thermal expansion coefficient and ΔT is how far it cools after solidifying. ABS (α around 90 × 10⁻⁶ per °C) cooling 80 °C shrinks about 0.7 %: on a 200 mm part that is 1.4 mm of tendency to move. PLA has a lower α and a lower solidification temperature, so its stress is typically much lower. Warping force grows with part length and with the temperature difference between layers, so long, flat, large parts warp worst.

Longeredges collect more shrink: double the length ≈ double the tendency to curl
Sharpercorners concentrate stress: round corners with 2–5 mm radius warp far less
Hotter chamberless temperature drop between layers = less stress

Anti-warp toolbox

  • Hold the heat: use an enclosure or closed chamber. Printers with an actively heated chamber (like the Bambu X2D with up to 65 °C) allow warp-prone filaments with far less drama.
  • No drafts: an open window, AC, or a fan blowing on the printer cools one side faster and tears that corner free.
  • Keep the part cooling fan low or off for ABS/ASA/PA/PC. For PLA, high fan is fine because PLA's shrink is small.
  • Design for it: round the corners, avoid large flat bottoms, add chamfers, split long parts, use "mouse ears", reduce the footprint's aspect ratio, and add holes or ribs that break up big flat layers.
  • Slow the layers: a longer layer time keeps the layer warm longer. Counter-intuitively, very high speed with strong cooling is the worst combination for warp-prone filaments.
  • Pre-heat (soak): let the bed and chamber reach temperature for 10–20 minutes before starting so the whole plate is evenly warm and the enclosure is warm.
  • Use a brim or raft with generous width for large parts.
  • Reduce infill density at the bottom? Not usually helpful; instead decrease the number of layers that are 100 % solid.

Drafts and enclosure

For PLA and PETG you can print open-frame on many machines, but any moving air that makes the room feel cool will cool the plastic unevenly. An enclosure does three things: stops drafts, holds heat, and reduces noise and fumes. For ABS and ASA it is practically mandatory. Be aware of the opposite problem: very warm enclosed chambers (above ~40 °C ambient) can soften PLA near the hotend and cause heat creep clogs. Open the lid or door for PLA when the chamber is heat-soaked, or use the printer's chamber-fan and vent setting. See cooling for per-material fan strategies.

Bed leveling, mesh and flatness

"Leveling" actually means two things: making the plate parallel to the nozzle's path (tramming), and compensating for the plate not being perfectly flat (mesh). The first-layer gap is only 0.2 mm, so a bed that is 0.1 mm off on one side has already ruined half the squish.

📐

Manual leveling

Turn screws or knobs at three or four points while a paper or feeler gauge sits under the nozzle. Needed on older or budget machines. Re-check after moving the printer or swapping plates.

📡

Auto bed leveling (ABL)

A probe (inductive, capacitive, strain-gauge, load-cell, or nozzle-contact) measures many points and the firmware tilts and warps the toolpath height to match. You still need correct Z-offset.

🗺️

Mesh bed leveling

A grid of probe points (for example 5 × 5 to 10 × 10) builds a height map. The firmware interpolates between points, which fixes bowed plates and warped heat beds.

Practical leveling rules

  • Level hot. Metal expands, so probe or tram at your usual print temperature for the material (heat-soak 5–10 minutes).
  • Clean the nozzle before any contact probing; blobs of plastic on the tip give false heights.
  • Re-mesh after changing plate, nozzle, or bed temperature by a lot. Many printers store a mesh per bed temperature.
  • Mesh cannot fix a bad frame. If the gantry is tilted, rails are loose, or the Z-axis binds, address mechanical causes first. See calibration: mechanical check.
  • Mesh size matters. For a small plate, 5 × 5 is plenty. For large beds, adaptive meshing probes only the area occupied by the print, saving time and focusing accuracy.
Bambu X2D note

Bambu printers run automatic bed leveling and Z-offset calibration as part of start-up calibration, so the manual paper test does not apply. What still matters: keep the supplied textured PEI plate clean and seated flat, make sure no debris is under it, and let the printer's pre-print routines run. If a first layer is off on a Bambu machine, clean the plate and the nozzle before touching anything else. Getting prints off the plate

A print that is stuck for life is as much a failure as one that peels away. Good adhesion means "stays during the print, releases afterwards".

  1. Let it coolWait until the bed is at room temperature (or below 30 °C). Contracting steel and plastic break the bond for you. This alone solves most "stuck" PLA on PEI.
  2. Flex the plateSpring-steel sheets are designed to bend. Remove the sheet from the printer and flex it gently in both directions. Use gloves for hot prints.
  3. Use a thin spatula, not a screwdriverSlide a thin plastic or flat metal scraper in at a corner with the blade angled slightly up. Pry with a low angle; do not hammer.
  4. Freeze trickFor stubborn large prints, put the plate in a freezer for 10–15 minutes (not a wet freezer: dry the bottom). The plastic and plate shrink differently and let go.
  5. Water or IPA at the seamFor glue-stick prints, a few drops of water at the edge dissolves the glue. IPA under a part on glass breaks the bond (use sparingly; keep it away from electronics).
  6. Never pry toward your handScrapers slip. Keep your free hand clear of the blade path and wear cut-resistant gloves for large prints.
Safety

Hot beds are 60–110 °C: they burn skin. Spatulas can cut. When using IPA near a hot bed, remember it is flammable and its vapor ignites easily: do not apply it to a hot plate and never inside a closed printer.

The first-layer calibration pattern

A single large square shows overall squish, but a five-square pattern (one near each corner and one in the middle of the bed) shows uniformity. If the center square looks different from the corners, the bed is bowed, tilted, or the mesh did not cover the area. Many printers and slicers include a built-in version; you can also model five 40 × 40 × 0.2 mm squares.

Mini case studies

Case 1: "PLA lifts only on the left side"

Left side of the plate was colder because the printer sat next to a window and the door of the enclosure was ajar. Closing the door and moving the printer fixed it; a brim was no longer needed. Lesson: location and drafts matter more than glue.

Case 2: "PETG tore my smooth plate"

PETG at 80 °C on smooth PEI with maximum squish welded into the coating and tore chips out when removed. Fix: raise Z by 0.03 mm, apply a thin glue-stick layer as release, and always let the bed cool before removing. The same part then popped off by hand.

Case 3: "ABS corners curl at 1 cm height"

An open-frame printer with a part fan at 50 %. Closing the enclosure, turning fan to 0–20 %, raising bed to 100 °C and adding a 10 mm brim with 0.05 mm gap solved the curl. Lesson: reduce temperature differences, not just add glue.

Case 4: "Perfect first layer, then rubbish after layer 1"

The first layer looked great but the second layer scraped through it. The second layer used 0.12 mm height after a 0.2 mm first layer and a part-fan blast. The plastic curled and caught the nozzle. A z-hop of 0.4 mm and a lower fan until layer 4 stopped the collisions.

Pre-print first-layer checklist

  • Plate is clean (soap or IPA) and I did not touch the print area.
  • Nozzle is clean; no filament blob on the tip.
  • Bed and chamber temperatures match the material and have soaked for a few minutes.
  • Z-offset or auto-leveling has run for this plate and temperature.
  • First-layer speed, fan, and brim/skirt are set for the material.
  • I will watch the first three minutes of the print.
  • Model bottom is flat on the plate (or has supports/raft where it is not).
Pro habit

Spend the first two minutes of every print watching the skirt and first layer. Cancelling a bad start costs two minutes; letting a bad start run costs hours and a messy plate. Your eyes are the best sensor you have.

Key takeaways

  • Squish decides adhesion: first fix Z-offset until lines are flat and fused, then look elsewhere.
  • Clean the plate; fingerprints and grease are the invisible villain.
  • Bed temperature should be near the material's glass-transition temperature, not just "as hot as possible".
  • Warping is shrink stress. Long parts, sharp corners, drafts and cold chambers make it worse; enclosure, brims, rounded corners and low fan make it better.
  • Brim and mouse ears are cheap insurance; a raft is the heavy tool.
  • Elephant foot comes from too much squish and heat: raise Z a hair, cool the bed, add compensation or a chamfer.
  • Level hot, mesh the real plate, and keep the nozzle clean.
  • Read the first layer from above: gaps mean too high, ridges mean too low, one-sided problems mean tilt.
