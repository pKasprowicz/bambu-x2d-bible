Speed, cooling & quality

Print faster without ruining the part. Understand what really limits speed (flow, acceleration, vibration, cooling), how to tune each limit, and how to diagnose the surface when something goes wrong.

Intermediate⏱ 40 min read
What you will learn

The speed–quality–strength trade-off, the volumetric flow equation and typical limits, acceleration and jerk, ringing and input shaping, pressure advance, how to cool each material, temperature towers, high-speed habits, noise, layer-height presets, adaptive layers, belt and vibration care, VFA and Z-banding, and a table of sensible speeds for each feature.

🔰 The speed – quality – strength triangle

Every 3D printing profile is a compromise between three goals. You can push hard on two of them, but the third pays the price.

Fast printers have shifted the whole triangle outward: a modern machine does in an hour what an older machine did in four. But the physics is unchanged, and understanding which limit you are hitting is the skill that separates tuned printers from lucky ones. There are four independent limits:

  1. Melting limit (volumetric flow)The hotend can only melt a certain volume of plastic per second.
  2. Motion limit (acceleration & vibration)The frame and belts ring when the toolhead changes direction; fast corners smear.
  3. Pressure limit (extrusion dynamics)The filament in the nozzle is compressible; flow lags commands, which bulges corners.
  4. Cooling limitThe plastic must solidify before the next layer lands on it, especially for small details and overhangs.

Volumetric flow: the hidden speed limit

🔰 What is volumetric flow?

Speed in mm/s sounds like the natural speed measure, but the hotend does not care how fast the nozzle moves; it cares how much plastic it must melt per second. A thin, narrow line at 300 mm/s may need less melting than a thick, wide line at 100 mm/s. That quantity is the volumetric flow rate, measured in mm³/s:

The key formula

Flow (mm³/s) = line width × layer height × speed. Example: 0.42 mm × 0.20 mm × 150 mm/s = 12.6 mm³/s. To find the fastest speed a hotend allows, rearrange: speed = max flow ÷ (width × height).

Maximum flow by hotend and material

Every hotend has a flow ceiling, set by heater power, melt-zone length and nozzle geometry. When you exceed it, the extruder cannot push the plastic through; you see under-extrusion, matte rough lines, skipped steps or the extruder clicking. Typical ranges for a 0.4 mm nozzle follow. Treat them as starting points, not guarantees and measure your own (see max volumetric speed test).

Practical rule

Set the filament's max volumetric speed in your slicer to a tested value (about 80–90 % of the failure point). The slicer then slows lines automatically when a thick, wide line would exceed it, so you can leave your speed settings high and let the flow cap protect quality.

Common mistake

Setting a sky-high speed and wondering why the part looks under-extruded. The printer might simply be asked to melt 40 mm³/s through a 15 mm³/s hotend. Compute flow (width × height × speed) before blaming the filament.

🚀 Raising the flow limit

  • Raise nozzle temperature by 10–20 °C: hotter plastic flows easier and melts faster. Compensate with stronger cooling.
  • Use a high-flow hotend or a longer melt zone (volcano-style, dual heater, ceramic/high-power heater).
  • Use a bigger nozzle (0.6 mm): the same speed gives 2–2.5× the flow, at the cost of fine detail.
  • Dry the filament. Wet filament boils in the nozzle, steam bubbles reduce throughput and quality; see drying & storage.
  • Use a good extruder. Strong dual-gear direct-drive extruders keep grip at high pressure. See printer anatomy.
Bambu X2D

Acceleration and jerk

🔰 Why top speed is not the whole story

Acceleration (mm/s²) is how quickly the toolhead changes speed. A part is full of corners, short walls and travel moves, so the toolhead is constantly speeding up and slowing down. With acceleration a, the time to reach speed v is v / a and the distance needed is v² / (2a). At 200 mm/s and 5,000 mm/s², it takes 0.04 s and 4 mm; at 500 mm/s and 10,000 mm/s², 0.05 s and 12.5 mm. If a wall is shorter than twice that distance, the toolhead never reaches the set speed.

Jerk and junction deviation

Jerk (older firmware) was the instant speed change allowed at a corner without slowing to a stop. Junction deviation (newer firmware, e.g. Marlin 2, Klipper) calculates corner speed from how sharp the corner is and how much deviation from the ideal corner is tolerated. Both allow smoother continuous motion; too high values cause corner shake and ringing, too low values make small details print slowly. With bicycle-like "square corner velocity" settings in Klipper, 5–8 mm/s is a common start; Bambu firmware manages these internally, but the printer's motion limits appear in the slicer profile as acceleration settings.

Acceleration per feature

Modern slicers let you set different accelerations for outer walls, inner walls, infill, top surfaces, first layer and travel. A common strategy:

Ringing, ghosting and input shaping

🔰 What ringing looks like

Ringing (also called ghosting or echoing) shows up as ripples on a wall after a sharp corner or a small feature like a hole or lettering. The toolhead changes direction abruptly, the frame and belts flex like a spring and then oscillate for a few cycles, leaving a decaying wave in the print.

🔧 How input shaping works

If you strike a bell, it rings at its natural frequency. If you tap it again exactly half a cycle later, the second tap cancels part of the first ring. Input shaping does the same with motion commands: each acceleration move is split into two or three smaller moves, timed so the vibration they excite cancels. The printer needs to know the resonance frequency (typically measured with an accelerometer while sweeping the toolhead) and chooses a shaper type: a mild one cancels well but needs little extra time; a stronger one cancels a wider range of frequencies at the cost of rounding corners slightly.

  • Resonance measurement: accelerometers (on Klipper machines) or built-in sensors (on Bambu machines) measure it automatically. On the X2D the printer performs vibration compensation as part of calibration; a separate optional Vision Encoder is available for motion-accuracy calibration.
  • What shapers cannot fix: loose belts, wobbly frames, bad bearings, heavy toolheads on flexing gantries, and printing on an unstable table. Shaping hides these problems; it does not cure them.
  • Re-calibrate after changing the toolhead, moving the printer, changing belt tension, or if ringing returns. See calibration: input shaping.
Ringing test

Print a tall tower with a sharp corner and small text on the side. Raise acceleration in steps along the height (for example 2,000 → 10,000 mm/s²). Pick the highest acceleration where ripples stay invisible, then set outer-wall acceleration to about 80 % of that.

Pressure advance and flow dynamics

🔧 The problem: plastic is springy

Molten plastic in the nozzle is compressed by the extruder. Pushing harder (to extrude faster) raises pressure and makes the plastic flow faster, but not instantly. So when the toolhead accelerates out of a corner, extrusion lags: the line starts thin. When it decelerates into a corner, pressure remains and plastic keeps oozing: the corner bulges. The result is rounded, blobby corners, thin starts to lines and uneven wall thickness.

Pressure advance (Klipper, Marlin "linear advance") predicts this lag and pushes extra filament during acceleration and pulls it back during deceleration. Bambu Studio calls its version Flow Dynamics (and the calibration is "Flow Dynamics Calibration"); they all rely on a single tunable value, usually called K or the advance length, specific to each filament, nozzle and hotend.

  • Typical K values differ wildly with firmware scale and hardware: direct-drive hotends need small values, long Bowden tubes need much larger ones. Do not copy numbers from another printer's forum post.
  • One value per filament: store it in the filament profile. Silk, TPU, CF-filled and wet filaments behave differently.
  • Flow dynamics on the X2D: Bambu's Flow Dynamics Calibration applies to the main nozzle only; the auxiliary nozzle needs a much larger K because of its longer filament path. See Bambu Studio settings & profiles.
  • It interacts with flow ratio: calibrate pressure advance first at a moderate speed, then flow ratio; re-check if either changes a lot.

Cooling

🔰 Why cooling matters

Plastic is only printable while soft. Once laid down it should solidify quickly so that it keeps its shape. If a layer is still soft when the next one arrives, details sag and overhangs droop. If the layer is too cold when the next one arrives, the layers bond weakly and may delaminate. Cooling is the tool that balances these two outcomes.

Minimum layer time

A tiny layer (the tip of a spire, a thin pin) is finished in a second or two, so the nozzle returns to the same spot before the plastic has cooled and the part melts into a blob. The minimum layer time setting slows the print so that each layer takes at least N seconds: typically 5–15 s for PLA and 8–20 s for PETG. The slicer reduces speed down to a minimum, increases fan, and may lift the nozzle away. For very small parts, printing several copies at once provides built-in cooling time.

Cooling vs layer adhesion

Layers bond when polymer chains from the new strand diffuse across the interface into the layer below. That happens only while the interface stays above the glass-transition temperature, which for PLA is around 55–60 °C, for PETG around 80 °C, and for ABS around 105 °C. Strong cooling cuts that diffusion time to a fraction of a second and can reduce layer strength noticeably. It is the reason functional PETG and ABS parts are printed with little fan and why warm chambers help.

Bridge and overhang fan boosts

Most slicers let you set a separate fan speed for bridges and overhangs (for example 100 % for bridges, with 40 % elsewhere in PETG). Always enable it for materials that need low fan for strength. You can also have the fan ramp up over 3–5 layers instead of switching on abruptly, and add "full fan below layer time X seconds" for small features. More in bridges.

Temperature towers

Every filament roll has a "best" nozzle temperature, but the label gives only a range (for example PLA 190–230 °C). The right value depends on your printer, nozzle, speed and the individual filament. A temperature tower prints a stack of sections, each at a different temperature, so you can compare them in one print.

  1. Choose the rangeStart 10–15 °C below and 20–30 °C above the manufacturer's recommended middle, in 5 °C steps (PLA 190–230, PETG 220–250, ABS/ASA 230–260).
  2. Print at normal speedUse the profile you plan to use. Speed changes the optimum temperature; fast printing needs hotter plastic.
  3. Evaluate each sectionBridge sag, overhang curl, stringing, surface gloss, corner sharpness and layer adhesion (try snapping a section: it should flex or break unevenly, not shear cleanly between layers).
  4. Pick the lowest good temperatureChoose the coolest section that still has good layer bonding and clean extrusion, then add 0–5 °C for margin when printing fast.
  5. Record itSave it in the filament profile and in your calibration log.
Trick

Temperature affects flow: raising it makes plastic flow more easily, so re-check flow ratio and pressure advance after a large change (more than 10–15 °C) in nozzle temperature.

High-speed printing practices

🚀 What changes when you go fast

Modern enclosed printers routinely print 200–500 mm/s on straight walls. Getting there reliably is not only about raising numbers. Several things have to line up:

  • Dry filament. Moisture turns to steam in the nozzle; at high flow rates the bubbles show up as rough, hazy surfaces and weak lines. See drying & storage.
  • Hotter nozzle. Plastic must absorb more heat per second. Add 10–30 °C over the slow-speed value, or use a high-flow hotend/nozzle.
  • Calibrated max volumetric speed for each filament. Do not trust a number someone posted online (procedure).
  • Input shaping and pressure advance calibrated, with both revisited after major speed changes.
  • Rigid, tight machine. Loose belts, wobbling tables and light frames amplify ringing.
  • Lower acceleration on outer walls and on detailed features; use high acceleration only on infill and travel.
  • Strong cooling for PLA; at speed the layer time is shorter, so cooling must be stronger or the print will soften.
  • Reliable adhesion and part stability. A tall, narrow part is a lever: a fast print can knock it over.
Do not chase the headline number

Speed claims on a printer's box are maximums for specific test models with specific filament. For typical parts, the time saved by going from 250 mm/s to 400 mm/s on outer walls is small, because acceleration, travel and small features dominate. Quality at a reasonable speed beats a ruined surface at maximum.

Noise versus speed

Noise comes from fans, motors, and vibrations in the frame. Rapid accelerations and resonant frequencies are the loudest. Practical tips:

  • Use a "silent" or "quiet" mode when printing overnight: it lowers speed and acceleration, and often reduces fan speed.
  • Lower acceleration reduces noise more than lowering top speed does, because the motors and frame suffer most during speed changes.
  • Put the printer on a heavy, solid surface with vibration-damping feet. A wobbly table amplifies noise and ringing.
  • Enclosures absorb some noise; closed doors help.
  • Grinding or rattling sounds that were not there before suggest wear (see maintenance), not speed.

Layer height is the biggest single lever on print time, because print time scales almost inversely with layer height for the same walls and infill. Adaptive layer height

🔧 Fine where it matters, fast where it does not

Stair-stepping is visible mainly on shallow slopes (domes, the tops of curved shapes). Vertical walls look the same at 0.08 mm and 0.28 mm. Adaptive (variable) layer height lets the slicer use thick layers on vertical sections and thin layers where the surface is nearly flat. You get close to fine-layer quality in much less time.

  • Enable it in the slicer and set a min and max (for example 0.08–0.28 mm with a 0.4 mm nozzle; the maximum should not exceed about 75 % of the nozzle diameter).
  • Use the "smooth" slider or a manual height-range modifier to apply extra detail only on selected zones, such as a face on a bust.
  • Adaptive heights change the thickness of every layer, so cooling, flow and temperature need margins; thin layers need a little less flow rate, thick ones a lot more.
  • Do not combine with sections that must match another part's layer lines (mating threads, interlocking parts).

Belts, frame and vibrations

Printing quality sits on the mechanical foundation. A printer with loose belts, play in the bed or a wobbly frame cannot be rescued by software. The main points:

  • Belt tension: Equal on both belts of a CoreXY machine. Pluck the belt: it should hum with a clear, equal tone on each side.
  • Pulley set screws and eccentric nuts: Check them twice a year; a loose pulley causes sudden layer shifts.
  • Linear rails and rods: Keep them clean and lightly lubricated per the manufacturer; dry rails cause rough motion and noise.
  • Frame: Square, tight bolts, standing on a firm surface. Do not store anything on top of a printer that vibrates.
  • Spool and cable drag: A heavy spool jerking the filament is a hidden source of artifacts, especially for filaments that stick on the spool edge.

VFA and Z-banding

Two kinds of regular surface bands are often confused.

Z-banding

A bent or poorly aligned lead screw, a wobbling coupler or an uneven Z motor step pulls the layer positions slightly off the ideal. The bands repeat every full turn or every fraction of a turn of the Z screw (for example every 2 or 4 mm on common designs). Fixes: straighten or replace the screw, align the coupler, adjust the Z-nut (or the antiwobble mechanism), and clean and lubricate the lead screw. Choosing layer heights that are exact multiples of the motor's full step distance on some machines (for example 0.04 mm multiples on a typical 0.4 mm lead-screw pitch) also avoids microstepping errors.

VFA (vertical fine artifacts)

VFA appear as very fine ripples tied to motor steps, belt teeth and resonances; they change with print speed. Fixes: tune belt tension, change the outer-wall speed by 10–20 % (the pattern often disappears at some speeds), reduce the acceleration, check the motor current and use a different stepper interpolation or driver mode if your firmware exposes it. A different speed is the quickest test.

Ironing

Ironing moves the nozzle across the top surface again with very low flow (5–15 % of normal) to smooth it. Use it for flat tops that will be seen or touched; it adds time, so reserve it for the final pass on important parts.

Key takeaways

  • Speed, quality and strength compete; choose the corner of the triangle that matches the part.
  • Volumetric flow (mm³/s = line width × layer height × speed) is the true speed limit. Calibrate max flow for each filament.
  • Acceleration matters more than top speed for both time and ringing. Input shaping and pressure advance let you go faster without paying with quality.
  • Cooling is a balance: more fan sharpens overhangs but weakens layers; engineering plastics want warmth.
  • Temperature towers find the cool end of the good range; re-check flow after large changes.
  • Layer height is the biggest time lever, and adaptive layers give fine surfaces for less time.
  • Mechanical condition (belts, frame, screws) underlies every result; software cannot hide a loose machine.
  • Z-banding repeats with the screw; VFA changes with speed. Diagnose by testing a different speed.
