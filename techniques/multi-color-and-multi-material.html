Multi-color & multi-material

Print in several colors or several plastics in one job. Learn the hardware approaches, why color changes create waste, how to shrink that waste, and how to design models that look great in color without hours of purging.

Intermediate⏱ 40 min read
What you will learn

Five ways to print with more than one filament, what happens inside the nozzle during a change, how prime towers and flush volumes work, how to cut waste, how to design for color, which materials stick to each other, and the usual AMS-style pitfalls and how to avoid them.

🔰 Five ways to print in more than one filament

A single-nozzle printer has one melt zone and one nozzle, so changing filament means getting one plastic out and the next in. Different machines solve that problem in different ways, each with its own strengths and costs.

1. Filament swaps (M600 and pause-at-layer)

The simplest method: tell the slicer to pause at a certain layer. The printer parks, you unload the old filament, load the new one, purge a short length until the new color flows cleanly, and resume. The G-code commonly used is M600 (filament change), though some firmware calls it a pause plus a macro. It needs no extra hardware and wastes only a few centimeters of filament, which makes it ideal for stripes and layers of color such as lithophanes, name plates, and a color change halfway up a vase. The drawback is that you must be at the printer, and every change adds a manual step.

2. Multi-spool feeders (AMS-style systems)

A feeder holds several spools and pushes the chosen filament through a tube to a single extruder. The system retracts the old filament out of the hotend (often into a buffer or back to the feeder) and loads the new one automatically. It can do hundreds of changes per print with no human help. Because there is still one nozzle, every change must flush the old color out of the melt zone before the new color is clean, and that creates waste (see waste mechanics). Examples include Bambu's AMS family, Prusa's MMU, Creality's CFS and similar systems.

3. Toolchangers

A toolchanger parks several complete toolheads on a rack. To change colors the gantry puts one head down and picks up another. Because each head carries its own nozzle and its own filament, there is nothing to purge from the melt zone; at most you wipe the nozzle on a small tower to remove ooze. The result is very low waste, and a toolchanger can combine very different materials (for example PLA and PETG with different temperatures). Costs are mechanical complexity, alignment between heads (offsets must be calibrated to a fraction of a millimeter), and heads that idle at a standby temperature and leak.

4. Dual-nozzle and multi-nozzle toolheads

Two nozzles share one carriage; the active one lowers while the idle one is lifted (or both sit at fixed offsets and the slicer plans around their positions). Each nozzle can hold a different filament, so for two-color or model-plus-support prints almost no purging is needed. A dual-nozzle machine with an AMS-style feeder on each side can do both: print fast with minimal waste while still reaching many colors. The nozzles have a fixed offset, so the build area for a given nozzle may be smaller than the full bed.

Bambu X2D

The X2D is a dual-nozzle printer designed to cut the purge needed for two-filament jobs. The two nozzles are not identical in role, so how a model is split between them matters; read Dual-nozzle printing for the limits and strategies.

5. Mixing hotends and gradient printing

A mixing hotend feeds two, three or more filaments into one melt chamber, where they blend before leaving the nozzle. By changing the ratio per layer you get gradients; by changing it quickly you get marbled or "tweed" effects. Colors are never crisp because the chamber holds a pool of the previous mix, and not many printers use this today. It is a fun niche for rainbow vases.

Painting versus assigning

Some slicers let you paint colors directly onto the model surface, while others only let you assign a filament to an entire object or part. The hardware method decides how many colors you can reach; the slicer's tools decide how comfortable it is to use them. Both are covered below.

Purge, flush and the waste problem

🔰 What actually happens during a color change

When a single-nozzle printer swaps filaments, the melted old plastic is still inside the nozzle and melt zone. If the new filament is pushed in, the first segment of extrusion will be a blend of old and new colors. Three jobs have to be done before printing resumes with a clean new color:

  1. Retract and unloadThe old filament is pulled up out of the melt zone, often with a cooling-and-tip-shaping routine so it does not leave strings or blobs in the extruder.
  2. Load and pushThe new filament is fed down to the extruder gears and pushed into the melt zone, shoving the remaining old melt out of the nozzle.
  3. FlushThe printer extrudes extra filament (the flush volume) until the output is clean new color. This material is thrown away or hidden in a purge area.

Flush volume is typically in the hundreds of cubic millimeters per change, but it depends strongly on the colors involved, as seen below.

The prime (wipe) tower

The usual dumping ground is a prime tower (also called a wipe tower): a thin rectangular stack printed beside the model. At each color change the nozzle moves to the tower, flushes there, and then lays down a few tower layers in the new color to condition the nozzle before returning to the model. The tower also serves as an anchor: its layers are present on every layer that changes color so that pressure and flow settle before returning to the part.

🔧 The flush matrix: dark to light needs more

The amount of flush needed depends on how visible the old color would be in the new one. Going from a pale color to a dark one hides leftovers easily, but going from black to white shows even a trace of the old plastic. Slicers store this as a flush matrix: a table whose rows are the "from" filament and columns the "to" filament, with a volume (mm³) in each cell. The slicer usually estimates it from the color values of the filaments and lets you edit it; a global multiplier scales everything.

  • Plan the color order. If the model allows, order layers so that changes go from light to dark more often than dark to light.
  • Multiplier. A global flush multiplier (for example 0.6–1.2 of the matrix) trades waste against color purity. Start at 1.0, lower slowly and inspect the first print for tinted areas.
  • Translucent and silk filaments hide leftovers poorly and often need more flush than the color suggests.
  • Material differences. When temperatures differ (PLA at 210 °C then PETG at 250 °C), the nozzle also has to change temperature, which adds waiting time and ooze.

Minimizing waste

On a busy multi-color print the flush and tower can exceed the weight of the model itself. A small figurine in four colors might use 20 g of model and 40 g of purge. The following techniques bring that ratio down, roughly in order of effect.

Techniques that work on any printer

  1. Reduce color changes per layerEvery extra color on a layer is another trip to the tower. Merge small color islands into the neighboring color or paint fewer regions. Changing color once per layer is far cheaper than five times per layer.
  2. Flush into infillMany slicers can flush the old color into the model's own sparse infill, and into the support, instead of the tower. The purge is hidden inside the part. Enable it in the multi-material options; it works best on parts with a lot of infill volume and no see-through walls.
  3. Flush into a "sacrificial" objectAdd a cheap, simple model, such as a calibration cube or a purge brick, and assign it as the flush target. It costs no extra time and becomes a useful test piece.
  4. Reduce the flush multiplierLower the global flush multiplier in 0.1 steps until you just see slight tint on the model, then go back up one step.
  5. Shrink the towerA narrower tower uses less plastic per layer, but too narrow a tower becomes unstable and cannot hold the required flush. Keep it as small as the flush volumes allow, and use a rib or brim on tall towers.
  6. Place the tower sensiblyPut it near the model so that travel is short and next to the corner where the printer parks, avoiding long moves. A tower too close to the model risks tool collisions.
  7. Reorder filamentsWhere the model lets you pick which color is which, assign the darkest colors to occur after light ones (light → dark is cheap).
  8. Use a skirt instead of wiping when possibleFor two-color prints, some slicers let you skip the tower altogether and purge at the edge.
Keep the waste usefully

Chop leftover purge into small pieces and keep a "rainbow bin"; some people melt them into purge bricks, others use them for prototypes where color does not matter. Pure waste can be sorted by material; do not mix PLA with PETG or TPU, as that cannot be recycled together.

🚀 Where the physics sets limits

Nozzle and melt-zone volume sets a lower bound on flush per change (the volume of the melt chamber and the nozzle channel). High-flow hotends with larger melt volumes need more flush per change. A shorter melt path and a small tip help. Higher temperature lowers viscosity so that the old color flows out faster, so some filaments flush better when printed hot, but ensure the hotter temperature is safe for both materials.

Another effect is filament moisture: wet filament bubbles and leaves residue, so flushing takes longer. Dry spools help every part of multi-color printing (see drying & storage).

Color layering tricks

🔰 One color per layer range

The cheapest kind of multi-color print changes color only at certain heights. Each change costs one flush, not hundreds. Use this for:

  • Stripes and bands on vases, towers and bottles.
  • Signs and plates where the base is one color and the first N layers of text are another. Print the text upside down in the slicer's layer view, or print the sign flat with the lettering raised on the final layers.
  • Color at a specific layer: use "add color change" at a layer in the slicer's preview slider so the printer pauses or swaps at that height.

Lithophanes in color

A lithophane is a thin plastic panel whose thickness varies with image brightness: thin areas let light through (bright) and thick areas block it (dark). Printed in white or off-white filament and lit from behind, it shows a photograph. Multi-color versions stack thin layers of different colors (for example cyan, magenta, yellow and white) so that light passing through several layers shows color, as in a subtractive print.

Filament-height-map art ("Hueforge"-style)

A related technique prints an image as a stack of thin translucent filaments of different colors, with thickness mapped from the image. By varying layer thickness in small steps, one image can show a full range of hues viewed from the front with reflected light. Special software generates the layer plan and the change list, typically with one change per layer range, and the slicer prints a mostly flat model whose "height map" is the picture. Prints are 1–3 mm thick, so the total time can be short, but the number of color changes can be very high. Use opaque white or black as a base to control contrast.

Why this is cheap

Because each change happens on a flat, fixed layer range, the printer can do many changes with little purge if the layers are thin and the flush lands on a hidden surface. This is why layer-based color art is the best starting point for beginners with a feeder.

Designing for multi-color

A well-designed multi-color model is one the slicer can easily split into regions. A badly designed one forces dozens of tiny color changes per layer.

Practical design rules

  • Flat color faces. A color applied on a vertical wall will show the layer lines, but color boundaries on vertical walls are crisp. On sloped surfaces, boundaries become jagged because layers step.
  • Separate bodies per color. Export each color as its own body (or STL part) that overlaps its neighbor by a very small margin (0.02–0.05 mm) or share the exact surface. In the slicer, assign a filament per body.
  • Embed, don't paint, for small icons. Cut a recess in the base and place a second body in the recess so that it fills it completely.
  • Boolean thoughtfully. Subtract the color body from the base so volumes do not overlap, or the slicer may print both.
  • Prefer thick color regions. A color layer less than two layers thick can show the color below.
  • Use the painting tool for organic shapes. Slicers with paint tools let you brush colors onto the surface by triangle, bucket fill, or by angle threshold. Use "fill by angle" to mark all faces facing up.
  • Check the preview. View the sliced model layer by layer, and count changes in the statistics.

Material compatibility in multi-material prints

Color changes inside one material (PLA to PLA) are easy. Mixing different plastics in one part is harder: the layers must bond across the interface, the nozzle temperatures differ and shrinkage can pull the materials apart. Adhesion depends on whether the two polymers are chemically similar enough to intermingle.

Hedge your expectations

This matrix summarizes common experience, not a guarantee. Support materials marked "good" next to a build material are designed to bond enough to print but still release or dissolve later. Where bonding is "poor", design a mechanical lock (dovetail, pin, overlap) rather than relying on stickiness.

Interface layers and bridging across materials

When the second material is printed on top of the first, the interface layer is where adhesion happens. The first layer on a different material is printed over a surface that may be much cooler or hotter than normal, and the flow settings must suit the new filament. To improve the bond:

  • Use a compatible middle layer. For a PLA part supported by a dissolvable filament, use the slicer's support-interface filament assignment, then print a few thin interface layers of the support material directly beneath the model.
  • Raise the nozzle temperature of the second material slightly for the first interface layers, so it melts into the surface rather than sitting on it.
  • Increase overlap. Let the two bodies share a wider surface with a "interlocking" geometry such as dovetails or dashes; most slicers have an "interlocking beam" or "wall overlap" setting.
  • Reduce the cooling on the interface layer to give the bond time to form.
  • Bridging across materials. Bridges that span over a different material can sag or let go. Shorten them with a design change, and test the bridge speed and fan for the upper material first. More in bridges.

Feeder and AMS-style pitfalls

🔧 Common problems and cures

Filament tangled or crossed on a spoolLoose end, spool wound badly, spool jerkRe-wind or secure the end in the spool clip every time; check spools before a long print.
Feeder cannot push or retract filamentBrittle or very soft filament, old dried-out spools, tube frictionAvoid very flexible filament through the feeder (use the external spool or direct feed); replace tubes with kinks.

Wrong color, tinted printInsufficient flush or leftover in the nozzleIncrease the flush multiplier; check the matrix; watch for transparent filaments.
Spool not recognized or runs out unexpectedlySensor not detecting, wrong filament length in the profileVerify the spool amount before the print; allow auto-refill from a same-color spool.
Tower collapsesToo thin, too tall, poor adhesionWiden the tower, add a brim, slow the tower, reduce its layer height cost.
  • Flexibles. Soft TPU compresses in tubes and gears. Most feeders handle only harder grades (for example around 95A); otherwise feed from an external spool straight to the extruder. See flexibles & specialty filaments.
  • Brittleness. Dry filament is more flexible. Heated dryers, or a feeder with built-in drying, help keep spools usable.
  • Mixed temperatures. A change from a low-temperature to a high-temperature filament means the nozzle must heat, wait, flush, and then settle; it takes longer and uses more flush than changes between similar materials.
  • Abrasive filaments. Carbon-fiber or glow-in-the-dark filaments wear the feeder's gears and the nozzle; keep those on a hardened-steel nozzle and consider direct feed.

Preview before you print

Always inspect the sliced preview: check the number of color changes, the tower size and the estimated filament per color. If the tower weighs more than the model, simplify the colors or merge regions before you press print.

Key takeaways

  • Choose the method by how many colors and how much waste you can accept: swap for a few layers, feeder for many colors, dual nozzle or toolchanger for low waste.
  • Every color change on a single nozzle costs a flush; the flush amount depends on the colors, and dark → light costs the most.
  • Cut waste by reducing changes per layer, flushing into infill, lowering the multiplier sensibly and shrinking the tower.
  • Layer-based color (stripes, lithophanes, height-map art) is the cheapest way to get color.
  • Design with big flat color regions and separate bodies; avoid tiny islands.
  • Different plastics bond best when they are chemically alike; otherwise lock them mechanically.
  • Keep filament dry, free-running and appropriately stiff for the feeder path.
