Post-processing

A print coming off the plate is a raw part. Post-processing is everything after: removing supports, sanding, filling, painting, smoothing, strengthening, joining and installing hardware. This page walks the whole ladder from “good enough” to “is that really 3D printed?”.

Intermediate⏱ 40 min read
What you will learn

Which finish to aim for (and how long it takes), how to remove supports cleanly, how to sand and prime properly, how to smooth, coat and paint, how to anneal for strength and heat resistance, how to glue and weld plastics, and how to install inserts, threads and magnets. Safety notes are included wherever a chemical or tool can hurt you.

Choose your finish level first

Post-processing time grows faster than quality. The first minutes give big gains; the last 5 % of perfection can take hours. Decide your target before you start, so you do not over-polish a functional bracket or under-finish a display piece.

Why order matters

Annealing and gluing change shape or add seams, so do them before sanding and painting. Paint and clear coat should come last because they can soften or contaminate surfaces that you still need to glue. Heat-set inserts go in before painting so a slipped iron does not scar the finish.

1 · Support removal and cleanup

🔰 Removing supports safely

  1. Let the plate coolMost parts release more cleanly when cool (below ~30 °C) because the plastic is stiffer and the bed contraction frees the first layer. Parts on textured PEI plates often pop off after a minute.
  2. Start from the outside inGrip the support near its base with pliers or your fingers and bend away from the model, not toward it. Peel in the direction that pulls the support off the surface rather than pushes it into it.
  3. Use flush cutters for stubborn bitsCut each support strut close to the surface, then remove the stub with a sharp craft knife or by sanding. Always cut away from your body and wear eye protection: small plastic fragments fly.
  4. Clean the interfaceRemove leftover interface layers with a deburring tool or a fine file. Smooth bumps with 180–240 grit sandpaper.
Safety

Never pull a blade toward your hand. Use a cut-resistant glove on the holding hand when cutting thick supports, and place the part on a non-slip mat. Eye protection is mandatory when snipping brittle materials such as PLA or PLA-CF.

Hand tools worth owning

🔧 Flush cutters

Trim supports, strings and filament ends cleanly. One flat side leaves almost no stub.

🔪 Hobby knife + deburring tool

Scrape elephant foot, brims and seam bulges. Rotating deburr blades work best for edges.

🧰 Needle-nose pliers

Grip support roots. Long tweezers retrieve small bits inside cavities.

📏 Needle files

Clean holes, slots and sharp corners; round files fix undersized holes.

🧽 Sanding sponges and sticks

Foam-backed blocks follow curves; flat sticks keep edges crisp.

🌬️ Compressed air / soft brush

Dust control. Keeps dust off wet paint and out of the printer.

🔧 Other support types

  • Soluble supports (PVA, BVOH): soak the part in room-temperature water for hours; stir occasionally and refresh the water. PVA is slow and smells when dissolving; BVOH dissolves faster. Dry the part afterwards before painting or sealing.
  • Breakaway supports: special filaments with a weak bond. Peel by hand; use the matching interface material recommended by the filament maker.
  • Dual-nozzle supports: on the Bambu X2D the auxiliary nozzle can lay down support in a different material so the interface peels off easily; see dual-nozzle printing and supports.

2 · Sanding

Sanding turns layer lines into a smooth surface by abrading plastic away. The trick is to work through grits in order, never skipping more than one step. Each grit removes the scratches of the previous one and leaves finer ones.

Pre-paint600–800Silky base for paintWetWipe clean, tack cloth

Technique

  • Wet sanding (with a few drops of dish soap in water) reduces dust, prevents clogging and keeps plastic cool. Use waterproof paper from 400 grit up. Dry sanding is fine for coarse grits, but dust from CF, GF or glass-bead filaments is hazardous: wear a P2/FFP2 respirator.
  • Use backing: sand over a flat block for flat faces, a sponge for curves, folded paper for tight spots.
  • Cross-hatch strokes (alternate 90° directions) so you can see when old scratches are gone.
  • Mind the heat: friction can melt PLA and TPU (gummy surface). Slow down or switch to wet sanding.
  • Keep edges crisp: use a block and avoid rocking; support corners with tape when sanding adjacent faces.
Dust safety

Plastic dust, especially from carbon-fiber, glass-fiber or metal-filled filaments, is a respiratory irritant and in some cases a long-term hazard. Wet-sand whenever possible and wear an FFP2/N95 (or better) mask when dry sanding, plus eye protection. Keep dust away from the printer’s electronics and rails.

Print for sanding

If you plan to sand, print at 0.12–0.16 mm layers with 3+ walls, slow outer walls and Z-seam aligned to a hidden edge. Add 0.1–0.2 mm of extra material in CAD on faces that must keep dimensions. Prints sand faster than they look: you only need to remove the ridges.

3 · Filler, putty and primer

Sanding alone rarely removes every layer line without damaging detail. Filler primer is spray paint loaded with solids that fill fine scratches and layer lines. It is the secret of a smooth painted print.

Raking light

Hold a phone torch at a very shallow angle to the surface and rotate the part. Tiny ridges and pits cast long shadows. Do this after every sanding or priming step; defects that vanish in normal light will otherwise reappear after painting.

4 · Smoothing: heat, solvent and coating

🔧 Hot-air and flame polish (use with caution)

A quick pass of a heat gun or flame can melt the outer skin slightly so layer lines flow together and strings vanish. It works on PLA at low skill levels and on ABS/ASA with practice. The window between “glossy” and “melted blob” is only a second or two.

  • Heat gun: 150–200 °C air from 15–20 cm, moving constantly. Good for removing strings and gentle gloss.
  • Flame (lighter / torch): very fast and risky. Only for non-critical parts, with a fire-safe surface, extinguisher or water nearby, and no solvents in the room.
  • Risks: warps thin walls, blisters PLA, smudges text and detail, and produces fumes. Always ventilate and test on a scrap print.

🚀 Acetone vapor smoothing (ABS and ASA)

ABS and ASA dissolve in acetone. In a closed container, acetone vapor condenses on the surface and softens a micron-thin layer; surface tension pulls it into a glossy, sealed skin. Layer lines vanish, parts become watertight, and gloss resembles injection molding. PLA and PETG do not respond to acetone.

  1. PrepareFinish sanding to ~320 grit first; vapor smoothing hides fine ridges but not large defects. Test on a scrap print.
  2. Set upPut damp (not dripping) acetone-soaked paper towels at the bottom of a sealed glass or metal container. Raise the part on a stand with hooks or a small platform made from skewers.
  3. ExposeClose the lid. Check every 5–10 minutes through glass; remove when the surface is shiny and just starting to look wet.
  4. Air outHang the part to dry in a ventilated area for several hours; handle only by the base because the surface remains soft for a while.
  5. DisposeAllow acetone-soaked towels to dry outdoors on a non-flammable surface; follow local waste rules for solvents.
Fire and health warning

Acetone vapor is highly flammable and can ignite from a spark, a heat plate or a switch. Do not do this near the printer’s hot parts, a heater, or an open flame, and do not heat the container. The vapor is irritating to eyes and respiratory tract and can cause dizziness at high concentrations. Use a ventilated or outdoor workspace and wear nitrile (or acetone-resistant) gloves and eye protection.

Alternatives: ethyl acetate or MEK work on some plastics but are more hazardous; for PLA, a mild approach uses tetrahydrofuran-based products, which are toxic and not recommended for hobby use. Sanding and coating is the safer path for PLA and PETG.

🔧 Epoxy and XTC-3D-style coating

A thin brush-on two-part epoxy flows into layer lines, self-levels and cures into a glossy, hard shell. Products sold for 3D prints (for example XTC-3D) are designed for low viscosity and long working time. You can also use clear laminating epoxy or a UV-curing resin as a top coat.

  1. Sand and cleanSand to 220–320 grit, wipe with isopropyl alcohol (IPA), let dry completely.
  2. Mix exactlyFollow the ratio by volume or weight; stir slowly for 2 minutes to avoid bubbles. Work at 20–25 °C.
  3. Apply thinBrush a thin coat with a disposable foam or soft brush, working quickly. Avoid pooling in recesses; remove drips with a brush.
  4. Rotate while curingOn a skewer or turning rack for 30–60 min to avoid sags; cure 4–24 h.
  5. Sand and finishWet-sand with 600–1000 grit if you need a mirror finish, then paint or polish.
  • Pros: fast build-up, strong, fills fine ridges, waterproofs.
  • Cons: softens sharp detail, adds ~0.1–0.3 mm per coat (account for fits), skin and respiratory sensitizer: gloves, ventilation and eye protection are required.

5 · Annealing for strength and heat resistance

Annealing heats a finished print above its glass-transition temperature (Tg) but below its melting point and holds it there. In semi-crystalline plastics (PLA, PETG partially, nylon, PET-CF, PEEK) the molecular chains rearrange into crystals. The result: higher heat deflection temperature (a PLA part that softened at ~55 °C can survive 80–100 °C), better stiffness and sometimes better layer strength. The price is shrinkage and warping.

PETG70–80 °C (limited benefit)30–60 minLow; mostly stress reliefSlightly more stable

PET-CF / PA-CFper maker, often 100–140 °C1–3 hSmallRaises HDT, relieves stress
ABS / ASANot crystalline: stress relief only at ~80–90 °C1–2 hWarping riskLittle strength gain

How to anneal PLA safely

  1. Pack the partBury it in dry table salt, sand or fine silica powder inside an oven-safe metal container so it keeps its shape and heats evenly. Thin or hollow parts can also be supported by a metal former.
  2. Heat slowlyPreheat to 60 °C, then step to the target (e.g. 90 °C) in 10 °C increments every 10 minutes. Put an oven thermometer next to the part.
  3. Hold15–60 min depending on wall thickness (about 10 minutes per mm of wall is a decent starting guess for thick parts).
  4. Cool slowlyTurn off the oven and leave the door closed until it is warm to the touch.
  5. MeasureCompare before and after; expect 0.5–3 % shrinkage in X/Y, mostly in the direction of extrusion, plus a little growth in Z. Design the part “oversize” (e.g. +1.5 %) if tolerances matter, and test with one part first.
Annealing caveats
  • Do not anneal parts with tight tolerances, threads or press-fits without a test.
  • Do not use a food oven for plastics you intend to cook with later; use a dedicated toaster oven or filament dryer with a high temperature range.
  • Thin walls and low infill collapse; fill the cavity with sand or salt.
  • Printing with a 40–60 °C chamber (see X2D chamber guide) gives some crystallization during the print and can reduce the need to anneal.

Techniques

Spray 2–3 thin coats at 20–25 cm, 10 minutes apart, then wait for a full dry. Heavy coats run and hide detail. Use a color-matched primer (light gray, white or black) under the final color: white under bright colors, black or dark gray under dark colors and metallics.

Thin acrylic with the maker’s thinner to a milk-like consistency; 15–25 psi; spray with overlapping passes. Pre-shading with dark color in recesses and post-shading highlights on top creates depth. Clean the airbrush immediately after use.

Load a stiff brush with a little paint, wipe almost all of it off on a paper towel, then lightly drag the brush across raised details. Paint catches only on high points, instantly highlighting texture on fur, scales and weathered surfaces.

A thin dark wash (diluted brown/black acrylic, or ink) flows into recesses for depth; wipe the surface clean. Weathering: sponge chipping (a sponge dabbed with dark paint), rust stippling, soot with powdered pigment or a spray of thin dark paint, and a final matte clear coat.

Pro tips
  • Spray in warm (15–25 °C), dry weather. Cold and humidity cause blushing.
  • Always let paint cure for 24 h before handling or assembly.
  • Mask with painter’s tape and cut tape with a knife along a straight edge. Burnish the edges to avoid bleed.
  • Paint before assembly where possible, and keep the mating surfaces unpainted (paint adds 0.05–0.15 mm).

Dyeing nylon (PA)

Nylon is porous at the micro scale and absorbs fabric dye. Dyeing gives deep, uniform color even where the surface is scratched, and works on nylon and PA-CF parts.

  1. Clean and sandWash the part, lightly scuff with 400 grit, and degrease.
  2. Prepare the dye bathUse a disperse or all-purpose fabric dye at 80–90 °C in a dedicated stainless pot with a little vinegar or detergent as recommended on the dye package.
  3. SoakImmerse for 5–20 minutes, stirring; remove when color is as strong as you want (it dries slightly lighter). Rinse and dry.

Caveats: the part will absorb water and may change size slightly; re-dry nylon afterwards (see drying & storage); never reuse the pot for food.

Metallic finishes, plating and transfers

  • Metallic paint: shiny metal spray needs a very smooth base (primed + wet-sanded to 800+ grit and often a gloss-black base). Seal with clear.
  • Rub-on waxes and powders (metallic waxes, pigment powders in clear lacquer) give an aged-metal look and highlight raised edges quickly.
  • Electroplating (overview): a conductive layer (graphite or copper conductive paint) is applied, then the part is plated in a copper bath, followed by nickel or chrome. It gives a true metal shell but needs chemicals and power supplies; many hobbyists use a service. The part must be sealed and smooth.
  • Vinyl wraps, decals and water-slide transfers: flat or gently curved surfaces take adhesive vinyl; hydrographic (“water transfer”) film wraps carbon or wood patterns around complex forms. Seal with clear coat.
  • Laser engraving: diode and CO₂ lasers can engrave painted or dark surfaces for logos and serial numbers. Many plastics release toxic fumes when lasered (especially PVC); use only materials you know are safe and ventilate.
  • Soft-touch coat: a rubberized spray (“rubber paint”) gives a velvety feel for grips and cases; apply thin coats on a clean base.

Waterproofing and food contact

  • Making a print watertight: print with 3+ walls, 100 % flow, slightly hotter temperatures and slower speeds, or use vase mode. Seal with epoxy, a brush-on polyurethane or acetone smoothing (ABS/ASA). Test with a water-filled cup for 24 h.
  • Outdoor use: ASA, PETG, nylon or PP survive better than PLA. UV-resistant clear coats help.
Food-contact guidance (caveat)

Even a food-safe filament does not make a printed object food-safe. Layer lines trap bacteria and are almost impossible to clean; nozzles may contain lead or other contaminants from earlier materials; dyes and additives vary. Use printed items for dry, short-contact or non-food uses (cookie cutters used once, then thrown away; stamps; molds for shaping). For long-term contact, print a mold and cast a food-grade silicone, or coat with a certified food-safe epoxy and follow its instructions. When in doubt, do not eat from or drink from printed parts.

  • Preparation: sand both faces with 120–180 grit and wipe with isopropyl alcohol. Rough surfaces give better mechanical keys.
  • Clamp: tape, rubber bands or clamps; epoxy joints are strongest with 0.1–0.3 mm of glue line.
  • Hard-to-glue plastics: PP, PE and TPU resist glue; use mechanical fasteners or plastic welding for PP/PE and flexible adhesive for TPU.

Glue safety

CA glue bonds skin in seconds and can release heat on porous materials like cotton (never wipe CA with a cloth or tissue on your fingers). Epoxy and solvents need gloves, ventilation and eye protection.

🔧 Plastic welding

Fusing plastic by melting both sides and adding filler works like welding metal. For a quick weld, use a soldering iron with a flat tip to melt both edges and push a filament rod into the seam. A 3D printing pen can add material without a tool. After cooling, sand the bump flat. For long seams, a hot-air plastic welder with a triangular nozzle gives a very strong bond in PETG, ABS and nylon.

8 · Threads, inserts, magnets and hardware

🔧 Heat-set threaded inserts

A heat-set insert is a knurled brass nut that you press into a slightly undersized hole with a heated tip. The plastic melts around the knurls and re-solidifies, giving metal threads that survive hundreds of screw cycles, far more than threads printed in plastic.

  1. Design the holeUse the insert maker’s recommended diameter (for most M3 inserts around 4.0–4.6 mm depending on the model) and make it 1–2 mm deeper than the insert so melted plastic has somewhere to go. Print the hole vertically (axis along Z) so it is round.
  2. Heat the iron200–250 °C for PLA/PETG (higher for ABS/ASA/nylon). Use a pointed or flat insert-tool tip rather than a worn soldering tip.
  3. Press slowlyHold the insert on the iron tip, set it on the hole, and apply light, even pressure straight down. You will see plastic flow up around it. Stop when it is flush.
  4. Flatten and coolPress the surface with a flat metal block while the plastic is soft to level any ridge, then let it cool 1–2 minutes before inserting a screw.
  5. Test the pull-outScrew in and pull gently. A good insert resists far more force than a printed thread.
Crooked insert?

Re-heat the tip and straighten while soft. Prevent crookedness with an alignment jig or the guide on the insert tool, and by pressing the iron tip vertically.

Use these as a starting point and check against your printer: holes print 0.1–0.3 mm small. See the cheat sheets for a printable version.

  • Tapping plastic: use a hand tap in a drilled or printed pilot hole; go slowly, back off often, apply a drop of oil on hard plastics. Self-tapping screws in a printed pilot hole (screw diameter minus ~0.5–0.8 mm) work for light duty; they strip if overtightened.
  • Printed threads: work for large coarse threads (M8 and up, or 1.5–2 mm pitch) printed vertically; M3–M5 threads are fragile. Use inserts instead.
  • Drilling: use a sharp bit at low speed; ease off when exiting; a printed pilot hole improves accuracy. Melted plastic clogs bits; clear them often.
  • Reaming: a reamer or drill bit twisted by hand cleans an undersized hole to the exact diameter (e.g. a 3 mm bearing pin).

Magnets, bearings and hardware

  • Magnets after printing: use a hole 0.1–0.2 mm bigger than the magnet; glue with epoxy or CA, checking polarity before the glue sets. Cover with a thin printed layer for a hidden mount (see pause-and-insert).
  • Bearings: press-fit with a hole 0.1 mm smaller than the OD, or use a hole of the exact size and a drop of CA or retaining compound. Do not hammer; use a vise or a bolt and washers to press evenly.
  • Washers and fasteners: use a washer under screw heads to spread load on plastic; stop tightening at the first resistance, because overtightening cracks plastic.
  • Thread locker and gaskets: light thread locker prevents vibration loosening but avoid on plastic unless it is approved for it; use rubber gaskets or foam tape for sealing.

For design-side detail (screw boss geometry, snap fits, captive nuts), see joints & fasteners.

9 · UV resin post-processing

Resin (MSLA/SLA/DLP) prints come out coated in liquid uncured resin, which is toxic to skin and the environment until cured. Post-processing is a must, not an option. See printing processes for how resin printing works.

  • Wash: isopropyl alcohol (≥ 90 %) or water-washable resin with water. Use two baths: a dirty one for most resin and a clean one for the final rinse. Do not wash longer than needed; over-washing can leave cracks and a sticky surface.
  • Cure: UV chamber or sunlight; rotate or use a turntable. Over-curing yellows and embrittles; under-curing leaves tacky, toxic surfaces. Water-submerged curing prevents oxygen inhibition and gives a dry, glossy surface.
  • Safety: nitrile gloves, eye protection, ventilation and a cover for the wash container. Never pour resin or IPA down a drain; cure leftover resin in the sun and dispose of it as solid waste; filter IPA and reuse it.
  • Finishing: cured resin sands and primes easily; wet-sand to avoid dust.

10 · Packaging and shipping

  • Protect fragile features: foam or bubble wrap around thin spikes; boxes at least 5 cm larger than the part in each dimension; fill voids so nothing moves.
  • Layers shear, so avoid pressure on edges: pack so the part is cushioned on its largest faces. Never pack a PLA print in a hot car or a hot warehouse: it can soften and deform above ~50 °C.
  • Documentation: include a short care sheet (cleaning, temperature limits, assembly tips) and, for functional parts, the material and recommended load limits.
  • Labeling: add a customs description and, for resin-printed or chemically treated parts, check shipping restrictions on solvents.
  • Test-ship: ship one to yourself first and inspect it; adjust packaging before selling many.

Common mistakes

Skipping steps
  • Painting directly onto a rough print (layer lines amplify under gloss).
  • Using thick primer coats that hide detail and then crack.
  • Dry-sanding PLA until it melts.
  • Gluing before test-fitting.
Safety slips
  • Vapor smoothing near a heater or an open flame.
  • Using the kitchen oven for plastic without a thermometer.
  • Breathing sanding dust or spray paint without a mask.
  • Mixing resin and epoxy in food containers.

Key takeaways

  • Decide the finish level first; most functional parts only need cleanup and a little sanding.
  • Sand in order through the grits and use raking light to check; primer is the real surface-smoother.
  • Acetone smoothing works only on ABS/ASA and is a fire and health hazard: cold-vapor method, ventilation, no flames.
  • Annealing increases heat resistance but shrinks and warps; test on a scrap and design for it.
  • Heat-set inserts give durable metal threads; follow the insert maker’s hole size and use a controlled iron.
  • Glue choice depends on material: epoxy is the all-rounder; solvent welding fuses ABS/ASA.
  • Treat solvents, resins and dust as real hazards: gloves, ventilation, eye and respiratory protection.
