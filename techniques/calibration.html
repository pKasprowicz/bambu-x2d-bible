Part 4 · Techniques

Calibration playbook

Calibration turns a printer that merely works into one that is predictable. Follow the order of operations, use the right test model for each step, learn what good and bad results look like, and record the numbers so you never repeat work.

Intermediate⏱ 45 min read
What you will learn

Why order matters, how to check the machine itself, how to tune bed, Z-offset, temperature, flow, pressure advance, retraction, volumetric speed, cooling, dimensions and input shaping, how to apply each result, how to manage per-filament profiles and when to repeat tests. A printable calibration log is at the end.

🔰 Order of operations

Calibration steps depend on each other. If you tune flow before the temperature is right, the flow number is wrong after you change temperature. If you tune pressure advance on a loose belt, the pattern is smeared by mechanics, not by plastic. The rule is simple: fix the physical machine first, then first layer, then plastic behavior, then speed and motion.

Not every printer needs every step

Bambu printers (and other enclosed, self-calibrating machines) perform steps 3, 4, 7 and 12 automatically; you should still understand them and know when to trigger a manual run. See Bambu automatic calibration. Other machines may need you to do them by hand.

1–2 · Mechanical check, belts and frame

🔰 Do this before touching the slicer

No software setting repairs a loose machine. Spend ten minutes on the hardware:

  • Frame: All visible screws snug, no wobble when you push the frame gently. The printer stands on a solid, level surface.
  • Axes move smoothly: Power off, move the toolhead and bed by hand (where safe). There should be no gritty spots, binding or loud knocks.
  • Belts: Equal tension, no frayed edges, teeth meshing with the pulleys. A plucked belt gives a clear tone (see belts & vibrations).
  • Nozzle and hotend: Nozzle seated and tight (when hot, per the manufacturer's procedure), no filament leaking around it, fan ducts attached.
  • Extruder: Gears clean, no filament dust build-up, tension correct, filament path free of kinks.
  • Bed and plate: Plate clean, flat and seated properly, with no debris beneath.
  • Squareness: If your printer allows, check that X and Y are square with a printed square or by measuring diagonals (they should be equal within a few tenths of a millimeter).

🚀 Steps per millimeter, e-steps and rotation distance

Firmware needs to know how far each motor moves per command. For axes this is set by belt pitch, pulley teeth and microstepping, and is almost always right by default. For the extruder it depends on the gear, so a wrong value means every flow calculation is off.

  • E-steps (Marlin) = steps per mm of filament. Rotation distance (Klipper) = mm of filament per motor revolution.
  • Test: Mark the filament 120 mm above the extruder entrance, command a 100 mm extrusion (hot, slowly, e.g. 50 mm/min), and measure what remains. If 100 mm was requested but only 97 mm moved, the new value is: new e-steps = old × 100 / 97. In Klipper: new rotation distance = old × actual / requested.
  • Do it once per extruder (and after replacing the gears), not for each filament. Differences between filaments belong to flow rate.
  • Printers with firmware and extruders from the manufacturer (including sealed, enclosed models) usually do not expose or need this step.

The same idea applies to motor current and thermal safety: do not raise motor current beyond what the driver and motor allow just to hide a mechanical issue.

🚀 PID tuning concept

The hotend and bed heaters are controlled by a loop that decides how much power to apply based on the difference between the target and the measured temperature. PID stands for proportional, integral and derivative: P reacts to the current error, I corrects accumulated error, D anticipates change. Badly tuned PID causes temperature to oscillate by several degrees, which shows up as periodic changes in surface gloss or flow, and sometimes as thermal-runaway errors.

Run auto-tune at the temperature you use most (nozzle at a typical print temperature, bed at your usual bed temperature) with the part-cooling fan at its usual speed. Repeat it after replacing a heater, thermistor or hotend, or when the temperature graph wiggles visibly.

3 · Bed leveling and mesh

The goal is a constant distance from nozzle to bed everywhere. Two things matter: the bed's tram (is the gantry parallel to the bed?) and its flatness (is the plate itself warped?).

  • Manual tramming: Adjust screws with a paper or feeler gauge at the four corners and center; repeat once since screws interact. Use the same bed temperature as printing; the bed bends when hot.
  • Automatic mesh: A probe measures a grid of points (for example 7×7), and firmware compensates by raising or lowering Z during the first layers. Make sure the mesh is made hot and with a clean nozzle.
  • Mesh cannot hide a tilt. A tilted gantry or bed should be corrected mechanically first: otherwise the mesh has to correct large errors that fade with height and distort the first layers.

4 · Z-offset

Z-offset sets the nozzle's height above the bed at the first layer. Too high and lines do not stick; too low and they are squashed, and the nozzle may scratch the plate. Detailed squish diagrams are in First layer & bed adhesion; the short version:

  1. Test modelPrint a single-layer large square (for example 150×150 mm) or a grid of 5 patches, one layer high (0.2 mm).
  2. What to look forLines slightly flattened and merged with no gaps, no ridges and no glossy transparent look. Rounded lines with gaps between = too high; ridges and a rough surface = too low.
  3. ApplyAdjust in 0.02 mm steps, with live adjustment during the print if available. Save per build plate.
  4. Re-checkAfter changing the nozzle, plate or filament type.

5 · Temperature tower

The nozzle temperature controls how easily plastic flows and how well layers bond. Procedure and figure are in temperature towers. Calibrate first for each new brand or color of filament, since pigments and additives change the optimum by 5–10 °C.

  • Pick the lowest temperature that gives strong layer bonding and clean bridges.
  • Apply to the filament profile as the default nozzle temperature (some profiles keep separate first-layer values).
  • Not a replacement for drying. Wet filament mimics bad temperature behavior. Dry first.

6 · Flow rate (extrusion multiplier)

🔧 Why it matters

The slicer assumes a perfectly round 1.75 mm filament and a particular density. Real filament varies in diameter by a few hundredths of a millimeter and in how it compresses when melted. A flow-ratio (extrusion multiplier) of 1.00 means "trust the numbers"; real filaments usually land between about 0.94 and 1.02. A 2 % error is invisible in walls but shows on top surfaces as ridges (too much) or gaps (too little), and changes dimensions.

Test and read it

Two common test models:

  • Single-wall cube / hollow box: print one perimeter, no infill, no top. Measure the wall thickness with calipers at several points (average them). Expected thickness = line width (for example 0.45 mm). New flow ratio = old × expected / measured.
  • Top-surface patch tests (coarse and fine): a set of flat squares, each at a slightly different flow ratio (for example −10 % to +10 % in 5 % steps and then ±2 % in 1 % steps). Pick the square with a smooth, closed top without ridges or gaps.

Apply: store the ratio per filament in its profile. Re-test when you change brand, color family (especially silk, matte or glitter), nozzle temperature by more than 10 °C, or nozzle diameter.

7 · Pressure advance / flow dynamics

This step fixes the lag between extruder motion and plastic flow (see pressure advance for the theory and Figure 5 there). It needs flow ratio set first, and a decent temperature.

  1. Test modelA tower or a pattern of lines with rising K (built into most slicers and firmware as a calibration command). Print at the speed and acceleration of your real profile.
  2. What to look forSharp, even corners, with uniform line width from start to end of each line. Bulges at the corners mean K is too low; thin or gapped corners mean K is too high.
  3. ApplyCompute K = start + height × step (as defined by the test), enter it in the filament profile and print the test again with it to confirm.
  4. Per filamentSilk, TPU and carbon-filled filaments differ widely.

On Bambu printers the Flow Dynamics Calibration does this for you, and in "auto" mode the printer can also run it at the start of a job (see below).

8 · Retraction tuning

When the toolhead travels between islands, the nozzle can leave strings, blobs and wisps because pressure keeps pushing plastic out. Retraction pulls the filament back a short distance before the move and pushes it forward after, so the melt pool recedes.

  • Test model: two or more thin towers spaced 30–60 mm apart (a retraction tower or a stringing test).
  • Order: Tune temperature first (hot plastic strings more), then retraction distance, then speed (30–60 mm/s is typical), and last "wipe" or "z-hop" if you still see scars.
  • Dry filament matters: Wet filament strings regardless of retraction.
  • Material hints: PETG strings easily and prefers slightly lower temperature and a little more retraction; TPU needs little or no retraction at slow speed.
  • X2D note: The auxiliary extruder with its longer path needs a much longer retraction than the main one; use the dedicated profile (see dual-nozzle printing).

9 · Max volumetric speed

Find the highest flow the hotend can melt cleanly with this filament. See the flow equation in volumetric flow.

  1. Test modelA tall, thin-walled ribbon or tower (slicer built-in or external) with the flow limit raised high, so the ramp is controlled by the test, printed in vase mode or one wall at the speed ramp from slow to fast.
  2. What to look forThe height where the surface changes from smooth to matte, then gappy; or where the extruder starts clicking.
  3. ApplyCompute flow (mm³/s) at that height from the ramp, multiply by 0.8–0.85, and set it as the filament's maximum volumetric speed.
  4. Dry filament first.

10 · Cooling and fan

Use an overhang test (a ladder of angles) and a bridge test; see supports, bridges & overhangs for the models and cooling for the strategy. Adjust fan in 10 % steps, and confirm layer bonding with a snap test. Record the settings for overhang and bridge fan separately.

11 · Dimensional accuracy

🔧 XY compensation, holes and shrinkage

Printed parts are rarely exactly the size you design. Three sources of error are separate and need separate fixes:

  1. Axis scaleMotors and belts moving a slightly wrong distance. Checked with a long printed rectangle (150–200 mm). Usually right in factory-calibrated printers.
  2. ShrinkagePlastic contracts as it cools. A percent of the whole part, mostly material dependent (below).
  3. Horizontal expansion / hole compensationThe extruded line is rounded and flows outward; outer dimensions grow a bit and holes shrink. Typically 0.05–0.2 mm, fixed per profile.
MaterialTypical shrinkageRemark
PLA~0.2–0.5 %Small; usually ignored.
PETG~0.3–0.8 %Low to moderate.
ABS / ASA~0.7–1.5 %Compensate for large or tight-fit parts.
PA (nylon)~1–2 %, more with moistureDry, hot chamber; compensate per brand.
PC~0.6–1 %Plus warp tendency.
TPU~0.5–1 %Soft parts: hard to measure.
CF/GF-filledOften half of the base materialFibers reduce shrinkage and warping.

Values vary strongly between brands, geometry and chamber temperature; measure your own. Most slicers have a per-filament shrinkage compensation percentage: set it as 100 × (designed − measured) / measured by testing a long dimension.

  • Test model: A calibration cube or a stepped block with holes and pegs of known dimensions, printed with your usual profile. Measure with calipers at three places each and average. Measure after cooling to room temperature.
  • Apply: Use shrinkage compensation for overall scale (affecting X, Y and Z), XY hole compensation and contour compensation for small shifts, and an offset for tight-fit parts.
  • Elephant foot: a separate first-layer effect; see first layer.

12 · Input shaping and ringing

Once the machine is mechanically tight, input shaping cancels the dominant vibration of the frame so that you can use higher accelerations. Theory is in ringing & input shaping. Calibration identifies the resonance frequencies of each axis using an accelerometer, microphone or a printed pattern.

  1. Test modelA tall block with sharp corners and text or an arrowed ringing tower, printed with high acceleration.
  2. What to look forEcho-like ripples after corners and text. Measure the spacing of ripples and the print speed to estimate the frequency: f = speed / ripple distance.
  3. ApplyEnter the shaper type and frequency per axis (or run the automatic measurement), then raise acceleration gradually and recheck.
  4. Re-doafter changing the toolhead mass, belt tension or when ringing returns.

A worked example

Suppose you open a new roll of a matte PLA from a brand you have not used before, on a printer whose frame you already checked last month. A sensible session takes about two hours of mostly unattended printing:

  1. Dry and loadDry the spool as the manufacturer suggests and load it. Clean the plate with isopropyl alcohol or the manufacturer's recommended method.
  2. Temperature towerPrint a tower from 190 to 230 °C in 5 °C steps. The bridges are cleanest at 205–210 °C and the layers are strongest at 210 °C, so you pick 210 °C.
  3. Flow ratePrint a single-wall box at that temperature. The walls measure 0.43 mm where the line width should be 0.45 mm. The new flow ratio is 0.98 × 0.45 / 0.43, about 1.03.
  4. Pressure advanceRun the K tower at your normal wall speed and pick the cleanest corner, then confirm with a small cube.
  5. Max volumetric speedPrint the flow ribbon; the surface turns matte at about 18 mm³/s, so you set 15 mm³/s with a margin.
  6. Verify and logPrint one real part with the new profile, compare the surface with the pictures in this page and write all values into the log.

Numbers here are only an example to show the arithmetic. Your values will be different; what matters is the method: change one variable, measure, apply, and write it down.

Common mistakes

Tuning with wet filament; calibrating at a speed you never use; changing temperature after flow and pressure advance; relying on a single measurement instead of an average of three; and forgetting that a dirty nozzle or plate can ruin any test. If a result looks strange, repeat the test once before changing a setting.

Let it calibrate, then verify

Automatic calibration does not replace judgment. After any auto routine, print one real part and compare it with the images in this playbook: smooth top, sharp corners, correct dimensions.

Per-filament profile management

Every result in this playbook belongs to a specific filament + nozzle + printer combination. Treat the profile as the "database record" for that spool type.

  • Name with context. For example "Brand PLA Matte 0.4 nozzle" rather than "Generic PLA copy 3".
  • Store one result per variable. A different color of the same brand may need a re-check of flow and temperature, especially silk, matte, glitter and translucent.
  • Back up exported profiles and keep the calibration log with them.
  • Do not stack fixes. If a profile has retraction, flow and pressure advance all adjusted to hide the same problem (for example wet filament), start again from a clean profile with dry filament.

Key takeaways

  • Order matters: hardware, first layer, plastic behavior, then speed and motion.
  • A loose or out-of-square machine cannot be tuned in software; check it first.
  • Change one variable at a time, and record every result in the log.
  • Dry filament before any test; moisture mimics many calibration problems.
  • Flow ratio, pressure advance and max volumetric speed are filament-specific; temperature changes affect all three.
  • Holes print small and parts shrink: use per-profile compensation instead of editing every model.
  • Automatic calibration saves time, but verify with a real print.
