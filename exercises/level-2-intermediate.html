Part 7 · Practice & exercises

Level 2 · Intermediate

From "it prints" to "it prints well, and I can prove why". Fifteen experiments in calibration, design and assembly where you collect data, change one variable at a time and make engineering decisions.

Intermediate⏱ about 35 hours in total15 exercises

What you should be able to do first

Level 2 builds on Level 1. You should be comfortable loading filament, reading the first layer, slicing a model and measuring with calipers. You do not need advanced CAD skills, but you will touch a parametric tool (OpenSCAD, Fusion, FreeCAD or Onshape) in exercises L2-7 and L2-14.

The core idea of this level is the controlled experiment: you change one setting, keep everything else fixed, and measure the effect. Experiments in 3D printing are noisy, so you also learn to repeat a test, record variation and decide when a difference is real.

Exercise 1 — Temperature tower

⏱ 2 h (incl. ~1 h print)
Goal

Determine the best nozzle temperature for a specific spool using a scored temperature tower, and justify the result with a table and a chart. Unlike L1-11 you now score every tier systematically and cross-check with a mechanical test.

You will need
  • temp_tower.scad (7 tiers; set start_temp and step to cover the label's range) or the built-in calibration tower of your slicer
  • One dry spool of the filament you want to tune (PLA, PETG, ABS …)
  • A magnifier or macro photo, a ruler
Steps
  1. Choose the range. Use the label range from L1-13. Seven tiers of 5 °C cover a 30 °C span. For PETG typical start values are 250 → 220 °C; for ABS 260 → 230 °C. Hedge: always stay inside your hotend's limits.
  2. Print the tower with the fan setting you normally use for this material and with the tier changes programmed in the slicer. Do not touch the cooling or speed during the print.
  3. Score each tier from 1 to 5 for layer bonding (try to snap the tier: higher effort is better), stringing, bridge underside, overhang underside, surface finish and detail sharpness.
  4. Chart the average per tier as in Figure 2. Mark the peak and the width of the plateau.
  5. Validate. Print one useful part (e.g. the cable clip) at the winner and at the winner minus 10 °C and compare breaking by hand.
  6. Save a filament preset with your chosen temperature and name it with brand and color.
Success criteria
  • A scored table for all tiers and a chart like Figure 2
  • A chosen temperature plus a plateau (e.g. "205–210 °C works, 207 °C chosen")
  • A saved filament preset in your slicer with the new value
Hint

A tower with tiers of 5 °C steps cannot show differences smaller than 5 °C. If two neighboring tiers look the same, pick the middle of the plateau, not the edge. Choose slightly hotter if you print fast or with a large nozzle (more melting needed) and slightly cooler for small detailed parts.

Exercise 2 — Retraction test

⏱ 2.5 h
Goal

Find the retraction length and speed that stop stringing and blobs for your extruder type without causing clogs or under-extrusion. Retraction is a short reverse move of the filament before the nozzle travels across empty space.

You will need
  • retraction_test.scad (four 40 mm towers) or any retraction test model
  • Filament at the temperature found in L2-1, dry
  • Your slicer's retraction settings (length, speed; optional wipe and z-hop)
Steps
  1. Note your starting values. Direct-drive extruders typically work with about 0.5–2 mm retraction at 25–45 mm/s; Bowden setups often need 3–7 mm. Write down both your starting length and speed.
  2. Print the towers with length 0 mm (retraction off). This is your "worst case" baseline.
  3. Change only the length in steps (e.g. 0.4, 0.8, 1.2, 1.6 mm). Use the slicer's retraction tower option if it has one, or print four separate towers.
  4. Compare the strings between towers under a good light and rank them 0 (none) to 5 (heavy).
  5. Test the speed at the best length: 20, 35 and 50 mm/s. Too fast can grind filament; too slow lets pressure leak out.
  6. Check for side effects: gaps after travel (under-extrusion) or grinding noise. Pick the smallest retraction that gives clean towers.
X2D note

The main (left) nozzle uses a direct-drive extruder and needs little retraction. The auxiliary (right) nozzle is fed through a PTFE tube from the rear extruder and needs a much longer retraction and a larger flow-dynamics value. Tune the two nozzles separately and do not copy values from one to the other. Details: Dual-nozzle printing.

Success criteria
  • A table of stringing score vs retraction length (and speed)
  • A chosen length and speed with no visible strings and no gaps after travel
  • Settings saved in a filament or process profile
Hint

Wet filament strings badly no matter what retraction you use. If increasing retraction never improves things, dry the filament and repeat the baseline (exercise L2-8). A nozzle that is too hot gives the same symptom, so check L2-1 first.

Worked solution / what good looks like
Retraction (direct drive, 35 mm/s)Stringing (0–5)Comment
0 mm5Heavy strings and blobs
0.4 mm3Fine strings
0.8 mm1Almost clean
1.2 mm0Clean
1.6 mm0Clean, small gap after travel

Choose 1.2 mm: the smallest value with score 0 and no side effects. Longer retraction does not make strings better once they are gone but increases wear and the chance of heat creep clogs. Go deeper in Calibration playbook.

Exercise 3 — Flow calibration cube

⏱ 1.5 h
Goal

Calibrate the flow ratio (extrusion multiplier) by measuring the real wall thickness of a thin-walled box and correcting the setting by the ratio of expected to measured thickness.

You will need
  • flow_cube.scad: a 20 mm open box with 0.84 mm walls (2 × 0.42 mm line width). Slice with 2 walls, 0 % infill, no top layers, a bottom of 4–5 layers.
  • Calipers (0.01 mm), ideally a micrometer for thin walls
  • Fine sandpaper to deburr the cut edge if needed
Steps
  1. Confirm the line width. Use the slicer's outer-wall line width (typically 0.42 mm for a 0.4 mm nozzle). The expected wall is the number of walls times the line width: 2 × 0.42 = 0.84 mm.
  2. Print the cube at the normal speed and temperature from L2-1. Write down the current flow ratio.
  3. Measure the wall with a micrometer or calipers at 8 points (two heights on each of the four walls), staying away from corners. Average them.
  4. Compute the new flow ratio with the formula in Figure 4 and enter it in the filament profile.
  5. Reprint and confirm. The measured wall should now be within ±0.03 mm of the target.
  6. Check a solid surface. Print a flat top surface; if the surface is rough (too much) or has gaps (too little), refine the value by 0.01–0.02 steps.
Success criteria
  • 8 measurements recorded and averaged
  • A calculated new flow ratio, applied and tested with a second print
  • Final wall thickness within ±0.03 mm of expected
Hint

Measurement error is huge compared to the numbers here: calipers can mislead on a 0.84 mm wall because of the jaw angle and compressed plastic. If you do not have a micrometer, measure many points and use the average. Also make sure the actual line width in the slicer equals the number you use in the formula.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Example: current flow 0.98; measured mean 0.89 mm vs expected 0.84 mm. New flow = 0.98 × 0.84 / 0.89 = 0.925, rounded to 0.92. After reprinting the mean wall is 0.85 mm. Typical flow ratios for PLA lie between 0.92 and 1.00; much lower or higher values often point to a wrong filament diameter setting, a partially clogged nozzle or the wrong line width. Bambu Studio's automatic flow calibration performs the same measurement with a scanned pattern; the manual method teaches the principle. See Bambu Studio settings & profiles.

Exercise 4 — Bridging test

⏱ 2 h
Goal

Measure how far your printer can bridge (print in mid-air between two supports) at your current settings, and improve it by changing speed, fan and flow for bridges only.

You will need
  • bridge_test.scad: five bridges with spans of 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 mm
  • A ruler and a feeler gauge or paper strips for sag measurement, a camera
  • PLA (bridges are easiest with PLA and strong part cooling)
Steps
  1. Print the test with your normal settings and note the bridge speed, bridge fan and bridge flow values from the profile.
  2. Inspect the underside of each bridge and score 0–3: 3 = flat and clean, 2 = slightly loose strands, 1 = visible sag, 0 = collapsed.
  3. Measure the sag at the center of the longest working bridge: slide a feeler strip or card under it until it just touches.
  4. Change one thing: fan to 100 % for bridges, then bridge speed to 30 mm/s, then bridge flow to 0.9. Reprint the test after each change (use the slicer's bridge options in the process profile).
  5. Plot maximum acceptable span (score ≥ 2) against each setting.
Success criteria
  • Five bridges tested at baseline and three variants
  • Sag or score recorded for each span and setting
  • A stated maximum bridge span for your printer and a recommended bridge setting
Hint

Orientation of the bridge direction matters: the first bridge line is anchored at both ends, the later ones lie next to it. If your slicer offers "bridge direction" or "thick bridges", test them separately. Cooling is the single biggest factor, so check that the part-cooling fan duct is clean and the fan actually runs at the commanded speed.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Typical PLA result: with default settings, 10–20 mm bridges are flat, 30 mm sags 0.5 mm and 40–50 mm look rough. With 100 % fan, 35 mm/s and flow 0.9, the 30 mm bridge becomes clean and the 40 mm bridge is acceptable. Bridges beyond about 50 mm usually need supports or a design change (arches, chamfers). In practice you design bridges below 20–30 mm for functional parts. More methods in Supports, bridges & overhangs.

Exercise 5 — Overhang test

⏱ 2 h
Goal

Find the steepest overhang angle your printer produces cleanly without support, then turn the result into a design rule you can apply in CAD (for example "never exceed 50° from vertical for visible surfaces").

You will need
  • overhang_ladder.scad with ramps from 30° to 80° measured from vertical
  • PLA at the temperature from L2-1, part cooling at 100 % for overhangs
  • A camera or phone for macro photos of the undersides
Steps
  1. Print the ladder without supports. Keep the layer height at 0.2 mm; a thinner layer height gives more overlap and slightly steeper possible angles.
  2. Photograph the underside of each ramp in the same light, from the same distance.
  3. Score each angle: 3 = smooth, 2 = slightly rough but functional, 1 = ragged or drooping, 0 = failed. Write the largest angle with score ≥ 2 as your "clean limit" and the largest with score 1 as your "absolute limit".
  4. Repeat with a different setting: reduce layer height to 0.12 mm, or lower the print speed on overhangs to 50 %. See if the limit shifts by 5–10°.
  5. Write a one-line design rule for yourself, such as "use 45° chamfers, no flat roofs".
Success criteria
  • All angles photographed and scored for two settings
  • A numeric clean limit and absolute limit
  • A design rule written in your notes
Hint

Be careful with what "angle" means. In this model the angle is measured from vertical: 0° is a straight wall and 90° would be a flat ceiling. Some slicers and models quote angles from the horizontal. Always check the model's description before comparing numbers with others.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Typical PLA with strong cooling: 30° and 45° are clean, 60° has visible stair-stepping but good strength, 70° starts to droop and 80° fails without support. The geometry explains it: at angle θ from vertical, each layer of thickness h is offset sideways by h·tan θ. For h = 0.2 mm and θ = 45° the offset is 0.2 mm, about half a line width, so every layer is half supported. At 70° the offset is 0.55 mm, more than the line width of 0.42 mm, so the new line is largely in the air. More in Design principles and Supports, bridges & overhangs.

Exercise 6 — Tolerance coupon

⏱ 2 h
Goal

Measure the clearance your printer needs for sliding fits, tight fits and press fits, and record a personal "tolerance table" you will use in every later design. Printed holes are usually slightly smaller and printed pegs slightly larger than designed.

You will need
  • tolerance_gauge.scad: set part="both" or print "plate" and "pegs" separately; clearances from 0 to 0.5 mm in 8 mm pegs
  • Calipers; a set of drill bits or pin gauges (optional)
  • Your most-used material (PLA or PETG)
Steps
  1. Print the plate and the pegs in the same orientation and with the same settings you will use for real parts.
  2. Measure the actual diameter of each peg and each hole with calipers (hole: use the inside jaws or the smallest drill that passes).
  3. Tabulate designed vs measured diameters and compute the offset for holes and pegs separately.
  4. Test the fit by hand: for every hole try the matching peg (and the neighbors). Record "falls through", "slides", "snug", "needs force", "stuck".
  5. Write your tolerance table: the clearance you will add for each fit class and a correction for small holes (usually bigger than for large holes).
Success criteria
  • Table of designed vs measured size for at least 5 pegs and 5 holes
  • A fit-class table with your recommended clearances
  • The table saved where you will find it when you start a new design
Hint

Do not trust the first measurement after printing: the plastic is warm and still shrinking a little. Wait until the part is at room temperature. Measure holes in two perpendicular directions: elephant-foot and first-layer squish can make holes slightly oval.

Exercise 7 — Design a parametric box in CAD

⏱ 3 h
Goal

Model a box with a lid whose dimensions come from a small set of named parameters, so that changing one number rebuilds the whole part. Parametric thinking is the biggest productivity step in CAD.

You will need
  • Any parametric tool: Fusion (user parameters), FreeCAD (spreadsheet or Part Design), Onshape (variables) or OpenSCAD
  • parametric_box.scad as a reference, to read after you tried yourself
  • The tolerance table from L2-6
Steps
  1. List the parameters first (Figure 8). Decide units (mm) and give each a clear name.
  2. Model the base using only parameters: outer rounded rectangle, subtract the inner cavity, keep a floor of 4 layers (0.8 mm at 0.2 mm layer height) or more.
  3. Model the lid with a lip that enters the base: lip outer size = inner size − 2 × lid_gap.
  4. Test the parametric behavior: change inner_l to 90 and inner_h to 20. Everything must update without errors. Fix anything that breaks.
  5. Print the box at a small size for speed (e.g. 40×30×20) and check that the lid fits as designed.
  6. Document: add a comment next to each parameter with its meaning and valid range.
Success criteria
  • All dimensions driven by parameters; at least two parameter sets rebuild correctly
  • Printed lid fits with the intended clearance
  • The file exports valid STL or 3MF with no non-manifold warnings
Hint

If the model breaks at a different size, you have most likely typed a number instead of using an expression, or the corner radius is bigger than half the wall-to-wall distance. Add a guard such as corner_r = min(corner_r, inner_w/2) in OpenSCAD or a constraint in the sketch.

Worked solution / what good looks like

In OpenSCAD the base is a difference() of two rounded boxes: the outer one of size inner + 2*wall and the inner one moved up by the floor thickness. The lid is a rounded plate of outer size plus a lip, rotated 180° so that its flat face lies on the bed. In a graphical CAD tool the same logic is a sketch with driven dimensions and a user-parameter table. The reference file shows both parts and a "preview" mode. Find the workflow in Parametric CAD workflow and a CAD overview in CAD & modeling software.

Exercise 8 — Filament drying experiment

⏱ 10 h (mostly waiting)
Goal

Prove for yourself how much moisture matters. You will print the same test with a wet and a dried section of one spool, and quantify the difference with a score and a weight measurement.

You will need
  • A hygroscopic filament: PETG, nylon, TPU or PVA show it fastest; PLA shows it more slowly. Use a spool that has been open on a shelf for weeks, or deliberately expose a short length to humid air for a day
  • A filament dryer or the AMS 2 Pro drying function, a kitchen scale with 0.1 g resolution (or better)
  • retraction_test.scad as the test print, plus a macro camera
Steps
  1. Weigh the spool (with a known empty spool weight, or use the same spool before and after) and note ambient humidity if you have a hygrometer.
  2. Print the wet test. Listen: popping or crackling at the nozzle and steam-like wisps are signs of moisture. Photograph the strings and surface.
  3. Dry the spool at the recommended temperature for 4–8 hours (see Drying & storage). Weigh again right after drying while the spool is still warm and again when it has cooled.
  4. Print the dry test with identical settings.
  5. Compare: stringing score (0–5), surface finish, popping, layer adhesion by hand snap. Compute the weight loss in percent.
  6. Store the spool properly afterwards (sealed container with desiccant) and write down the time until symptoms come back, if you test that later.
Safety

Never dry above the recommended temperature and never leave a heating device unattended near flammable materials. Use a dedicated filament dryer or the AMS 2 Pro with its documented limits.

Success criteria
  • Two prints with photos and scores, with identical settings
  • Weight before and after drying, plus the calculated loss in percent
  • A written rule for how you will store this material in the future
Hint

The weight change for a 1 kg spool is small: 0.1–0.5 % is only 1–5 g. A scale with 0.1 g resolution is needed. To make the effect clearer, weigh a short loose piece (e.g. 20 g of filament) instead of the whole spool, or let a small sample absorb moisture for a day before drying.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Example for PETG: wet print score 4 (heavy strings, rough surface, popping), dry print score 1; sample weight dropped from 20.00 g to 19.92 g (0.4 %) after 6 h at 65 °C. Interpretation: even sub-percent moisture is visible. PLA may show almost no change after one week in a dry room, whereas nylon can change visibly in hours. Typical symptoms and a summary of drying temperatures are in Drying & storage and AMS 2 Pro deep dive.

Exercise 9 — PETG vs PLA bend test

⏱ 3 h
Goal

Compare how two materials behave under a repeatable three-point bend test and learn to separate stiffness (how much it bends), strength (when it breaks) and toughness (how much energy it absorbs). This is a simple home test, not a standardized material test.

You will need
  • Two test bars per material (e.g. 100 × 10 × 4 mm), printed flat with identical settings
  • A test rig: two supports 80 mm apart (two bolts or a jig) and a way to load the center: a bucket hung with a loop, filled gradually with water or sand
  • A kitchen scale, calipers, a ruler to measure deflection
Steps
  1. Print the bars flat, 4 walls, 100 % infill (or the real infill you want to compare), same layer height. Use dry filament and the temperatures from L2-1.
  2. Measure the real width and thickness of each bar with calipers. Thin bars are much more sensitive to dimension errors: stiffness scales with thickness cubed.
  3. Mount the bar on the 80 mm supports and apply the load gradually: add 200 g at a time, wait 10 s and measure deflection at the center.
  4. Continue until failure or until the bar slips off the supports. Wear eye protection; plastic pieces can fly.
  5. Record the load at 2 mm deflection (a stiffness measure), the maximum load, and whether the break was clean (brittle) or the bar bent permanently (ductile).
  6. Test at least two bars of each material and compare averages.
Safety

Wear safety glasses, keep hands and feet out of the area below the load and do not stand over the bar when it fails.

Success criteria
  • Load–deflection table or sketch for each material
  • Stiffness, maximum load and failure mode recorded
  • A short conclusion about when to choose each material
Hint

For a rectangular bar of width b and thickness t on a span L, the load at a given deflection is proportional to b·t³/L³ and to the material's modulus. If your bars differ in thickness by only 0.1 mm (2.5 % of 4 mm), the stiffness differs by about 7.5 %, more than many material differences. That is why you measure the real dimensions.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Typical qualitative result: PLA bars carry a higher load at 2 mm deflection, then snap suddenly with a clean fracture; PETG bars deflect further, whiten at the bend and may not break at all within the rig's range. So PLA is the better choice for rigid, unloaded or lightly loaded parts; PETG for parts that must flex, take impacts or bear loads at a hinge. The quantitative values depend heavily on brand, color, moisture and print settings, so avoid quoting a number as "the" PLA value. See Filament overview and PLA, PETG & everyday plastics.

Exercise 10 — Support settings comparison

⏱ 3 h
Goal

Compare normal (grid), tree and no supports on an overhanging part and measure print time, material and surface quality, plus how well each variant releases. You decide which strategy is best for which geometry.

You will need
  • A test model with an arch, an overhanging shelf and a bridge, for example a 40 mm tall arch with a 30 mm bridge or any support benchmark. You can build one in 10 minutes in a CAD tool.
  • Pliers, a deburring tool and your slicer (Bambu Studio offers normal and tree supports, with auto and manual placement)
Steps
  1. Slice the model three times with the same settings and only the support type changed: normal, tree, none. Record the estimated print time and filament use from the slicer.
  2. Print all three variants and weigh them after removing the supports.
  3. Rate the result: surface quality of the supported areas (0–5), how hard the supports were to remove (0–5), any damage to thin features.
  4. Test the interface settings on normal supports: Z-gap (top distance) 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 mm. Smaller gap = better surface but harder removal.
  5. Summarize in a table and write one recommended rule for yourself.
Success criteria
  • Time and material figures for all variants
  • Quality and removal ratings with photos
  • A recommendation: which support type for which geometry
Hint

Weigh the part before and after removing the supports; the difference is the support waste. A slicer estimate is usually close but includes purge or wipe material on multi-material jobs, so compare like with like. Tree supports are not automatically better; they can fail on large flat undersides.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Exercise 11 — Seam placement

⏱ 2 h
Goal

Understand where the slicer starts and ends each outer loop (the seam) and compare four seam strategies on the same cylinder: nearest, aligned, back (rear) and random. Choose the best for visible parts.

You will need
  • A 30 mm tall, 25 mm diameter cylinder with a small notch (a vertical groove 1 mm wide) and a box shape
  • Your slicer with the seam position setting (Bambu Studio: Quality → Seam position; also scarf seam if available)
Steps
  1. Slice and print the cylinder four times (or four cylinders on one plate) with each seam option.
  2. Inspect in raking light. Photograph the seam from the side and compare how visible it is.
  3. Try the notch: with "aligned" seam, set the seam position to hide in the groove or a corner of the model and check again.
  4. Paint the seams on a sharp part: use seam painting in the slicer to place them in a corner of a box and print.
  5. Decide your default and write down when you choose another (round vs sharp shapes, functional vs display parts).
Success criteria
  • Photos of four seam types on the same shape
  • A ranking and a reasoned default
  • One seam hidden in a corner or groove
Hint

Seams are the most visible on smooth curves and the least visible on sharp corners and textured surfaces. A tiny retraction or pressure advance mismatch makes seams worse, so if the seam bulges or has a gap, revisit the pressure advance (L3-1) and flow (L2-3).

Worked solution / what good looks like

Typical result: aligned gives a neat vertical line you can hide; nearest gives a messy diagonal pattern; random hides the seam as texture but is not good for sealing surfaces; back is excellent for display parts viewed from the front. On a box, the best seam is on a vertical corner at the back. For curved parts a scarf seam (where the slicer ramps the start and end of the loop) reduces the visibility further. See Slicer settings explained.

Exercise 12 — Fillet vs chamfer

⏱ 3 h
Goal

Test how an inside corner treatment (sharp, 45° chamfer or rounded fillet) changes the load a bracket can carry, and how an outside edge treatment changes printability and first-layer quality. Sharp inside corners concentrate stress; generous radii spread it.

You will need
  • Three L-shaped brackets with a 20 mm leg, 5 mm thickness and 15 mm width, differing only in the inside corner: sharp, 3 mm chamfer, 5 mm fillet. Make five of each if you want statistics
  • A way to apply load to the end of one leg while the other is clamped (a vise or a bolted plate) and a bucket with water, as in L2-9
Steps
  1. Print all brackets lying on their side so that layers run along the leg (the load will act across layers at the corner, the usual worst case). Use the same settings and walls for all.
  2. Load each bracket in the same way and record the load at failure. Increase gradually; avoid shocks.
  3. Note where each bracket breaks. Sharp corners usually break exactly at the inside corner; fillets may break a little away from it or at a layer boundary.
  4. Second question: print a chamfer and a fillet on the bottom outside edge of a small block. Which one has the cleaner first layer (elephant foot)?
  5. Write a rule for yourself about radii for inside and outside edges.
Success criteria
  • Failure loads for the three corner types (average of at least 2 each)
  • Photos of the fracture locations
  • A design rule: minimum inside radius and when to use a chamfer instead
Hint

Printed parts are weakest between layers. If the corner is oriented so the layers peel apart under load, the corner shape matters less than the orientation (see L2-13). Make sure only the corner geometry changes between the samples; even different seam positions can shift results.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Typical result: sharp corner fails first; the chamfer carries noticeably more; the fillet carries the most (relative improvements of tens of percent are common, but vary widely). For outside bottom edges, a 0.5–1 mm chamfer avoids elephant foot and eases removal, while a fillet there creates tiny overhangs at the first layers. Rule of thumb: inside radius ≥ wall thickness, outside bottom edge chamfer instead of fillet. Read Strength & orientation.

Exercise 13 — Orientation strength test

⏱ 3 h
Goal

Measure the anisotropy of an FDM part: print identical bars flat, on edge and standing upright, and compare how much load each carries. This is the single most useful strength lesson in 3D printing.

You will need
  • Three or more bars per orientation, 80 × 10 × 5 mm, sliced with identical settings; the rig from L2-9 or any simple way to load them to failure
  • Optionally a hook and a bucket for tension tests of dog-bone shapes
Steps
  1. Print the bars in three orientations (flat, on edge, upright). Upright bars are tall and thin: add a brim for stability.
  2. Test each bar in bending (or, if you can, tension) and record the load at failure.
  3. Inspect the breaks: flat and on-edge bars should break across several layers; upright bars delaminate along one layer.
  4. Calculate the ratio upright : flat. Express it in percent of the strongest orientation.
  5. Apply: take a real part you printed in the past and decide whether its orientation matched the load direction.
Success criteria
  • At least 2 bars per orientation tested to failure
  • A table of failure loads and a ratio upright:flat
  • A rule: "orient layers so the main load runs along them, never across them"
Hint

A flat bar in 3-point bending has its layers parallel to the neutral axis and its outer layers in tension along the filament direction. A bar standing upright has its bottom-side layers pulled apart. The load direction relative to the layers matters, not the part's shape alone.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Typical PLA outcome: on-edge and flat bars carry similar high loads; the upright bar fails at a much lower load (often only a third to a half). Annealed or hot-chamber printed parts (ABS, ASA, nylon at chamber temperature) lose less strength between layers, which is a reason to use the X2D's heated chamber for functional parts. A higher nozzle temperature, slower speed and less cooling also improve layer adhesion. See Strength & orientation and Engineering materials & chamber.

Level 2 · Medium

Exercise 14 — Design with a heat-set insert

⏱ 3 h
Goal

Design and install a threaded brass insert (for example M3 × 5.7 mm long) into a printed boss and test its pull-out strength. Heat-set inserts give plastic parts strong, reusable metal threads.

You will need
  • Brass heat-set inserts M3 (typical outer diameter about 4.6 mm, length about 5.7 mm; check your supplier's datasheet for the recommended hole size)
  • A soldering iron with a tapered insert tip at 200–230 °C for PLA/PETG (lower for PLA than for ABS/PC; follow supplier guidance)
  • M3 screws, a luggage scale or bucket for pull-out tests
  • The tolerance table from L2-6, a CAD tool and snap_fit_test.scad or parametric_box.scad as a source of bosses
Steps
  1. Read the supplier's datasheet for the recommended hole diameter and depth. Design the hole in CAD as a parameter and add 1–2 mm of depth below the insert.
  2. Design the boss with a wall thickness of at least 1.5× the insert's outer diameter's wall (e.g. outer Ø 8–9 mm for an M3 insert) so that the plastic does not split.
  3. Print three samples with different hole diameters (e.g. 4.0, 4.2 and 4.4 mm) with the hole axis vertical so it is round.
  4. Install the inserts at the right temperature, pressing straight, slowly, until flush. Do not push below the surface. Let the plastic cool before loading.
  5. Pull-out test: screw an M3 into the insert and hang a load or pull with a scale until it fails. Compare to an M3 screw tapped directly into plastic.
  6. Record the best hole diameter in your tolerance table.
Safety

The iron tip and the insert are hot enough to burn skin and to emit fumes. Work with ventilation, use a stand and hold the part with pliers or a fixture.

Success criteria
  • Inserts installed straight and flush in three hole sizes
  • Pull-out values recorded and compared with a screw in plastic
  • A recommended hole diameter for your printer and material
Hint

If plastic bulges around the insert, you pushed too fast or the iron is too hot; if the insert pulls out easily, the hole was too large or the iron too cold. The correct condition produces a small ring of molten plastic that you can scrape off.

Worked solution / what good looks like

With an insert of 4.6 mm outer diameter, a 4.2 mm hole worked best in PETG; 4.0 mm split the boss and 4.4 mm held about half the load. A well-installed M3 insert carries far more load than a self-tapping screw and survives repeated assembly; the exact pull-out force depends on material and depth, so use your own test. Joint and fastener details: Joints & fasteners.

Exercise 15 — Two-color print

⏱ 2 h
Goal

Print a two-color object (a name plate or a logo tile) using color changes, compare two methods (a mid-print filament swap and a multi-filament system) and measure the waste each produces.

You will need
  • text_label_test.scad or your own tile with raised text
  • Two filaments of the same type and similar temperatures, in high-contrast colors
  • AMS or an AMS 2 Pro on the X2D (or a manual pause-and-swap if you have no multi-filament system)
Steps
  1. Prepare the model so that the text is a separate body or sits above a fixed layer height (the text must start exactly at a layer boundary).
  2. Method 1: insert a pause or color-change command at that layer in the slicer and swap the filament by hand. Print and check the transition line.
  3. Method 2: assign the two colors to two AMS slots and print with automatic changes. Read the purge volume and the estimated flush waste in the slicer preview.
  4. Compare the two prints: color separation, surface at the color boundary, time, extra waste, number of manual interventions.
  5. Try a lighter-to-darker order: changing from a dark to a light filament needs more flushing than light to dark. Test the effect on waste with the slicer's flush settings.
X2D note

On the X2D, put the colors that must flow through the main nozzle in the AMS 2 Pro and keep the combination within the supported nozzle limits (the main nozzle handles every colored change; the auxiliary nozzle is designed for specific use such as a second material). Check Dual-nozzle printing and Multi-color & multi-material before you assign materials.

Success criteria
  • One successful two-color print by each method (or one method and a documented attempt)
  • Waste in grams for the automatic method
  • Notes on which method you prefer for which job
Hint

Flat-colored text on a base is much more forgiving than a color change on a vertical wall. Use a high contrast pair (white on black) for your first try, and weigh the purge tower or the flushed material (some slicers list it in the preview).

Worked solution / what good looks like

Example: 60 × 25 mm nameplate. Method 1: one swap, a 0.4 g strand of waste, clean transition, 1 manual step. Method 2: two changes per layer in the text region, 6.5 g flushed, no manual step. For a single plate the manual method wins; for many plates or many colors automation wins. Ways to reduce waste are discussed in exercise L3-8.

Key takeaways

  • A good experiment changes one variable, controls the rest, repeats and records. A beautiful single print proves nothing.
  • Calibrate in order: first layer, temperature, flow, retraction, then bridges, overhangs and tolerances.
  • Keep a personal tolerance table and design rules; they make every later design faster.
  • Orientation matters more than infill for strength: layers are the weak direction.
  • Moisture, temperature and cooling explain most of the "mystery" defects; measure them before changing slicer magic.
  • Support is a design decision: chamfer or reorient first, add support last.
