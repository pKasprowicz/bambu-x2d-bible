Part 7 · Practice & exercises

Level 1 · Beginner

Fifteen small, doable projects that turn "I have a printer" into "I understand what my printer is doing". Each one has a goal, a materials list, steps, success criteria, a hint and a worked solution.

Beginner⏱ about 20 hours in total15 exercises

How this level works

Level 1 assumes only that you own (or can use) a working FDM printer and a spool of PLA. You do not need CAD skills, special filament or expensive tools. Every exercise ends with success criteria you can tick off, and the tick boxes at the bottom of each exercise are saved in your browser, so your progress bar above fills up as you go. Nothing is uploaded anywhere.

Work in order if you are brand new. If you already have a few prints behind you, skim the goals and jump to the ones that teach something you have not tried. Exercises that involve printing say so in the time estimate: a "⏱ 1 h" badge means about an hour of your attention plus any printing time listed under You will need.

Your starter toolkit

Most exercises use the same few items. Collect them once and keep them next to the printer. Prices are not listed on purpose: a basic version of everything here is inexpensive, and you do not need professional-grade tools at Level 1.

🔧 Must-have

  • Digital calipers (0.01 mm resolution)
  • Flush cutters
  • Scraper or spatula (plastic or thin metal)
  • Isopropyl alcohol (90 % or more) and a lint-free cloth
  • Notebook or phone for photos and notes

👍 Nice-to-have

  • Kitchen scale (0.1 g or better)
  • Tweezers and needle-nose pliers
  • Deburring tool or hobby knife
  • Small steel ruler
  • A 2 mm and a 1.5 mm hex key for your printer

🧵 Consumables

  • 1 spool of PLA, any common color (dry!)
  • Optional: a second color for experiments
  • Painter's tape (for marking and hold-down)
  • A small container for failed prints and brims
Safety first

The nozzle runs at roughly 200 °C and the heated bed at 55–60 °C even for PLA. Never touch either while the printer is on or still cooling, keep hair and sleeves out of moving parts, and never leave a printer running unattended in a room where you sleep. Exercise L1-14 turns this into a proper audit; read Safety & workspace before you start.

Exercise 1 — First print checklist

⏱ 1.5 h (incl. ~40 min print)
Goal

Build a repeatable pre-flight routine and use it to complete one successful, unattended-quality print. Good habits formed now prevent most failed prints later.

You will need
  • Your printer, set up and connected (see Your first print)
  • 1 spool of dry PLA and the printer's default PLA profile
  • Isopropyl alcohol and a lint-free cloth
  • A simple model: the 20 mm calibration cube or any small model from your printer's built-in library
Steps
  1. Walk the checklist below once, slowly. Tick each item physically on the printer, not just in your head.
  2. Clean the build plate with alcohol and let it dry. From now on, avoid touching the printing surface with bare fingers: skin oil is the number-one cause of first-layer failures.
  3. Run the printer's start-up calibration (automatic bed leveling or similar) if the printer offers it.
  4. Slice the model using the default profile for your printer and PLA. Check the estimated time and make sure the model sits flat on the plate.
  5. Start the print and stay for the first three layers. This is where almost all failures begin. Watch for lines that curl, skip or do not stick.
  6. Let the plate cool (or remove the flex plate and flex it gently) before removing the model. Never pry hard with a tool aimed at your hand.
  7. Write down what you did and how it looked in your journal.
  • Printer is on a stable, level surface with free space around it
  • All shipping locks, foam and tape are removed
  • Build plate is clean and dry; correct plate is selected in the slicer
  • Filament moves freely off the spool (no tangles, spool turns easily)
  • Nozzle is clean (no old plastic hanging from it)
  • Slicer profile matches printer, nozzle size and material
  • Model sits flat on the plate and fits inside the build volume
  • You know where the power switch or emergency stop is
Success criteria
  • The print finished without you intervening
  • The model came off the plate in one piece and with a flat bottom
  • Your journal has an entry with material, temperatures and a note on quality
Hint

If the first layer lifts at the corners or the line looks thin and does not stick, do not keep watching a failing print. Stop it, wipe the plate with alcohol, rerun the printer's calibration and try again. If it fails twice with a clean plate, jump to exercise L1-3 to learn to read the first layer.

Worked solution / what good looks like

A good first print has a bottom surface with smooth, evenly spaced lines that touch each other without gaps, straight vertical walls and visible but regular layer lines. A 20 mm cube typically takes 20–45 minutes depending on the profile. Minor stringing or a slightly rough top surface at the default profile is normal. Record the nozzle (around 200–220 °C for most PLA) and bed (55–60 °C) temperatures you saw in the printer screen: they are your baseline for later experiments.

Exercise 2 — Measure a calibration cube with calipers

⏱ 45 min (+ ~30 min print)
Goal

Learn to use calipers correctly and find out how dimensionally accurate your printer already is. You will calculate the error on each axis in millimeters and in percent.

You will need
  • The XYZ calibration cube (calibration_cube.scad, 20 mm) printed in PLA with default settings
  • Digital calipers; a fresh battery helps
  • Flush cutters or a hobby knife to remove any small blobs and the first-layer "elephant foot" burr
Steps
  1. Close the calipers and zero them. The display must read 0.00 with closed jaws. If not, clean the jaws and press zero.
  2. Deburr gently. Remove any thin ridge at the bottom edge (the first-layer bulge, often called elephant foot) and any blob at the seam. Measure only the true wall, not the burr.
  3. Measure each axis three times at different heights or positions (X: left to right, Y: front to back, Z: bottom to top) and write down all values.
  4. Average the three readings per axis, then compute error = average − 20.00 mm and error % = error / 20 × 100.
  5. Compare axes. Is one axis consistently larger or smaller? Is the Z height within a layer height of nominal?

Success criteria
  • Calipers were zeroed and every value was measured at least three times
  • You calculated the error for X, Y and Z in mm and in percent
  • You can say in one sentence whether your printer is accurate to ±0.2 mm on a 20 mm part
Hint

If X and Y differ from each other by more than 0.3 mm, check that the belts are tight (pluck them; they should twang, not flap) and that the cube was not rotated or the burr measured by mistake. If all three axes are about 0.1–0.2 mm too large, that is usually over-extrusion and not a calibration error.

Worked solution / what good looks like

A well-tuned consumer printer usually lands within ±0.1–0.2 mm (0.5–1 %) on a 20 mm cube. Example: X = 20.08, Y = 20.12, Z = 19.96 gives errors of +0.08, +0.12 and −0.04 mm (+0.4 %, +0.6 %, −0.2 %). Z is often slightly short because the first layer is squished into the plate and the top layer is rounded. Remember that holes come out smaller and outside walls slightly larger than modeled, so the same cube tells you which way to compensate when you design functional parts later (see Tolerances & fits).

Exercise 3 — Spot first-layer quality

⏱ 1 h
Goal

Recognize a too-high, correct and too-low first layer by eye and by touch, and fix it using the printer's calibration (Z offset / first-layer squish). Nearly every print quality problem starts here.

You will need
  • A clean build plate, PLA, and a first-layer test: slice a 100 × 100 × 0.2 mm square (a thin plate in the slicer, or any model with a large flat bottom)
  • A phone camera in macro mode or a magnifier
  • Scraper
Steps
  1. Print the test square with the default profile. Watch the nozzle as it lays the first lines.
  2. Look at the top side of the printed plate (it faces the bed): are the lines flat, fused and glossy-smooth like Figure 3 center, or round and spaced?
  3. Run your finger and fingernail across the surface. Ripples or sharp edges mean too low; a rope-like feel means too high.
  4. Try the "scrape test": push a corner with the scraper. If it comes off in a flash with no resistance and no marks, it may be too weak; if it needs a lot of force and leaves residue, it may be too squished.
  5. Adjust once. Change Z offset or first-layer height by a small step (about 0.02–0.05 mm) in the right direction, and reprint. Only change one thing per attempt.
  6. Photograph both results side by side.
Success criteria
  • You can name which of the three conditions your first print showed and why
  • A second print, after one small adjustment, shows a visibly better first layer (or you documented why none was needed)
  • Photos of both layers are saved with your journal notes
Hint

Printers with automatic bed calibration usually let you apply a small live Z offset while the first layer prints, which is the fastest way to learn. Move in small steps and wait 10–20 seconds to see the effect on the line currently being drawn. Also check that the plate is clean; dirt can mimic a "too high" problem.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Typical beginner finding: the first print shows slightly round, separated lines (too high). Lowering Z offset by 0.04 mm makes lines touch and the plate surface looks uniform. If you pass further than needed, you will see the nozzle drag, thin transparent-looking lines and ridges at the sides. The sweet spot is a narrow range; once found, note it in your journal for this plate and material. More background in First layer & bed adhesion.

Exercise 4 — Identify the parts of your printer

⏱ 30 min
Goal

Name the ten main parts of an FDM printer and find each one on your own machine. Knowing the vocabulary makes manuals, forums and troubleshooting guides readable.

You will need
  • Your printer (powered off and cool) and its manual or the Anatomy of an FDM printer page
  • Paper and pen, or a phone to photograph and annotate each part
Steps
  1. Name the parts in Figure 4 from memory or with the anatomy page. Write the number and name in your notes.
  2. Find all ten (or their equivalents) on your own printer. Photograph each and label it. Some printers hide parts inside the toolhead or housing; note "hidden" and where it would be.
  3. Trace the filament path with a finger from the spool to the nozzle tip and note every component it passes.
  4. Locate the heat sources and the moving parts. Mark with a sticky note which parts get hot and which can pinch fingers.
  5. Find the emergency exit: where can you cut power quickly?
Success criteria
  • All ten parts in the figure are named correctly
  • You located the equivalent of every part on your own printer
  • You can describe the filament path from spool to nozzle in one sentence
Hint

Follow the filament: it starts at the spool, passes through a tube or guide, is gripped by gears (the extruder), is melted in the hotend, and leaves through the nozzle onto the build plate. The remaining parts are the structure that moves the nozzle and the platform it prints on.

Worked solution / what good looks like

1 filament spool (on a spool holder) · 2 filament path through a PTFE tube or guide (a Bowden tube on some printers) · 3 extruder: motor and drive gears that push filament · 4 hotend heater block with thermistor (temperature sensor) and heater · 5 nozzle · 6 X-axis gantry/rail that carries the toolhead sideways · 7 vertical (Z) rail or lead screw that raises the gantry or lowers the bed · 8 build plate (removable sheet, usually PEI) · 9 heated bed · 10 display or control panel. On a CoreXY machine such as the Bambu X2D the bed moves in Z only while the toolhead moves in X and Y; on a "bed-slinger" the bed moves in Y. The parts are the same, only the motion layout differs. See the X2D hardware tour for a dual-nozzle example.

Exercise 5 — Load and unload filament

⏱ 30 min
Goal

Change filament confidently, without clogs, tangles or burnt fingers. You will do a complete unload–cut–load cycle and learn why a clean 45° tip and a preheated nozzle matter.

You will need
  • Your printer with two spools of the same material type (or one spool you can unload and reload)
  • Flush cutters
  • Paper towel or a small container for purged filament
Steps
  1. Preheat the nozzle to the temperature of the filament currently loaded (or the printer's "unload" function does this for you).
  2. Unload. Use the printer's unload function or pull gently while the extruder reverses. Never yank cold filament; if it resists, wait for the nozzle to reach temperature.
  3. Inspect the tip. A thin string or a bulge means the filament cooled in the hotend while retracting; cut it off with the flush cutters at 45°.
  4. Secure the loose end of the old spool in its hole or clip so it does not tangle, and store the spool in a sealed bag.
  5. Load the new filament: straighten the first 10 cm, cut a 45° tip, thread it into the entry point until the gears grab it, then let the printer purge.
  6. Watch the purge. Wait for the color to change completely and the extruded line to be smooth and round. Wipe or remove the blob after it cools.
Success criteria
  • Filament was unloaded and loaded with the nozzle at temperature
  • The new color extrudes smoothly with no clicking or grinding from the extruder
  • The spool turns freely and the loose end is secured
Hint

If the extruder clicks or the gears slip, the nozzle is either too cold or already partly blocked. Check the temperature first. Also verify that the filament is not wrapped around itself on the spool, since a crossed loop can stop feeding in the middle of a print.

Worked solution / what good looks like

A clean cycle takes five minutes. The old filament comes out with a slim, evenly shaped tip; the new one purges a 5–10 cm length of a continuous round line with no bubbles or stripes. For an automated system like the Bambu AMS, the machine does the cutting and purging itself, but the same rules apply: dry filament, tidy spool, clean tip (see AMS 2 Pro deep dive). More about drying in Drying & storage.

Exercise 6 — Read a slicer preview

⏱ 45 min
Goal

Use the layer preview in your slicer to predict what the print will look like, where supports are needed and how long and how much material it will take, before you waste any filament.

You will need
  • A slicer (Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, PrusaSlicer or Cura)
  • Two models: the overhang ladder and any organic model with a curved surface (e.g. a figurine from a model repository)
Steps
  1. Import and slice the overhang ladder with supports off and note the estimated time and filament weight.
  2. Scrub the layer slider from first to last layer. Stop at each overhang and look for lines hanging in air.
  3. Switch the color scheme to "line type" (or "feature type") and identify outer wall, inner wall, infill, top/bottom surfaces and, where present, support.
  4. Turn supports on and compare: how much extra time and weight do they add? Which angles trigger supports?
  5. Find the seam on the curved model: where does each layer start and end?
  6. Check estimated values (time, weight) for three settings: 0.12 mm, 0.2 mm and 0.28 mm layer height and note them.
Success criteria
  • You can name every feature color in your slicer's legend
  • You identified the angle at which supports first appear
  • You noted time and weight at three layer heights and can explain the pattern
Hint

Overhang angles are normally measured from vertical: 0° is a wall, 90° is a flat ceiling. Many slicers add supports beyond about 45–55° by default, but a well-cooled PLA print can often manage 60° without. Compare what the slicer wants with what your printer can really do after you print the ladder in exercise L2-5.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Typical results for the overhang ladder: without supports, the 30°–50° wedges look clean in the preview, while 70° and 80° show lines floating above empty space (the preview may highlight them). With supports on, the slicer adds supports below the steepest wedges and the estimate grows by a few minutes and a few grams. Moving from 0.12 to 0.28 mm layers typically cuts time by more than half with the same model. Key idea: the preview tells you what the printer will try; it does not tell you whether the plastic will hold. Read Slicer settings explained for the settings behind each feature.

Exercise 7 — Layer height experiment

⏱ 2 h (mostly printing)
Goal

See and measure how layer height changes surface quality and print time. You will print the same small model at three layer heights and make an evidence-based choice for different jobs.

You will need
  • One small model with a smooth curve and a slope, e.g. a 30 mm sphere-top cylinder, a small vase or the calibration cube with a 45° chamfer
  • PLA (same spool for all prints), calipers, a phone camera, a stopwatch or the slicer's time estimate
  • About 20–30 g of filament for all three prints
Steps
  1. Slice three versions with 0.12 mm, 0.20 mm and 0.28 mm layer height. Everything else (temperature, speed, infill) stays identical. Note the estimated times and filament weights.
  2. Print all three on the same plate session; label each with a pen on the underside.
  3. Compare surfaces under the same light and distance, and take a photo of each from the same angle with a coin for scale.
  4. Measure actual print time from the printer's report and compare with the slicer estimate.
  5. Decide which layer height you would use for (a) a visible figurine, (b) a drawer organizer, (c) a quick prototype.

Success criteria
  • Three prints with only layer height changed, labeled and photographed
  • A table of estimated vs real time and weight
  • A written choice of layer height for three different jobs, with one-line reasons
Hint

Layer height has a ceiling set by your nozzle: stay between about 25 % and 75 % of the nozzle diameter (0.1–0.3 mm for a 0.4 mm nozzle). Time does not scale perfectly with layer count because acceleration, travel and cooling add fixed overhead per layer.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Example outcome for a 30 mm figure: 0.12 mm takes 2 h 10 min, 0.20 mm takes 1 h 20 min and 0.28 mm takes 1 h. Layer lines are hard to see at 0.12 mm and obvious at 0.28 mm on the shoulder, while vertical walls look similar in all three. Recommended choices: figurine at 0.12 mm (or 0.16 mm), organizer at 0.20 mm, prototype at 0.28 mm. Layer height also affects strength: thicker layers are usually bonded slightly less per unit of material, so a stronger part is not always a coarser one. See Slicer settings explained and Speed, cooling & quality.

Exercise 8 — Infill experiment

⏱ 2 h (mostly printing)
Goal

Find out how much infill you really need. You will print identical bars at different infill densities, weigh them, time them and test how much load they take.

You will need
  • A kitchen scale and calipers
  • A bar model: 100 × 15 × 10 mm (any CAD, or a rectangular prism in the slicer)
  • PLA, about 40 g for four bars; a rigid support at two ends (two books), and a water bottle or backpack with weights for the optional load test
Steps
  1. Slice four bars with the same wall count (3), same top/bottom layers (4–5) and densities of 5 %, 15 %, 30 % and 60 % in the same pattern (gyroid or cubic).
  2. Print, label each bar with its infill percent and weigh them after cooling.
  3. Record slicer-estimated time and weight vs measured weight.
  4. Flex test. Hold a bar at both ends and bend gently; rank stiffness from 1 (floppy) to 4 (rigid). Do not apply enough force to hurt your hands.
  5. Optional load test. Support each bar on two books 80 mm apart and hang a bag of known weight at the center. Record the weight at which it visibly sags 2 mm or breaks. Add weight in small steps and keep your face away from the bar.
  6. Plot weight vs infill % and stiffness vs infill %.
Success criteria
  • Four bars printed with only infill density changed
  • Weights and times recorded in a table
  • You can state at which infill the extra strength per gram drops off
Hint

Increasing walls from 2 to 4 often adds more stiffness than raising infill from 20 % to 50 %. If your results do not follow the pattern, check that you changed only one parameter and that all bars printed at the same orientation.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Exercise 9 — Print a simple practical part: a cable clip

⏱ 1.5 h (incl. ~25 min print)
Goal

Go from a measurement on your desk to a useful printed object that fits. You will adapt one dimension of a parametric design, print it and test whether it holds your cable.

You will need
  • A cable (phone charger, USB cable, mouse cable) and calipers
  • cable_clip.scad (needs the free OpenSCAD). No OpenSCAD? Re-create the clip in Tinkercad (exercise L1-15) or download any cable clip from a model repository.
  • PLA, two small screws (M3 or 3 mm wood screws) or double-sided foam tape
Steps
  1. Measure the cable in two directions (cables are often slightly oval) with calipers. Write down the larger value.
  2. Set cable_d in the file to that value. Keep fit = 0.3 and set opening to about 70–75 % of the cable diameter.
  3. Export an STL (F6 to render, then F7) or save a 3MF and slice with 3 walls and 15–20 % infill. Place the foot on the plate; no supports.
  4. Print and test. Press the cable in: it should snap in with a small click and stay, but not need a tool.
  5. Fix if needed. Too tight: raise fit by 0.1 or widen the opening by 0.5. Too loose: lower fit or narrow the opening. Reprint (it takes only a few minutes).
  6. Mount it with two screws or foam tape and use it for a week; note any cracks.
Success criteria
  • The cable snaps in and stays in place when you pull sideways gently
  • The clip does not crack when the cable is inserted or removed
  • You wrote down the final cable_d, fit and opening values
Hint

Bending forces act across layer lines if the clip is printed standing up, which is why it is designed to print flat: the layers then run around the ring and the C-shape flexes without splitting. If your clip cracks at the opening, add one more wall or choose PETG, which is tougher than PLA.

Worked solution / what good looks like

For a 4.2 mm USB cable: cable_d = 4.2, fit = 0.3, opening = 3.0. Typical result: a first print that is slightly tight (printed holes shrink a little), solved by fit = 0.4. Final clip fits and holds a cable that weighs a few grams per meter. This is your first iteration loop: measure → design → print → test → adjust. Real engineering parts follow the same loop, only with more tolerances; read Tolerances & fits when you are ready.

Exercise 10 — Brim vs no brim

⏱ 1.5 h
Goal

Learn what a brim does and when it pays off. You will print a tall, narrow part twice, once with and once without a brim, and compare stability and clean-up effort.

You will need
  • A tall, thin model with a small footprint: a 12 × 12 × 50 mm column, or a tall figure on a small base
  • PLA, a clean plate, a scraper and a hobby knife
Steps
  1. Slice the column twice: brim type "none", then "outer brim" of 5 mm width. Keep every other setting the same.
  2. Print both one after another on the same area of the plate. Watch for the first 10 layers: does the unbrimmed column wobble?
  3. Test adhesion after cooling: push each column sideways at the top with one finger. Which one moves?
  4. Remove the brim with your fingers or a knife held away from your body. Does the brim leave a rough edge? Note the time it took.
  5. Repeat with a flat wide part such as a 60 × 60 × 2 mm plate. Is the brim needed there?
Success criteria
  • Both columns were printed with identical settings except the brim
  • You compared adhesion and removal effort and wrote down your finding
  • You can list three situations where you would add a brim
Hint

PLA on a clean textured plate often sticks without a brim even for tall parts. If your unbrimmed column prints fine, make the challenge harder: use a 6 × 6 mm base or a plate that has seen a few finger touches, and notice how quickly adhesion changes.

Worked solution / what good looks like

A typical outcome is that both columns finish, but the unbrimmed one detaches from the plate with a light push or shows corner lift on the first layers, while the brimmed one stays put. Use a brim when: the footprint is small compared to the height, sharp corners lift (especially in ABS/ASA/PETG), or the material shrinks (nylon, ABS). Skip it when it would add cleanup on a visible edge. A brim of 3–5 mm (about 8–12 lines) is a typical start. See First layer & bed adhesion.

Exercise 11 — Temperature observation

⏱ 2 h (mostly printing)
Goal

See how nozzle temperature changes the look and quality of the same filament, and choose a good starting temperature for a new spool. At Level 1 this is purely observational; exercise L2-1 turns it into a measured calibration.

You will need
  • The temperature tower (temp_tower.scad), or the temperature calibration tower built into your slicer
  • A spool of PLA with its recommended range printed on the label (see L1-13)
  • A good light and a phone for macro photos
Steps
  1. Prepare the tower with your slicer. In the OpenSCAD version set start_temp and step to cover the label range (PLA: 220 to 190 °C in steps of 5). Insert a temperature change at each tier boundary using your slicer's custom G-code at layer change or its built-in calibration tool.
  2. Print the tower at your normal speed and with fan on.
  3. Examine each tier: bridge underside, overhang fin underside, surface gloss, strings in the opening, and how easily the tier breaks when you bend it off (do this last).
  4. Score each tier from 1 to 5 in a table for stringing, bridge quality, overhang quality, surface and layer bonding.
  5. Pick your temperature for this spool and write it on the spool or in your journal.
Success criteria
  • A tower was printed with at least five distinct temperature tiers
  • Each tier is scored in a table and the best temperature is chosen
  • You can explain in one sentence why higher temperature increases stringing
Hint

Look at bridges first. Hot plastic stays soft longer and sags; cold plastic leaves frayed lines. The best tier often shows the straightest and flattest bridge underside. Judge stringing on the same tier by looking between the pillars, not just on the surface.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Typical PLA result: 220 °C shows glossy walls and strings across the opening; 190 °C looks dull with gaps between layers and the tier breaks cleanly along a layer line; the best looks around 205–210 °C. The difference between neighboring tiers is small, so compare tiers that are two or three steps apart. If every tier looks poor, the filament may be wet (popping, bubbles, rough texture); read Drying & storage. If you use Bambu filament with its RFID tag, the default profile is already tuned; the tower then simply confirms it.

Exercise 12 — Remove supports cleanly

⏱ 1.5 h
Goal

Print a part that needs supports and remove them without damaging the surface. You will compare standard and tree supports and see how the support-to-model gap decides how clean the underside is.

You will need
  • A model with an overhang, e.g. a letter "T", a mushroom or the overhang ladder (steep wedges)
  • PLA, flush cutters, needle-nose pliers, a hobby knife, a deburring tool, safety glasses
Steps
  1. Print version A with normal (grid) supports and the slicer defaults for the support gap.
  2. Print version B with tree or organic supports, again at default settings.
  3. Remove supports wearing safety glasses. Start at the thinnest point, wiggle gently and pull away from the part. Use flush cutters for stubborn islands, aimed away from the model.
  4. Clean up the contact surface with a deburring tool or fine sandpaper (220 grit and up).
  5. Compare the two undersides, the time spent removing, and the filament used for support (from the slicer).
Success criteria
  • Both versions were printed and the supports removed without breaking the model
  • You compared surface quality, removal time and extra material
  • You know which type you prefer for a visible versus a hidden underside
Hint

Warm supports release more easily and the surface scuffs less. A hair dryer for 10–20 seconds on a PLA part softens residue; never overheat PLA, which starts to deform around 55–60 °C. Support removal also depends on orientation: whenever you can rotate the model so that fewer surfaces need support, do it.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Typical findings: normal supports come off as a few big pieces and leave a regular rough texture; tree supports use 30–60 % less material, come off in thin branches and leave isolated dots that sand away quickly, but they may break during printing if the model is large. A gap that is too small makes supports fuse to the model; one that is too large leaves a droopy underside. If the first support layers print poorly, the support interface may be too sparse. Deeper material in Supports, bridges & overhangs. On a Bambu X2D the aux nozzle can print supports in a different material for an even cleaner release.

Exercise 13 — Read a spool label

⏱ 20 min
Goal

Extract every piece of useful information from a filament label, verify the diameter with calipers and estimate how many meters you hold. Good labels tell you the right temperatures and whether the filament needs drying.

You will need
  • Two or three spools (different brands or materials are best) or their labels and boxes
  • Calipers, a kitchen scale and a calculator (or the calculators page)
Steps
  1. Fill in the table below for each spool: material, color, diameter, net weight, nozzle range, bed range, drying recommendation, lot number.
  2. Measure the diameter with calipers at five points along 1 m of filament and rotate the filament 90° at each point. Note the spread.
  3. Compute the length: for 1.75 mm PLA (density ≈ 1.24 g/cm³) 1 kg is roughly 330 m, i.e. about 3 g per meter. Check the formula: length = mass / (density × π × (d/2)²).
  4. Compare spools: do the nozzle ranges agree with what you chose in L1-11? Which label gives drying advice and which does not?

Worked solution / what good looks like

Worked example: 1 kg of 1.75 mm PLA: cross-section = π × 0.0875² cm² ≈ 0.0241 cm²; mass per meter = 0.0241 cm² × 100 cm × 1.24 g/cm³ ≈ 2.98 g, so 1000 g ≈ 335 m. For PETG (≈ 1.27 g/cm³) it is ≈ 327 m; for ABS (≈ 1.04 g/cm³) ≈ 400 m. A spread below ±0.03 mm is very good; 0.05 mm is acceptable. Exercise 14 — Workspace safety audit

⏱ 45 min
Goal

Inspect your printing area for fire, burn, fume, electrical and mechanical risks and fix the three most important ones. A safe workspace is not a luxury: unattended hot machines and plastic dust are real hazards.

You will need
  • Your printer area, a phone for photos and a pen
  • The Safety & workspace page for reference
Steps
  1. Photograph your setup from two angles.
  2. Walk through the checklist in Figure 14 and score each item: 0 (not present), 1 (partly), 2 (fine).
  3. Check the electrics. Is the power cable intact, plugged in firmly and not under tension or covered? Is the printer connected to a wall outlet or a quality power strip with an approved plug, not a chain of adapters?
  4. Check the air. PLA smells mildly and emits ultrafine particles; ABS, ASA and nylon emit more. Where does the air go? Is there a window or filter?
  5. Plan three fixes in order of risk, and do at least one today.
Success criteria
  • All eight items are scored with a note
  • At least one fix is implemented and photographed
  • You know who to call or what to do if a hotend or heater fails
Hint

The highest risks are almost always the same: no smoke detector in the room, flammable material near the printer, unattended long prints and poor ventilation with ABS or other engineering plastics. A smoke detector costs a few dollars; a switchable power strip lets you cut power with one hand.

Worked solution / what good looks like

A score of 12 or more out of 16 is a solid start. Typical fixes: move the printer off a cardboard-covered shelf, add a smoke detector in the same room, store spare filament in a container, label the power strip "printer", and write on the wall which materials you may print indoors without extra ventilation. Many modern printers (including enclosed ones with filters) reduce but do not remove the need for good air exchange. The X2D, for example, has an integrated filter and exhaust option, see X2D overview. Never leave a fire to burn: if something ignites, power off at the switch, close the lid and leave the room if smoke persists.

Exercise 15 — Your first model in Tinkercad

⏱ 1 h (+ ~30 min print)
Goal

Design a printable key-tag from scratch in a free, browser-based CAD tool. You will combine solid and hole shapes, add text, export a file and slice it, taking one idea all the way from head to printed object.

You will need
  • A free account at tinkercad.com (browser-based, no installation)
  • Your slicer and printer, PLA
  • Optional: calipers to check the final size
Steps
  1. Create a new design and drag a Box onto the workplane. Resize it to 40 × 20 × 3 mm (click the box, then type values in the handles).
  2. Round the corners. Use the Box's "Radius" setting or add a rounded shape and group.
  3. Add a hole. Drag a Cylinder, set it to "Hole" in the Inspector, 5 mm diameter and 10 mm height, and place it 5 mm from the left end, centered. Check from the top view.
  4. Add text. Drag the Text shape, type your name (max ~8 characters), set height to 1 mm and place it on top of the box so it overlaps. Choose a bold font for strength.
  5. Group everything (Ctrl+G). The hole should now cut through the box and the text sits on top.
  6. Export as STL or 3MF and slice. Print flat with the text up, 0.2 mm layers, 3 walls, and 20 % infill.
Success criteria
  • The design has a through-hole, text and rounded corners
  • The tag printed and the hole fits a key ring (about 4–5 mm)
  • You can explain the difference between a solid shape and a hole shape in one sentence
Hint

If the text does not appear in the slicer preview, it probably floats above or is not touching the box: the text should overlap the box by a small amount, at least 0.2 mm. A raised text of 1 mm height is plenty; very small letters (smaller than 5 mm) get muddy with a 0.4 mm nozzle. The text label test model shows what your printer can read.

Worked solution / what good looks like

A good result: 40 × 20 × 3 mm base with 1 mm raised text, a 5 mm hole with the nearest edge ≥ 4 mm from the border, so the key ring does not tear through. Check the minimum wall around the hole: 4 mm of material around the hole in PLA is robust; 2 mm is fragile. After printing, measure with calipers: the hole should be about 0.2 mm smaller than designed, so a 5 mm hole may come out at 4.8 mm. If you want to go further, move on to parametric design with OpenSCAD (Parametric CAD workflow) and to the Level 2 box exercise L2-7.

Key takeaways

  • Most print quality depends on the first layer, clean plates and dry filament; learn to read them early.
  • Change one variable at a time and write down results: your own data beats generic advice.
  • Layer height trades time for smoothness; infill beyond ~25 % rarely pays for itself.
  • Supports, brims and temperature are tools, not defaults: use them when the part needs them.
  • Safety is part of printing: detect, ventilate and never leave hot machines unobserved.
