1 · Basics 0 / 15

Read first: What is 3D printing?, Printing processes, First print.

1. What does FDM stand for?

  • Fast Digital Molding
  • Fused Deposition Modeling
  • Filament Density Measurement
  • Flat Dimensional Manufacturing

FDM (also called FFF, fused filament fabrication) melts plastic filament and deposits it layer by layer.

2. Which process cures liquid photopolymer with light?

  • Resin printing (SLA, DLP, LCD)
  • FDM
  • Selective laser sintering
  • CNC milling

Vat photopolymerization hardens liquid resin with UV light. SLS fuses powder; FDM melts filament; milling cuts material away.

3. What does a G-code file contain?

  • The 3D mesh of the model
  • The color of each filament spool
  • Machine instructions: moves, temperatures, extrusion, fan speeds
  • A photograph of the finished part

The slicer converts a mesh (STL or 3MF) into G-code, the movement and temperature commands the printer follows line by line.

4. A common layer height for general-purpose printing with a 0.4 mm nozzle is:

  • 0.02 mm
  • 0.05 mm
  • 0.2 mm
  • 0.8 mm

A rule of thumb is a layer height between 25 % and 75 % of the nozzle diameter, so 0.1–0.3 mm for a 0.4 mm nozzle, with 0.2 mm as the default.

5. Which file format stores only surface geometry, with no units or colors?

  • 3MF
  • STL
  • STEP
  • G-code

STL is a triangle mesh without units or color. 3MF can carry units, colors and settings; STEP is a CAD exchange format with exact surfaces.

6. Why is the first layer printed slightly “squished” onto the bed?

  • To save filament
  • To press the plastic into the surface for adhesion
  • To make the part taller
  • To cool the nozzle

Good squish gives wide, flat lines that fuse with each other and grip the plate. Too much causes elephant foot; too little lets the part peel off.

7. What is a brim?

  • A support structure under overhangs
  • A cooling fan setting
  • A flat ring of extra first-layer lines around the part to increase bed contact
  • A way to hide the seam

A brim attaches to the part and resists warping and tipping. It is removed after printing; a skirt, in contrast, is a loop that does not touch the part.

8. The nozzle is too far from the bed. What does the first layer look like?

  • Flat and transparent
  • Smeared and cut through by the nozzle
  • Round lines that barely stick and may drag or peel
  • Perfect but too thick

If the gap is too large, the extruded lines stay round and do not bond to the bed or each other. Lower the Z offset in small steps.

9. What does a slicer do?

  • Heats the filament
  • Cuts the model into layers and generates the toolpath (G-code)
  • Repairs the printer firmware
  • Measures the model with a scanner

Slicers such as Bambu Studio turn a 3D model and your settings into G-code for a specific printer and filament.

10. A typical nozzle temperature range for PLA is:

  • 120–140 °C
  • 190–220 °C
  • 260–290 °C
  • 320–350 °C

PLA prints well at roughly 190–220 °C. Higher temperatures are used for PETG, ABS, nylon and polycarbonate.

11. In a typical FDM printer, which axis is vertical?

  • X
  • Y
  • Z
  • E

Z is vertical and moves in layer-height steps. X and Y span the print plane; E is the extruder axis.

12. Which of these is a subtractive manufacturing process?

  • FDM
  • Resin printing
  • CNC milling
  • Powder bed fusion

Subtractive processes remove material from a block. Additive processes, including all kinds of 3D printing, build the part up.

13. A surface that overhangs by more than about 45–60° from vertical usually needs:

  • A larger nozzle
  • Support, a different orientation or a redesigned angle
  • A hotter bed
  • Thicker filament

Beyond roughly 45° each layer is increasingly unsupported by the one below, so the underside gets rough or droops without support.

14. Which safety measure is most important for a printer in a living space?

  • Covering the printer with a blanket
  • A working smoke detector nearby and a hard, non-flammable surface; do not leave it unattended for hours without precautions
  • Printing only at night
  • Removing the filament sensor

Fire is rare but serious. See Safety & workspace for ventilation, burn hazards and fire precautions.

15. What does “0.4 mm nozzle” refer to?

  • The nozzle length
  • The filament diameter
  • The diameter of the hole the plastic comes out of
  • The maximum layer height of the machine

The orifice diameter limits line width and detail. Filament is typically 1.75 mm in diameter, which is unrelated.

2 · Materials 0 / 15

Read first: Materials overview, Everyday plastics, Drying & storage.

1. Which filament is generally the easiest for a beginner?

  • Nylon
  • Polycarbonate
  • PLA
  • ABS

PLA prints at low temperature, hardly warps and needs no enclosure.

2. A PLA part is left on a car dashboard in summer. What is the most likely outcome?

  • It becomes stronger
  • It softens and deforms
  • It turns to powder
  • Nothing, because PLA withstands 100 °C

PLA softens at about 55–60 °C, a temperature that a closed car easily exceeds. Use PETG, ASA or stronger material for heat.

3. Compared with PLA, PETG is typically:

  • More brittle and easier to print
  • Tougher and more heat-tolerant, but more prone to stringing
  • Water soluble
  • Magnetic

PETG has better impact resistance and a higher softening temperature than PLA, but it strings and sticks very strongly to some build plates.

4. Why do ABS and ASA usually need an enclosure?

  • They are toxic when cold
  • They shrink noticeably on cooling, so drafts cause warping and layer splitting
  • They are conductive
  • They only print in the dark

A warm, draft-free chamber slows cooling and reduces stress. It also contains fumes, so ventilate or filter the space.

5. What is ASA's main advantage over ABS?

  • It dissolves in water
  • It is flexible
  • Much better UV and weather resistance
  • It prints without any heat

ASA is chemically similar to ABS but keeps color and strength outdoors, which makes it popular for outdoor parts.

6. What does a Shore hardness of 95A on a TPU filament tell you?

  • The nozzle temperature
  • How stiff or soft the material is; 95A is a fairly firm flexible material
  • The melting point
  • The humidity limit

A lower Shore A number means softer. 95A TPU is easier to print than 85A or softer grades.

7. Nylon (PA) is described as hygroscopic. What does this mean?

  • It repels water
  • It absorbs moisture from the air, so it must be dried and stored dry
  • It conducts heat very well
  • It glows under UV light

Wet nylon bubbles, pops and gives weak, rough parts. It is best dried before use and printed from a dry box.

8. Which is a sensible drying temperature range for PLA?

  • 150–180 °C
  • 90–110 °C
  • About 45–55 °C for several hours
  • Room temperature for 5 minutes

PLA softens at around 55–60 °C, so dry well below that. Check the manufacturer's recommendation for the particular spool.

9. Why should carbon-fiber or glass-fiber filled filaments be printed with a hardened nozzle?

  • They are too cold for brass
  • The fibers are abrasive and wear soft brass nozzles quickly
  • They need a thicker filament
  • Hardened nozzles reduce humidity

Brass wears out after a short time with abrasive fillers, and the nozzle diameter grows. Hardened steel or similar nozzles last much longer.

10. Which of these materials has the highest heat resistance?

  • PLA
  • PETG
  • ABS
  • Polycarbonate (PC)

PC has a glass transition above roughly 140 °C, so it is the most heat tolerant of these. It is also demanding to print: high temperatures and an enclosure are needed.

11. Which is a typical symptom of wet filament?

  • Popping or crackling sounds, tiny bubbles, stringing and a rough surface
  • A smoother surface
  • Stronger bridges
  • Less stringing

Water in the filament turns to steam in the hot nozzle. The result is bubbles, noise, rough finish and weaker parts.

12. What is the best way to store filament long term?

  • On an open shelf in the bathroom
  • In direct sunlight
  • In a sealed container or bag with desiccant, away from heat and light
  • In the freezer, uncovered

Humidity and sun are the enemies. A sealed bag with desiccant keeps the spool dry for months.

13. What is PVA used for in 3D printing?

  • As a very strong structural plastic
  • As a water-soluble support material
  • As an electrical conductor
  • As a flexible gasket

PVA dissolves in water, so it supports complex overhangs that cannot be removed by hand. It is very sensitive to moisture and needs careful storage.

14. Why is a printed part not automatically food safe, even if the filament is rated food safe?

  • Plastic cannot touch food
  • Layer lines and gaps trap bacteria and are hard to clean; additives and nozzles may also contaminate
  • Because filament is always toxic
  • Because food is too hot

Rough, porous surfaces are difficult to sanitize. Short contact with dry food may be fine; reusable utensils and cups are a risk.

15. Which statement about PETG and bed adhesion is correct?

  • PETG never sticks to anything
  • PETG can bond so strongly to smooth PEI that it can damage the surface, so use a release agent or a textured plate
  • PETG needs a bed at 0 °C
  • PETG only sticks to glass

The classic mistake is printing PETG with too little Z offset on smooth PEI. A thin glue-stick layer or a textured plate prevents damage.

3 · Slicing 0 / 15

Read first: Slicers, Slicer settings, Speed, cooling & quality.

1. For most functional parts, which setting adds more strength per gram of filament?

  • Infill from 20 % to 100 %
  • More perimeters (walls)
  • Higher print speed
  • Larger skirt

Bending loads act mostly on the outer shell, so walls add far more strength than infill. Infill mainly supports the top surfaces and adds compression resistance.

2. What is a typical infill density for a general-purpose part?

  • 0 % for all parts
  • 10–20 %
  • 60–80 %
  • Always 100 %

Above about 25 % the gain in strength is small compared with the extra print time and material.

3. What is retraction for?

  • To cool the part
  • To pull filament back during travel moves, so that oozing and stringing are reduced
  • To fix the Z-offset
  • To change filament

Retraction reduces pressure in the nozzle before a travel move. Too much retraction can cause clogs; too little causes strings.

4. A 0.42 mm line width, 0.2 mm layer height and 100 mm/s speed require which volumetric flow?

  • 0.84 mm³/s
  • 8.4 mm³/s
  • 84 mm³/s
  • 840 mm³/s

Flow = width × height × speed = 0.42 × 0.2 × 100 = 8.4 mm³/s. Compare it with the maximum flow of your hotend and filament.

5. What is the seam?

  • The border between two filament colors
  • The point where each outer wall loop starts and ends, which often leaves a visible line
  • The place where two STL files overlap
  • A crack in the print

Seam position is adjustable (aligned, nearest, back, random). Hide it in a corner or on the back face.

6. Tree supports compared with normal grid supports usually:

  • Use more material and are harder to remove
  • Use less material and touch the model in fewer places
  • Cannot support overhangs
  • Only work with PLA

Trees branch to touch only where needed, which saves time and often leaves a better surface. Thin branches can be fragile on tall models.

7. Which kind of cooling does PLA benefit from the most?

  • No fan at all
  • High part-cooling fan speed
  • Heated chamber at 60 °C
  • Fan only on the first layer

PLA hardens quickly with strong cooling, which gives better overhangs and bridges. ABS and ASA need little or no part cooling.

8. What does ironing do?

  • Removes support material
  • Passes the hot nozzle over the top surface with very little extrusion to smooth it
  • Heats the bed to a higher temperature
  • Calibrates the flow

Ironing improves flat top surfaces at the cost of print time and can leave artefacts on small areas.

9. Which is the best way to improve long bridges?

  • Faster speed with no fan
  • Slower bridge speed, strong cooling and a tuned bridge flow
  • Hotter nozzle
  • Thicker layers on the bridge

Cooling solidifies the plastic strand before it sags, and a lower flow or thinner bridge line makes it lighter.

10. What does variable (adaptive) layer height do?

  • Changes the nozzle diameter
  • Uses thinner layers on curved surfaces and thicker layers on vertical areas
  • Prints every second layer
  • Increases the bed temperature

It saves time and keeps the fine detail where stair-stepping would otherwise be visible.

11. High acceleration and high print speeds mostly cause which defect?

  • Warping
  • Ringing (ghosting) and loss of corner sharpness
  • Bubbles in the filament
  • Elephant foot

Fast direction changes excite frame vibration. Input shaping and lower acceleration reduce the ripples.

12. What is a “skirt”?

  • A loop printed near the part that does not touch it, used to prime the nozzle
  • A support structure
  • A top layer pattern
  • The same as a brim

A skirt primes the extruder and lets you check the first layer. A brim, in contrast, touches the part to increase adhesion.

13. Gaps in the top surface of a part with sparse infill most likely indicate:

  • Too many top layers
  • Too few top layers or too sparse infill under the top surface
  • A bed that is too hot
  • A nozzle that is too big

The top skin bridges the infill. Use at least 4–5 top layers at 0.2 mm, or a denser infill beneath.

14. Which slicer feature compensates for the slightly squashed first layer that makes the bottom of a part too wide?

  • Ironing
  • Fuzzy skin
  • Elephant-foot compensation
  • Spiral vase mode

It shrinks the first layer outline by a small amount (for example 0.1–0.2 mm), so the final bottom edge is correct.

15. What happens when you print faster than the hotend can melt plastic?

  • The part gets stronger
  • Under-extrusion: gaps, weak layers and a clicking or grinding extruder
  • The nozzle gets colder
  • The bed gets hotter

Every hotend has a maximum volumetric flow. Limit it in the filament profile (mm³/s) so that speeds drop automatically on thick lines.

4 · Design 0 / 15

Read first: Design principles, Tolerances & fits, Joints & fasteners, Strength & orientation.

1. What is the practical minimum wall thickness for a 0.4 mm nozzle?

  • 0.1 mm
  • 0.4 mm, one line, for any load
  • About 0.8 mm (two lines), with 1.2 mm or more for load-bearing walls
  • 5 mm

Wall thickness should be a multiple of the line width so that the slicer fills it cleanly with whole lines.

2. A printed 6.0 mm hole in CAD usually measures:

  • Exactly 6.0 mm
  • Slightly less than 6.0 mm, so add clearance (often 0.1–0.3 mm)
  • Much larger, around 7 mm
  • Random, depending on the weather

Plastic flows inward and corners get rounded, so holes print small. Print a hole gauge (hole_gauge.scad) and keep the numbers for your printer.

3. Which direction is a standard FDM part weakest in?

  • Along the layer lines in the XY plane
  • Across the layers (Z direction), where the bonds between layers carry tension
  • In compression
  • It is equally strong in all directions

Layers are welded together by heat, so tension across layers is often 30–60 % weaker than along them. Orient parts so the main tensile load runs along the layers.

4. Why add a fillet to an inside corner?

  • To save filament only
  • To reduce stress concentration, which makes parts break less often
  • To make the part heavier
  • To hide the seam

Sharp inside corners concentrate stress, so cracks start there. A radius of 1–3 mm makes a large difference.

5. How do you print a horizontal round hole without support?

  • Make it a perfect circle and hope
  • Make the top of the hole a teardrop or a diamond shape (about 45° slopes)
  • Make it square at the bottom
  • Print it with 100 % infill

A circle has a nearly horizontal ceiling at its top, which sags. A teardrop keeps every slope self-supporting.

6. Which installation method is typical for heat-set inserts?

  • Gluing with superglue only
  • Pressing them into a slightly smaller hole with a soldering iron until they are flush
  • Printing them in place
  • Hammering them in cold

The molten plastic flows into the knurls, so the insert anchors well. The hole size is given in the insert's datasheet.

7. How can you lower the strain in a snap-fit cantilever beam?

  • Make it shorter and thicker
  • Make it longer, thinner (within limits) or reduce the deflection
  • Add sharp corners at the base
  • Print it with the layers across the beam

Strain is proportional to thickness × deflection ÷ length². Always print a snap beam with the layers along its length.

8. What is the main advantage of parametric design?

  • The part prints faster
  • You change a named value (for example wall thickness) and the whole model updates
  • It needs no CAD software
  • It makes STL files smaller

Parameters let you adapt a design to another size or printer in seconds. See Parametric CAD workflow.

9. How do you avoid support on the bottom edge of a part that overhangs?

  • Use a fillet
  • Use a chamfer of 45° or less instead of a fillet or a flat overhang
  • Use a bigger brim
  • Make the part thicker

A fillet starts with a nearly horizontal slope at the bottom. A 45° chamfer prints cleanly without support.

10. A self-tapping screw goes into a printed boss. What is good practice?

  • No hole at all
  • A pilot hole slightly smaller than the screw's core diameter and a thick wall around it
  • A hole larger than the screw's outer diameter
  • Use only a single wall around the boss

A thick boss (wall at least equal to the screw diameter) resists splitting. Repeated assembly is better done with heat-set inserts.

11. What is the recommended minimum feature size for embossed text on a 0.4 mm nozzle?

  • 0.1 mm strokes
  • About 0.8–1.0 mm stroke width and 0.4–0.6 mm height
  • 10 mm stroke width
  • Any size works

A stroke should be at least two line widths wide; a sans-serif bold font is easiest. Check text_label_test.scad for a quick test.

12. Which orientation gives the strongest bracket that carries a load across its arm?

  • Layers perpendicular to the arm, so that the load pulls layers apart
  • Layers parallel to the plane in which the arm bends, so that tension runs along the lines
  • Orientation does not matter
  • Standing on its tip, in all cases

Place the layers in the plane of the largest stress. Print one test bracket in each orientation if you are unsure.

13. Which thread is easiest to print directly?

  • A very fine M3 thread
  • A large-diameter, coarse-pitch thread with some clearance
  • A micro thread with 0.5 mm pitch
  • Any thread, since printers have infinite resolution

Pitch must be much larger than the layer height. For small fasteners use metal screws with inserts or taps instead.

14. What does a tolerance coupon show?

  • The color accuracy of the filament
  • Which clearances give a sliding, snug or tight fit on your printer and filament
  • The maximum print speed
  • The weight of the print

Results depend on printer, nozzle, material and orientation. Print one with your usual settings and write the result in your notes (tolerance_gauge.scad).

15. Why is a 45° chamfer on the first-layer edge useful?

  • It increases elephant foot
  • It hides the squashed first-layer bulge and avoids sharp edges that catch on things
  • It makes the printer go faster
  • It lowers the nozzle temperature

A small chamfer (0.5–1 mm) at the bottom edge makes parts fit together even if the first layer flares slightly.

5 · Troubleshooting 0 / 15

Read first: Troubleshooting, First layer & adhesion, Calibration.

1. The bottom edge of a part is bulged and wider than the rest. This is called:

  • Ringing
  • Elephant foot
  • Stringing
  • Z-banding

The first layers are squashed outward by the nozzle and the warm bed. Raise the Z offset slightly, lower the bed temperature or use elephant-foot compensation.

2. Thin strands of plastic span the gaps between separate parts of a print. What is the best first fix?

  • Print faster
  • Dry the filament and tune retraction and temperature
  • Increase the bed temperature
  • Remove the cooling fan

Wet filament and a hot nozzle are the most common causes of stringing. Dry first, then tune.

3. The corners of a large flat part lift off the bed. What helps?

  • Open the door of the printer and add a fan
  • A brim, a clean plate, a suitable bed temperature and a draft-free or enclosed chamber
  • Printing with thicker layers only
  • Turning the bed off

Warping is caused by uneven cooling and shrinkage. Hold the corners down and slow the cooling.

4. The first layer will not stick; lines are round and peel off. The most likely cause is:

  • A Z offset that is too low
  • A Z offset that is too high, or a dirty plate
  • Too many walls
  • Too much infill

Clean the plate with isopropyl alcohol or warm water and soap, then lower the nozzle in small steps (0.02 mm).

5. The extruder clicks and the print shows gaps. What is a probable cause?

  • The bed is too cold
  • A partial clog, wet filament, a temperature that is too low or a speed that is too high for the flow
  • The model is too small
  • The spool is too full

Under-extrusion means the nozzle receives less plastic than the slicer expects. Check the path of the filament and reduce the speed.

6. Layers in the upper part of a print are shifted sideways. What do you check first?

  • The filament color
  • Collisions with curled parts, belt tension, loose pulleys and excessive acceleration
  • The wall count
  • The spool weight

A layer shift means that the toolhead lost position. Curled edges that the nozzle hits are a common cause.

7. Ripples that echo sharp features on the surface are called:

  • Elephant foot
  • Ringing or ghosting
  • Warping
  • Blobbing

Vibration after fast direction changes. Reduce acceleration or enable input shaping, and make sure that the printer stands on a stable surface.

8. A regular wave pattern repeating every few millimeters in height is called Z-banding. A typical cause is:

  • Wet filament
  • A bent or loose lead screw, a wobbling Z axis or inconsistent extrusion
  • A too-large nozzle
  • A cold room

Check the Z motion mechanics first, then temperature stability. Printer type matters: not every machine has a visible lead screw.

9. Small blobs and zits appear at the seam. Which fix is typical?

  • Tune pressure advance (flow dynamics) and the retraction, and choose the seam position
  • Increase the infill
  • Use a bigger nozzle
  • Switch off the fan completely

Blobs at the seam are the result of pressure in the nozzle at the start and end of each loop. Pressure advance reduces this.

10. A top surface shows pillowing: bumps and holes. What is the usual remedy?

  • Fewer top layers
  • More top layers, a denser infill under the top, and better cooling
  • A hotter nozzle
  • A thinner first layer

Pillowing happens when the top skin bridges sparse infill before it has cooled. Add layers and cooling.

11. The filament comes out brown and the nozzle clogs often when printing PLA. What is likely?

  • The temperature is too low
  • Overheating or heat creep, or burnt residue from a previous high-temperature material
  • The print is too slow
  • The bed is too clean

Do a cold pull or replace the nozzle, and check that the cooling of the heatbreak works.

12. A print has turned into a tangled “spaghetti” mess halfway. What is the most likely start of the failure?

  • The part detached from the plate or a layer failed, and printing continued in the air
  • The model had too few triangles
  • The G-code was too short
  • The filament was too long

Improve adhesion first. Printers with camera-based spaghetti detection can pause the print for you.

13. Long bridges sag. What do you try first?

  • More heat, less cooling
  • More cooling, lower bridge speed and flow, and shorter spans or an added support
  • Fewer walls
  • A larger layer height

The strand must solidify before it sags. Print the bridge test to find the longest reliable span.

14. The underside of an overhang curls upward. What does that tell you?

  • The overhang is cooled too little and the edges curl and catch the nozzle
  • The bed is not heated
  • The filament is too thin
  • The nozzle is too clean

Increase cooling, slow down on overhangs, and lower the temperature for PLA. See the overhang ladder.

15. There are small gaps between the infill and the inner wall. What helps?

  • A lower infill
  • A higher infill/wall overlap and a correct flow ratio
  • A shorter nozzle
  • Turning off the walls

The infill must touch the wall to bond to it. A few percent more overlap closes the gap; calibrate flow first.

6 · Bambu X2D & AMS 2 Pro 0 / 15

Read first: X2D overview, AMS 2 Pro, Dual-nozzle printing, Filament compatibility.

1. What is the build volume of the X2D (single or left nozzle)?

  • 180 × 180 × 180 mm
  • 256 × 256 × 260 mm
  • 350 × 350 × 350 mm
  • 220 × 220 × 250 mm

256 × 256 × 260 mm is the full build volume with the main (left) nozzle.

2. Which nozzle is the main nozzle with the direct-drive extruder in the toolhead?

  • The left nozzle
  • The right nozzle
  • Both are identical in every way
  • Neither: the extruder is behind the printer

The left hotend is the main one with the servo-driven extruder inside the toolhead. The right auxiliary hotend is fed by an extruder at the rear through a PTFE tube.

3. What is the maximum temperature of the X2D's actively heated chamber?

  • 35 °C
  • 45 °C
  • 65 °C
  • 120 °C

The chamber heater reaches up to 65 °C, which helps with warping of materials such as ABS, ASA and PC. It is a plus for engineering plastics; for PLA, cooler air is desirable instead.

4. What is the maximum nozzle temperature of the X2D?

  • 220 °C
  • 260 °C
  • 300 °C
  • 400 °C

The hotends reach 300 °C, enough for PETG, ABS, ASA, PA, PC and many composites. Check the filament compatibility page for what each nozzle can run.

5. What speed and acceleration limits apply to the auxiliary (right) nozzle?

  • No limits: it is identical to the main nozzle
  • Max about 200 mm/s and about 1000 mm/s² acceleration
  • Max 2000 mm/s
  • It cannot move at all

The auxiliary extruder is a conventional stepper-driven one with a tube to the toolhead, which limits speed and acceleration and demands longer retraction.

6. Flow Dynamics Calibration on the X2D applies to:

  • Both nozzles
  • The main nozzle only
  • The AMS only
  • The camera

The auxiliary nozzle uses a much larger flow-dynamics (K) value because of its long tube path, and you cannot calibrate it with the same automatic procedure.

7. How many filament slots does one AMS 2 Pro have?

  • 1
  • 2
  • 4
  • 16

One AMS 2 Pro holds four spools. Several units can be combined for more colors.

8. What is the maximum drying temperature of the AMS 2 Pro?

  • 45 °C
  • 65 °C
  • 100 °C
  • It has no dryer

Two heater and fan modules can dry filament up to 65 °C, suitable for PLA, PETG and similar materials. Use the printer or Bambu Studio to set drying.

9. What does the color change of the indicator in the AMS desiccant tell you?

  • The filament type
  • The desiccant is saturated with moisture and should be replaced or regenerated
  • The nozzle is too hot
  • The firmware is out of date

Saturated desiccant stops protecting the filament, so check the indicator regularly, especially in humid climates.

10. To connect more than two AMS units to the X2D you need:

  • A second printer
  • A 4-in-1 PTFE adapter (FAZ013-N)
  • A longer power cable only
  • No extra part, since the printer has six ports

The buffer has two 6-pin ports; for more units the 4-in-1 PTFE adapter combines the filament paths.

11. How does the filament reach the auxiliary nozzle?

  • From a spool or the AMS, through the auxiliary extruder at the top of the rear panel and a PTFE tube to the toolhead
  • Directly from a spool on the toolhead
  • By hand, one centimeter at a time
  • Through the main extruder

The tube path explains the lower speed limit, the longer retraction and the larger flow-dynamics value.

12. How does the X2D switch between nozzles?

  • By swapping hotends by hand during printing
  • A mechanical gear-and-trigger mechanism in the toolhead, without an additional motor
  • With a magnet that falls off
  • It does not switch

This lets the toolhead stay light and makes color changes faster because less filament is purged.

13. Which filtration does the X2D use for chamber air?

  • None
  • A paper filter only
  • A G3 pre-filter, an H12 HEPA filter and an activated carbon (coconut shell) filter
  • An ozone generator

The three stages catch dust, fine particles and some odors. This is helpful for ABS and ASA, but it does not replace ventilation in a small room.

14. What is the print area when only the auxiliary (right) nozzle is used?

  • The same as the main nozzle, 256 × 256 × 260 mm
  • About 235.5 × 256 mm with a height of 256 mm
  • 100 × 100 mm
  • 350 × 350 mm

The flow blocker and the lowered auxiliary hotend reduce the area, so check where your model sits when you plan an auxiliary-only print.
