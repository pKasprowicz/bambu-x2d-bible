Exercises · Bambu X2D + AMS 2 Pro

X2D + AMS 2 Pro challenges

Twelve hands-on exercises that teach the X2D's dual-nozzle logic, the chamber, TPU routing, drying and upkeep by doing. Each has a goal, a materials list, steps, success criteria, a hint and a worked solution.

Beginner → ExpertX2D + AMS 2 Pro⏱ 60 min read
How to use these exercises

Work through them in order the first time; later ones reuse skills from earlier ones. Mark each as done with the button under the exercise; progress is stored in your browser only. Open the hint only when stuck, and the solution after you have tried. Numbers are starting points, not guarantees: your filament, room and plate will shift them. Background reading: settings and profiles, engineering and chamber, TPU and flexibles, maintenance and troubleshooting, and the recipes.

Safety and honesty

Work in a ventilated space, let the chamber and hotends cool before touching them, and never override a safety feature. Where an exercise asks you to measure something, record your result; the page deliberately does not promise specific outcomes except where the manufacturer documents them (chamber up to 65 °C, right nozzle up to 200 mm/s, TPU left nozzle only, and so on).

The exercises

Beginner

X-1 · Setup validation print

⏱ 1–2 h
Goal

Prove that a freshly set-up X2D + AMS 2 Pro can load, switch and print in both nozzles, so you have a known-good baseline before every other exercise.

You will need
  • X2D with firmware and Bambu Studio up to date (Studio 2.5.3.60 or newer for the 01.01.00.00 features)
  • AMS 2 Pro with at least two spools of PLA in different colors
  • Textured PEI plate, clean
  • A small multi-color test model with overhangs (e.g. a calibration cube with a letter in a second color)
Steps
  1. Check the basics. Confirm the AMS is detected, the PTFE tubes are seated, the nozzle diameter shown in Studio matches the hotends fitted, and the plate type matches the real plate.
  2. Sync filaments. Read the slot types from the AMS into Studio; confirm that colors and types match the spools.
  3. Slice. Use default PLA presets, prime tower on, and note which nozzle each color is assigned to in the preview.
  4. Print and watch. Watch the first layer, the first filament change and the first nozzle switch (if any).
  5. Inspect. Look for stringing at color changes, layer consistency, and any color contamination.
Success criteria
  • Every color loads and changes without a manual intervention.
  • The first layer is even across the plate.
  • The part matches the model in the slicer within a visual check.
  • You noted the print time and the amount of waste in your log.
Hint

If a color change fails, first check the AMS slot LED state and tube seating (see the LED table in the maintenance page) before changing any slicer settings.

Worked solution / what good looks like

A clean baseline has a uniform first layer, crisp color boundaries at the letter, and a purge in the prime tower that is a solid, un-tangled block. If the tower collapses, widen its footprint or reduce its height in the preview; if colors bleed, re-run the flush settings in X-3. Record the date, firmware version and the filaments used: this is the reference for later comparisons.

Beginner

X-2 · Nozzle assignment drill

⏱ 45 min
Goal

Learn to read and control which nozzle prints which filament, and see how the assignment affects the number of nozzle switches and the waste.

You will need
  • Bambu Studio with an X2D printer preset
  • A model with 3–4 colors (any multi-part project)
  • No printing is required for the first pass: slicing alone is enough
Steps
  1. Load a multi-color project. Assign four different filaments to its parts.
  2. Try Filament-saving grouping. Slice and record the estimated time, filament use and the number of nozzle changes.
  3. Try Convenience grouping. Slice again and record the same three values.
  4. Use Custom. Assign filaments manually to left or right, then slice and record.
  5. Compare. Fill in a small table with the three strategies and write one sentence on which you would choose for this model.
Success criteria
  • You can state which nozzle each color uses in each mode.
  • You can explain why the number of switches changed.
  • You noticed that the prime tower is still needed in all modes.
Hint

Check the filament and nozzle compatibility chart before assigning: a material that is not supported on the right nozzle (e.g. TPU) can never go there, whatever the grouping mode says.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Typically Filament-saving trades extra switching for less purged material, Convenience keeps assignment simple and predictable, and Custom lets you force a material that needs a specific nozzle. Your table might show, for instance, fewer filament grams in Filament-saving but more changes; the right answer depends on your model, which is why you measure.

Intermediate

X-3 · Flush-volume reduction experiment

⏱ 2–3 h
Goal

Find the smallest flush volumes that still give clean color transitions for two specific filament pairs, and quantify the waste you saved.

You will need
  • Two filament pairs: one similar-color pair (e.g. light blue → white) and one hard pair (e.g. black → white)
  • A small two-color test model
  • Scale (optional) to weigh the waste
Steps
  1. Baseline. Slice with default flush volumes; record purge amount and prime-tower size.
  2. Open the flush dialog. Note the automatic values per color pair and write them down.
  3. Reduce. Lower the multiplier or the individual values by about 20–30 % for the easy pair and keep the hard pair as it is.
  4. Print and compare. Print both versions and examine the first layers after each color change for contamination.
  5. Iterate. Repeat once more with a further reduction until you see contamination; then step back.
Success criteria
  • You have a table of pair → flush value → visual result.
  • The easy pair uses clearly less purge than the default without visible bleed.
  • You did not reduce the hard pair so far that dark pigment shows in the light color.
Hint

Dark → light transitions need far more flushing than light → dark. Reduce the light → dark direction first.

Worked solution / what good looks like

A typical outcome: the flush matrix is asymmetric, so the savings come from the easy directions; the hard pair stays near its default. Document the final matrix as a preset note so you can re-apply it. Also check whether the dual-nozzle assignment (X-2) can avoid the hard transition altogether by putting dark and light colors on different nozzles: that saves more than any flush tweak.

Intermediate

X-4 · Aux-nozzle speed test

⏱ 2 h
Goal

Observe how the right (auxiliary) nozzle's documented limits (up to about 200 mm/s and roughly 1000 mm/s²) change print time and quality compared with the left nozzle, and decide which jobs belong where.

You will need
  • A second AMS (AMS HT or AMS 2 Pro) on the lower inlet, or a Filament Track Switch setup, so the right nozzle has a filament source
  • PLA, a simple test model (cube with an overhang and a bridge)
  • Timer or the printer's estimated time
Steps
  1. Print on the left nozzle. Use Standard speed; note the estimate and the real time.
  2. Reassign to the right nozzle. Slice again with the same settings; check the estimate.
  3. Compare. Print the right-nozzle version and compare surface quality and time.
  4. Calibrate if needed. The right nozzle uses manual flow-dynamics calibration; run it if the surface shows over- or under-extrusion.
  5. Conclude. Write which kinds of jobs (supports, draft, utility parts) suit the right nozzle.
Success criteria
  • You measured a time difference and can explain it.
  • You compared surface and bridge quality between the nozzles.
  • You listed three jobs for each nozzle.
Hint

Only compare like with like: same filament, same layer height, same plate. Check the compatibility chart for the filament on the right nozzle.

Worked solution / what good looks like

The right nozzle is capped, so jobs that benefit from high speed run longer there. That is why supports, utility parts and slower, cleaner jobs fit the right nozzle, while fast prints, TPU and anything the chart marks unsuitable stay on the left. Time estimates will differ by your model: record the ratio rather than assuming one.

Advanced

X-5 · Chamber warp test

⏱ 4–6 h
Goal

Measure how chamber temperature and soak time affect warping on a large ABS or ASA footprint, and set your own pre-heat rule.

You will need
  • ABS or ASA, dry
  • Engineering or smooth PEI plate with glue stick (check your plate guidance)
  • A 200 × 100 × 5 mm flat bar model (or any large flat part)
  • A straightedge and feeler gauges (or a ruler and paper strips)
  • Fume filter fitted and the room ventilated
Steps
  1. Print A: no soak. Heating air mode, start the print right after the bed reaches temperature.
  2. Print B: soak. Pre-heat the bed (above 90 °C takes about 30 minutes to condition the chamber) and let the chamber warm before starting.
  3. Print C: soak plus brim. As B but with a wide brim and rounded corners.
  4. Measure. After full cooldown, lay each part on a flat surface and measure the largest gap at the corners.
  5. Record. Log temperatures, times and the gap in a table.
Success criteria
  • Three parts printed with identical settings except the variable.
  • Measured corner lift recorded for each.
  • A written rule for your preferred pre-heat time and brim for large ABS/ASA parts.
Hint

Keep the door and lid closed until the part is cool; opening early undoes the point of the test. Do not compare different spools.

Worked solution / what good looks like

Expect less lift with a soaked chamber and a brim, and the most with a cold start; the size of the difference is yours to measure. Bambu documents a chamber heater up to 65 °C and a longer pre-heat for beds above 90 °C. If lift persists, reduce part cooling, use a bigger brim, or round the corners, as explained on the engineering page.

Advanced

X-6 · TPU + rigid combo

⏱ 3–4 h
Goal

Print a rigid part with an integrated TPU element (e.g. a PETG clip with a soft grip), respecting the rule that TPU runs on the left nozzle only.

You will need
  • TPU 95A HF on an external spool (or 85A/90A via the top holder/Feed Assist)
  • PETG or PLA in the AMS
  • Hardened-steel hotend of a suitable diameter: TPU 85A needs 0.6 or 0.8 mm; 95A HF works on 0.4–0.8 mm
  • Engineering or smooth PEI with glue, or textured PEI without glue
Steps
  1. Check compatibility. Match the TPU hardness to the nozzle diameter using the chart.
  2. Route the TPU. Feed from the external path to the left inlet (95A HF) or the top holder/Feed Assist (85A/90A); do not use the Filament Track Switch.
  3. Design the interface. Model the TPU element in contact with the rigid part over a large area or with mechanical interlocks (the two bond weakly).
  4. Slice. Assign both filaments to the left nozzle; keep the default volumetric limit for TPU; set flow dynamics to Auto or Off.
  5. Print, then test. Pull, flex and twist the combined part; look for delamination at the interface.
Success criteria
  • TPU printed from the correct path and only on the left nozzle.
  • No jams or clicking during the TPU sections.
  • The interface survives a hand test, or you can explain why not and what to change.
Hint

Interlocks beat adhesion: dovetails, undercuts or holes through which the TPU flows hold better than flat overlap. Place the model at the middle-front of the plate for TPU.

Worked solution / what good looks like

The reliable pattern is a rigid frame with through-holes or grooves that the TPU fills, printed with a slow TPU speed and a thorough flush on each material swap. Dry the TPU first (Bambu documents oven 70 °C for 8 h or bed 75–85 °C for 8 h), keep the chamber unheated, and never glue on textured PEI. See the TPU page.

Intermediate

X-7 · Drying experiment with a weigh-in

⏱ 10–12 h (mostly waiting)
Goal

Quantify how much moisture a spool loses during drying by weighing it before and after, and link that to print quality.

You will need
  • A hygroscopic spool that has been exposed to air for some time (PETG, nylon or TPU)
  • A kitchen scale accurate to 1 g
  • The X2D's drying function for the AMS 2 Pro (6-pin connection needed), or an oven/dryer for TPU as documented
  • A small test model, e.g. a stringing tower
Steps
  1. Weigh. Weigh the sealed-in-air spool with its holder; write the value down.
  2. Print a control. Print the test model before drying; photograph stringing and surface.
  3. Dry. Dry at the temperature and time recommended for the material (for the AMS 2 Pro, temperatures up to 65 °C and a printer ambient of at least 25 °C are documented; do not mix high- and low-temperature spools in one drying run). TPU: oven 70 °C or bed 75–85 °C, for 8 h as documented.
  4. Weigh again. Let the spool return to room temperature in a sealed environment before weighing, so condensation does not skew the result.
  5. Print a second test. Compare it with the control.
Success criteria
  • Mass before and after recorded, and the difference calculated.
  • Two test prints compared with photos.
  • Drying time and temperature logged for the material.
Hint

Weight loss is small (typically a few grams), so use the same scale, same spool holder and the same conditions. A gram or two is a meaningful signal.

Worked solution / what good looks like

A successful experiment shows a measurable drop in mass and less stringing and a smoother surface in the second print. Note that the spool may swell slightly after drying (Bambu documents a TPU spool going from 65.4 to 69.4 mm in diameter), so check that it still fits the holder. Store dried spools below about 20 % relative humidity, using the AMS 2 Pro's desiccant (yellow indicates good, purple means replace).

Intermediate

X-8 · AMS slot planning puzzle

⏱ 45 min
Goal

Plan filament slots for a realistic job list across one AMS 2 Pro, satisfying nozzle, material and routing constraints. This is a paper (or Studio) exercise.

You will need
  • Pencil and paper, or the Studio project with printer set
  • The compatibility chart and the notes on the TPU page
The puzzle

You have one AMS 2 Pro (four slots, upper inlet → left nozzle) and one external spool. Jobs: (a) PLA figure in 3 colors plus PLA support; (b) PETG bracket; (c) TPU 95A HF gasket; (d) a PA6-CF jig. You do not want to change spools between (a) and (b), and the TPU must not touch the AMS.

Steps
  1. List the constraints. Which jobs can share an AMS load-out? Which need the external spool?
  2. Group the jobs. Put jobs that share a load-out in the same session; separate the rest.
  3. Assign slots. Fill the four slots for the first session and name the external spool.
  4. Assign nozzles. State which nozzle each filament uses (all left with one AMS).
  5. Check. Verify each filament against the chart; mark any drying needs.
Success criteria
  • No TPU in the AMS or on the right nozzle.
  • PA6-CF is on the left nozzle and dried.
  • At most one spool swap between sessions.
Hint

Four slots hold PLA ×3 colors + PETG; the support can share a slot with one of the colors if the support is in the body color (or print it as a second pass).

Worked solution / what good looks like

Session 1 (AMS A): slot 1–3 PLA colors, slot 4 PETG; run jobs (a) and (b). Session 2: load PA6-CF into the AMS (dry it first, keep it away from low-temperature spools while drying) and run (d). Session 3: TPU on the external spool, left nozzle, job (c). Alternative: a second AMS on the lower inlet or a Filament Track Switch widens the options, but it does not change the TPU rule.

Advanced

X-9 · Support-interface material test

⏱ 3–4 h
Goal

Test whether a different material for the support interface (PETG under PLA, or a rigid/soft combo) improves surface finish and removability compared with same-material supports.

You will need
  • PLA and PETG spools (a third material if available, for example PVA, which Bambu lists as recommended on the right nozzle)
  • A test overhang model (a bridge plus a 45° and a 70° overhang)
  • Deburring tool and a camera
Steps
  1. Baseline. Print with PLA supports in PLA; note removal effort and the underside quality.
  2. Variant A. Set the support interface filament to PETG; keep the body in PLA.
  3. Vary the gap. Try a top Z-distance of 0.1 and 0.2 mm.
  4. Rate. Score removal ease (1–5), surface quality (1–5) and filament waste (grams).
  5. Pick a rule. Write which combination you will use for which parts.
Success criteria
  • At least three variants printed and scored.
  • The prime tower and flush volumes were reviewed for each material change.
  • A one-line support rule for your future prints.
Hint

Each material change costs a flush, so interface-only changes add purge; compare the score gain against the waste.

Worked solution / what good looks like

The usual finding is that a dissimilar interface material (PETG under PLA) separates cleanly and leaves a smoother underside, at the price of more purge. Whether it is worth it depends on the part: visible faces, yes; hidden ones, no. See also the support-interface recipe.

Advanced

X-10 · Filament profile creation

⏱ 3 h
Goal

Create and calibrate a custom filament preset for a non-Bambu spool, including the flow dynamics setting, and verify it with a test print.

You will need
  • A third-party spool you want to use regularly (PLA is simplest)
  • Its manufacturer's temperature range from the label
  • Bambu Studio on the X2D
Steps
  1. Duplicate a close preset. Start from the generic profile of the same material.
  2. Edit. Set nozzle temperature within the manufacturer's range, bed temperature, and the volumetric limit; name the preset clearly.
  3. Choose the flow-dynamics mode. Filament presets have Auto and Off; pick Auto for the left nozzle, and for the right nozzle run the manual calibration.
  4. Dry and load. Dry the spool if it is hygroscopic; load it into an AMS slot and select the new preset.
  5. Test and save. Print a test model, adjust, and save the final version.
Success criteria
  • The preset appears for the AMS slot and prints without under- or over-extrusion.
  • The preset name records brand, material and date.
  • You noted any changes from the starting profile.
Hint

Change one parameter at a time and test; changing temperature and flow together hides cause and effect.

Worked solution / what good looks like

A good preset starts from the generic preset, stays inside the label range, and is verified with the same test model each time. Export it (or share it with the project) so you can rebuild it. Values you arrive at are specific to your spool and are not documented figures, so keep notes. See settings and profiles.

X-11 · Multi-plate batch

⏱ 3–5 h
Goal

Set up a project with several plates that print in sequence with minimal human intervention, and share it as a .gcode.3mf.

You will need
  • PLA or PETG, two matching spools in adjacent slots (for Auto-Refill: same brand, type and color, same side)
  • Three different small models
  • Optional: the Handy app for remote monitoring
Steps
  1. Create plates. Place each model on its own plate; arrange for spacing.
  2. Per-plate settings. Check the plate type, nozzle assignment and prime tower on each.
  3. Slice all. Review time and filament per plate; make sure the total filament fits the loaded spools.
  4. Send. Export or send via the cloud or LAN, and start. Plan to swap the plate between jobs if the printer cannot clear it.
  5. Review. After the batch, log failures and the cause.
Success criteria
  • All plates sliced with correct plate type and nozzle assignment.
  • Filament budget checked before starting.
  • Print options and AI detections (spaghetti, foreign object, etc.) enabled.
Hint

Estimate the filament use per plate; a spool that is almost empty will pause the print unless an identical backup spool is present.

Worked solution / what good looks like

The key is a filament budget: add up the grams per plate and compare with the spool weights. Keep the part count per plate moderate, leave clearance, and use a tested filament profile. Because the printer does not unload parts by itself, each plate needs you (or a part-removal routine of your own) between jobs. Review the logs afterwards and update the next batch.

Expert

X-12 · Maintenance log

⏱ 1 h + ongoing
Goal

Build and keep a maintenance log for your X2D and AMS 2 Pro, turning the manufacturer's maintenance topics into a schedule you actually follow.

You will need
  • A spreadsheet or notebook
  • The maintenance page and the screen's toolbox (Dry Filament, Linear Rod Cleaning, Lead Screw Lubrication, Nozzle Cold Pull, Heating Base Maintenance Mode)
  • Isopropyl alcohol, lint-free cloth, spare PTFE and desiccant as needed
Steps
  1. List the items. Nozzles and the Gear-and-Trigger mechanism, aux extruder and its PTFE tube, filters (G3, H12 HEPA, carbon), cameras, rods and lead screws, build plate, AMS desiccant and drying modules, firmware.
  2. Set intervals. Use documented ones where they exist (for example desiccant is replaced after roughly 4–6 weeks, or when the indicator turns purple); label the rest "typical" and adjust to your use.
  3. Do a first pass. Perform each task once and record the date, hours (if known) and findings.
  4. Record anomalies. Note any wear, noise or print-quality change and link it to the task that fixed it.
  5. Review monthly. Update the intervals from what you see.
Success criteria
  • A log with at least ten items and an interval for each.
  • First-pass results filled in.
  • One improvement made from what the log revealed.
Hint

Tie tasks to events, such as "every spool change check the PTFE end" or "every firmware update repeat the validation print (X-1)", rather than to dates alone.

Worked solution / what good looks like

A good log is short enough that you use it. Split it into per-print (glance at plate and nozzle), per-week (clean rods, check desiccant), per-month (filters, PTFE ends, camera lenses) and per-update (X-1). Pair it with your own error notes so that recurring faults stand out; if you meet an issue you cannot resolve, collect logs and contact support as described on the maintenance page.

Key takeaways

  • Nozzle assignment, flush volumes and slot planning decide waste and reliability on the X2D.
  • TPU stays on the left nozzle and off the AMS; the right nozzle is limited in speed.
  • Chamber and dry-filament practice make the biggest difference to engineering materials.
  • A maintenance log turns good intentions into a habit.
