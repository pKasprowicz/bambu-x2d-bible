Part 7 · Practice & exercises

Level 4 · Expert

Capstone projects. They are open-ended: you define the requirements, plan the work, produce evidence and document the result. Each one takes a weekend or more and ends in a deliverable you could show to a customer or a colleague.

Expert⏱ 8–20 h per project12 projects

How capstone projects work

Levels 1–3 gave you steps. Level 4 gives you a brief, a list of deliverables and acceptance criteria; the path is yours. Before you start any project, write a half-page plan with the requirement, the risks, the measurements you will take and a time budget. When you finish, compare the result with the plan and write down what you would do differently.

These projects assume the skills of Level 3: calibrated profiles, tolerance tables, calculation habits and basic scripting. You will also use the calculators for cost, flow and time estimates.

Self-assessment rubric

Score each project from 0 to 3 in each column (0 = missing, 1 = partly, 2 = good, 3 = excellent). A total of 10 or more out of 15 counts as a pass for your own tracking; the aim is honest feedback, not a grade.

Project 1 — Compliant mechanism design

⏱ ~10 h
Brief

Design a single-piece mechanism that moves by bending, with no pins, hinges or assembly: for example a parallel gripper, a constant-force clip or a micro-positioning stage. Flexures replace joints: they have no backlash and no friction but store elastic energy and can fatigue.

Deliverables
  • A sketch with the flexure dimensions and a strain calculation for each flexure
  • CAD files (parametric), printed prototypes in two materials (e.g. PETG and nylon or TPU 95A)
  • A test report: stroke, force, and cycles to failure
Suggested path
  1. Specify the required stroke (e.g. 6 mm jaw opening), grip force and cycle life.
  2. Choose flexure geometry (simple neck, notch or leaf) and size it with the formula in Figure 2 so ε stays below half the allowable strain.
  3. Print with layers parallel to the bending plane (flexure lying flat) and at least 3 walls across the thin section. Flexures of 0.8 mm require line widths that fit exactly: set the wall width in the slicer to a multiple of the line width.
  4. Test stroke and force with a spring scale; cycle 1,000 times and check for whitening, cracks or loss of preload.
  5. Iterate once: make the flexure 25 % longer and compare force and life.
Acceptance criteria
  • Mechanism works as one piece with the specified stroke
  • Calculated and measured stroke agree within 20 %
  • At least 1,000 cycles in the best material without failure, or a documented failure analysis
Hint

A 0.8 mm wall prints as two lines with a 0.4 mm nozzle (line width about 0.42 mm each), so there is no gap-fill guesswork. A thickness of 0.9 mm can become 0.84 plus a thin gap fill, which behaves differently. Start from multiples of the line width.

Worked example

A PETG gripper with 0.84 mm flexures, 12 mm long, bent ±15° has ε = 0.84 × 0.26 / 24 = 0.9 % and survived 5,000 cycles; the same design in PLA cracked at the neck after 40 cycles. A 3 mm × 5 mm test coupon of TPU 95A could bend 90° but gave a weak grip. The lesson: material allowable strain dominates; geometry must adapt to it. See Joints & fasteners and Flexibles & specialty filaments.

Project 2 — Topology-driven bracket

⏱ ~12 h
Brief

Design a bracket for a defined load (for example 150 N at the tip of a 100 mm arm, with two bolt holes), use a topology or load-path method to remove material where it is not needed, and verify with physical tests that the optimized bracket beats a simple solid bracket in strength-to-weight ratio.

Deliverables
  • Load case drawing with loads, supports and keep-out regions
  • Solid baseline and an optimized bracket (CAD and STL)
  • Test results for both brackets (n ≥ 3 each) and a mass comparison
Suggested path
  1. Define the loads in numbers: force, direction, bolt positions, keep-out zones for fasteners and tools. Include a safety factor (2–3 for printed parts).
  2. Print the solid baseline and test it first, so you know the load at failure and the mass.
  3. Find the load paths with a free FEA or topology-optimization tool (e.g. FreeCAD FEM or a generative-design function in your CAD package) or by hand sketching force flow: material should follow tension and compression lines.
  4. Rebuild a clean shape from the result: remove sharp inside corners, set a minimum feature size of 3× the nozzle diameter, orient so layers do not take tension across them.
  5. Print and test both versions three times each in the same orientation and load rig. Record failure load and mass; compute failure load per gram.
  6. Compare prediction and result, and document where the model was wrong (usually layer adhesion).
Acceptance criteria
  • Optimized bracket carries at least 90 % of the baseline load at 20 % or more less mass
  • Mass, load and strength-to-weight table with repeated tests
  • A written explanation of where and why it failed
Hint

Simulation tools assume isotropic material; printed parts are weaker across layers. Use a lower allowable stress for the Z direction (for example 30–50 % of the in-plane value as a first guess) and always validate by testing.

Worked example

Baseline: 62 g PETG bracket, failure at 410 N (6.6 N/g). Optimized: 41 g, failure at 380 N (9.3 N/g). Mass −34 %, strength −7 %, strength-to-weight +40 %. Failure started at a fillet next to a bolt hole where layers ran across the load; adding a 4 mm fillet and rotating the part 90° in the slicer improved the next print by 15 %. Related theory: Strength & orientation.

Project 3 — Multi-material functional assembly

⏱ ~10 h
Brief

Design a product with at least one rigid and one flexible material in a single print or assembly: a gasketed enclosure, a tool handle with a soft grip, or a gripper with soft pads. Use each material where it matters, document the interface, and test the function.

Deliverables
  • A bill of materials (parts, material, nozzle, mass) and an exploded drawing
  • Print files with filament assignments, plus the printed assembly
  • A function test (for example water or dust resistance, grip force, drop test)
Suggested path
  1. Write the function requirements: e.g. "survives 1 min under 5 cm of water", "grip holds 5 N pull".
  2. Choose materials by function and check compatibility of the pair: some combinations bond to each other (PETG on TPU), others do not (PLA on PETG may bond weakly). Review Flexibles & specialty filaments and Filament compatibility.
  3. Design the interface with a mechanical lock (dovetail, undercut or through-holes) so that you do not depend on chemical bonding alone.
  4. Plan the print: which parts print together, which nozzle prints which material, expected flush waste (see L3-8).
  5. Print, assemble and test against the requirements. Photograph failures.
Acceptance criteria
  • All functional requirements met and measured
  • Interface survives a pull-off test (at least a number you wrote down beforehand)
  • BOM and filament assignment documented
Hint

Flexible materials are very sensitive to moisture and to the path to the extruder. Dry them and print slowly. Check the machine's guidance for which nozzle and which feeding path to use for TPU, and keep flexible material out of an automatic filament feeder that does not support it.

Worked example

PETG enclosure with a 1.2 mm TPU gasket printed directly onto the lid with a dovetail groove. Test: 5 cm water for 1 min, no leakage at 4 screws torqued by hand; at 2 screws, a drip appeared in the corner. Lesson: gasket compression of about 20–30 % and screw spacing decide sealing, not the material alone. See also TPU & flexibles on X2D.

Project 4 — Production batch optimization

⏱ ~12 h
Brief

Choose a small product (e.g. a 40 mm cable organizer) and make 20 identical copies as efficiently as possible. Build a cost model, then optimize plate layout, settings, post-processing and failure rate to lower the cost per good part and keep quality within spec.

Deliverables
  • A cost model (machine time, material, electricity, labor, failures) in a spreadsheet
  • Three layouts or settings variants with measured time, mass and quality
  • A finished batch with inspection records and a final cost per part
Suggested path
  1. Build the cost model: cost per part = (machine rate × print time + material price × mass + electricity + labor per batch ÷ parts per batch) ÷ yield, where yield is the fraction of good parts (0–1).
  2. Define the quality spec: critical dimensions, tolerance, surface and function test. Decide what counts as a failure.
  3. Test layouts: parts per plate, orientation, spacing, print sequence (the plate that you can unload without tools), and a variant with a faster profile (see L3-13).
  4. Reduce hands-on time: support-free orientation, brim-free adhesion, a standard plate cleaning routine, a quick inspection template.
  5. Make the batch of 20 parts and inspect each one; record failures and the cause.
  6. Report the final cost per good part and the three biggest savings.
Acceptance criteria
  • Final cost per good part at least 25 % below the baseline
  • All 20 parts inspected against the spec, with records
  • A sensitivity analysis: what happens to cost if failure rate doubles or labor time halves
Hint

Do the sensitivity analysis before you optimize. If labor is 60 % of the cost, saving 5 minutes of print time per plate does not matter; saving 2 minutes of handling does. The calculators include cost and time estimators to cross-check your spreadsheet.

Worked example

Baseline: 6 parts per plate, 2 h each plate, 1.30 per part. Layout optimization: 12 parts per plate with 4 mm spacing, one plate of 12 and one of 8: 1.00 per part. Faster profile (0.24 mm layers, 4 instead of 6 walls) and no brim: 0.82 per part with yield 95 %. A failed corner on two parts was traced to a plate-edge adhesion problem; the fix was a cleaning step. All figures are illustrative. See Economics, licensing & sustainability.

Project 5 — Statistical process control (Cp/Cpk)

⏱ ~10 h
Brief

Treat your printer as a process: print 30 identical parts, measure one critical dimension on each, and compute the process capability indices Cp and Cpk. Decide whether your printer can reliably meet a given tolerance, and what to change if it cannot.

Deliverables
  • A table of 30 measurements with the print order
  • An X-bar/individuals control chart and a histogram with specification limits
  • A capability report with Cp, Cpk and recommended action
Suggested path
  1. Choose the characteristic and tolerance, for example the diameter of a 20 mm vertical hole from hole_gauge.scad or a 20 mm outside dimension, with a tolerance of ±0.20 mm.
  2. Print 30 parts in sequence in as few sessions as possible (e.g. 3 plates of 10), recording plate, position and time. Use the same filament spool, temperature and plate.
  3. Measure every part at room temperature, with the same calipers and procedure. Use a gauge study: measure 10 of them twice to estimate the measurement error (it should be under 10 % of the tolerance).
  4. Plot an individuals control chart in print order, with the mean and limits at ±3σ. Look for trends, steps after a spool change and position effects.
  5. Compute Cp and Cpk (formulas in Figure 6). A common target for stable processes is Cpk ≥ 1.33.
  6. Act: shift the mean with a CAD offset or a compensation, reduce variation (dry filament, plate calibration, constant ambient temperature) and repeat with 30 new parts.
Acceptance criteria
  • 30 measurements, a control chart and a histogram with specification limits
  • Calculated Cp and Cpk with a one-paragraph interpretation
  • A second run after an improvement, showing Cpk improved
Hint

Do not mix data from different conditions in one capability study. If two plates have different means, analyze them separately first. If your measurement error is large, repeat each measurement three times and use the average; otherwise Cp mostly shows how bad your calipers are.

Worked example

The 30-part run with mean 20.06 mm and σ = 0.05 mm gives Cp = 1.33 and Cpk = 0.93: the printer is precise but the average is 0.06 mm too large. After a −0.06 mm CAD offset on the diameter, the second run had mean 20.00, σ 0.045 → Cpk = 0.2/0.135 = 1.48. A trend of +0.02 mm over the run suggested a temperature drift in the room; the third run used an enclosure and reduced σ to 0.035. All numbers are examples. Tools for tolerances: Tolerances & fits.

Project 6 — Design of experiments for settings

⏱ ~12 h
Brief

Replace "change one thing at a time" with a designed experiment: vary several settings at once in a planned pattern and calculate each factor's effect and the interactions from far fewer prints. Use it to find the best combination of settings for the strength or the accuracy of a part.

Deliverables
  • A DoE table with randomized run order
  • Responses for every run and a main-effects chart
  • Recommended settings and a confirmation run
Suggested path
  1. Choose a response you can measure well (failure load of a standard bar, dimension error, surface roughness score) and 3 factors you believe matter, with a low and a high level each; keep levels far enough apart to see an effect but inside safe printing limits.
  2. Write the 2³ table (8 runs, as in Figure 7), and randomize the order with a dice or a spreadsheet to avoid time trends being mistaken for effects.
  3. Print and measure all runs; if you can afford it, replicate each run twice to estimate the noise.
  4. Calculate main effects: for each factor, mean response at the high level minus mean at the low level. Calculate at least one interaction (A×C = mean of the runs where A and C have the same sign minus the others).
  5. Rank the effects and decide which factors to keep. Pick settings to maximize the response (or to balance it with print time).
  6. Confirm with a run at the chosen settings, which was not part of the design, and compare it with the prediction.
Acceptance criteria
  • A complete randomized table with all responses
  • Main effects and one interaction calculated by hand or in a spreadsheet
  • A confirmation run within about 10 % of the prediction
Hint

An effect is only credible if it is larger than the run-to-run noise. Replicate at least the center point (a ninth run between the low and high levels) three times to estimate the noise. If a factor's effect is smaller than the noise, set it to the cheapest level.

Worked example

From Figure 7: A (temperature) = +37.5 N, B (speed) = −12.5 N, C (layer height) = −40 N. Conclusion: high temperature and thin layers help most; speed matters little, so run at the fast level. Predicted best run: temperature 220 °C, speed 200 mm/s, layer 0.12 mm → run 4 = 335 N, which is close to the best measured run (340 N at 100 mm/s) but 5 minutes faster per part. The confirmation print gave 330 N. Full-factorial designs grow quickly (2⁵ = 32 runs); with many factors use a fractional design. Practical background: Calibration playbook.

Project 7 — Reverse-engineer and replace a part

⏱ ~8 h
Brief

Pick a broken or worn part in your home or workshop (a knob, a bracket, a battery cover, a drawer guide). Measure it, understand what it does, model it, and print a replacement that fits and works at least as well as the original. Redesign weak points rather than copying them.

Deliverables
  • A dimensioned sketch with datum surfaces, plus photos of the original from several sides
  • A parametric CAD model and the printed replacement
  • A short analysis of why the original failed and what you changed
Suggested path
  1. Understand the function: what loads, what mating parts, what environment (heat, UV, chemicals). Pick a material that suits it (see Materials overview).
  2. Choose datums (two or three flat faces) and measure every feature from them. Measure critical features three times and note any wear.
  3. Make a quick fit-check model of only the interface features and print it in 10–20 minutes before you model the whole part.
  4. Model parametrically, with each measured value as a named parameter and a clearance parameter that you can adjust (see Parametric CAD workflow).
  5. Improve the failure points: add fillets at the crack origin, rotate the print orientation, add ribs.
  6. Install and use the replacement for at least a week, then inspect.
Acceptance criteria
  • The replacement fits without filing or force and performs its function
  • All dimensions are parameters in the model
  • You documented one improvement over the original and the reason
Hint

Do not copy dimensions into the final model without allowing for the way a printed hole shrinks and a printed post grows. Use your tolerance table from L2-6 or a hole-gauge print (hole_gauge.scad) to choose the clearance.

Worked example

A broken oven-knob adapter: the original was a thin brittle sleeve with a D-shaped bore. Measurement from the flat of the D gave a width of 12.1 mm across the flat; the fit-check print needed +0.15 mm clearance. The replacement used a wall of 3 mm and a PETG body printed with the axis horizontal so that layers did not split along the bore. A heat-resistant variant (ASA) was used at the hot side. After a month of daily use there was no cracking.

Project 8 — Jig or fixture for manufacturing

⏱ ~10 h
Brief

Choose a repeated manual operation: drilling holes in a plate, gluing two parts at an angle, bending wire, assembling a small product. Design and print a jig or fixture that locates the workpiece, makes the operation faster and the result repeatable, and include a go/no-go gauge to check the output.

Deliverables
  • The operation described in steps, with a time-and-error baseline (10 repetitions without the jig)
  • The jig and a go/no-go gauge
  • A comparison with the jig (10 repetitions): time, spread of the result, mistakes
Suggested path
  1. Time the baseline: do the operation 10 times, noting the time and the error (for example the hole position error in mm).
  2. Locate the workpiece on a flat face with three points, and push it against two more points on edges. Remove any excess constraint.
  3. Add a stop or a clamp that is quick (a cam, a toggle, a rubber band or a magnet) and that does not damage the work.
  4. Guide the tool with a bushing or hole sized for the drill (+0.2 mm clearance). Make the guide replaceable when it wears: use a metal bushing or a replaceable printed insert.
  5. Make a go/no-go gauge for the critical dimension, based on the tolerance band you want.
  6. Compare time and error with the baseline.
Acceptance criteria
  • Operation time reduced by at least 30 %, or error spread halved
  • The jig can be used by someone else after reading the instructions you wrote
  • A gauge checks the critical dimension
Hint

Print fixtures in PETG or nylon-based material for wear and impact resistance, with 4–6 walls and 30–40 % infill, since the loads are mostly local. Put the locating surfaces on the bottom of the print so they are flat and accurate (use design patterns for clamps and stops).

Worked example

Drilling two 4 mm holes 40 mm apart in aluminium strips. Baseline by hand with a marked line: 70 s per part, position error up to ±0.8 mm. With a printed jig with a hardened-steel drill bushing: 25 s per part, error ±0.15 mm. A go/no-go gauge with 4.0 / 4.3 mm pins checked hole positions on a 40.0 mm centre. The jig paid for itself after about 40 parts. All numbers illustrative.

Project 9 — Material characterization test plan

⏱ ~15 h
Brief

Create a small datasheet for one filament, with the property values measured as you print it, in your orientation and with your settings. This is more useful than a manufacturer's datasheet, which is measured on injection-molded samples.

Deliverables
  • A test plan: properties, specimens, sample counts, orientation, standard (or the standard you adapted from)
  • The printed specimens and raw data
  • A one-page datasheet with mean ± standard deviation for each property
Suggested path
  1. Define the properties: for example tensile strength and elongation, flexural modulus, notched impact, heat softening (a simple oven or hot-water test), moisture uptake after 24 h, shrinkage.
  2. Plan specimens: pick geometry from a standard (ISO 527 type 1BA, ISO 178 flexural bar) or a scaled version; list the orientations and the number of samples (≥5 each).
  3. Standardize preparation: dry the filament the same way, print in a random order, label every specimen, and condition them for 48 h at room temperature.
  4. Test with a method you can defend: for bending, use known weights or a luggage scale and a fixture and record the load at break and the deflection. For tensile, a home-built rig with a hanging bucket of water can work if the load is applied slowly.
  5. Analyze: mean, standard deviation and the coefficient of variation per condition; compute the anisotropy ratio (Z strength ÷ XY strength).
  6. Write the datasheet with the settings used, the test method, its limitations and a statement that the values apply to your conditions only.
Acceptance criteria
  • At least two properties and two orientations measured with ≥5 specimens each
  • Datasheet states mean, standard deviation, method and settings
  • Limitations written down honestly
Hint

Printed specimens break at the weakest spot, often at the grip. Add a radius at the transition and print the grip areas with more walls so the break happens in the gauge section. If more than a third of the specimens break at the grip, repeat the test with a different grip. See Engineering plastics for expected values.

Worked example

PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 5 specimens per orientation. Flat: 42 ± 2 MPa. On edge: 38 ± 3 MPa. Standing: 17 ± 4 MPa. Anisotropy Z/XY ≈ 0.40. Moisture gain after 24 h at 60 % RH: roughly 0.2 %. Heat softening (loaded bar in a heated oven): visible sag at about 70 °C. These values sit in the range often quoted for PETG, but your own numbers are what you should design with. See also L3-9 for annealing.

Project 10 — Large-part segmentation

⏱ ~12 h
Brief

Make a part that is larger than the build plate (for X2D that is 256 × 256 × 260 mm): a 450 mm shelf bracket, a helmet, a lamp shade, a drone body. Split it into segments, print them, align them, and join them so the seams are strong and invisible enough.

Deliverables
  • The master model and the split plan (cut positions, joint type, orientation of each segment)
  • Printed segments, assembled part and a strength check at the joints
  • Notes on tolerances, glue and finishing
Suggested path
  1. Decide the number of segments from the build volume with a margin of 10 mm for brim and skirt, and from the print time per segment (keep each under 12 h).
  2. Pick cut positions in low-stress places and where the seam is hidden by a step, edge or color change. Avoid cutting through holes or thin walls.
  3. Add alignment features: pins and holes, tongue and groove, or a dovetail. Use a 0.15–0.25 mm clearance and test a joint coupon first. Each segment must lie flat on the plate and printable without support if possible.
  4. Print all segments from the same spool and with the same settings to keep color and shrinkage consistent.
  5. Dry-fit before gluing. Sand mating faces flat. Glue with a glue that suits the material (cyanoacrylate with a filler, two-part epoxy for PETG or nylon, solvent for ABS or ASA).
  6. Test the joint: the assembled part must carry the target load without failing at a seam.
Acceptance criteria
  • Final part is more than 1.5× longer than the build plate in at least one direction
  • Segments align without gaps larger than 0.3 mm
  • Joint test passes the planned load, with a failure that is not at the seam
Hint

Most CAD tools can split along a plane and many slicers can cut a model and add connectors automatically, but check the result: automatic pins are generous with clearance and can wobble. Always check the fit of a joint coupon before you print all the segments.

Worked example

A 620 mm shelf bracket in three segments of 215, 190 and 215 mm. Cuts at the two non-loaded straight sections with 6 × 8 mm dovetails (clearance 0.2 mm) and two M4 threaded rods through the whole length for stiffness. Epoxy in the joints. At a load of 120 N at the end of a 600 mm arm there was no joint failure, and a 4 mm V-groove hid the seam lines. For 3 segments of 230 mm the print time was 9 h each with 0.2 mm layers.

Project 11 — Mould or casting workflow

⏱ ~15 h
Brief

Use 3D printing to make a master or a mould and cast a small series in silicone, polyurethane resin, plaster or wax. Printing gives you the geometry; casting gives you the material and the surface. Typical projects: soft silicone feet, resin knobs, plaster tiles.

Deliverables
  • A design drawing with parting line, pour gate and vents
  • The printed master or mould, in a material that is compatible with the casting material
  • At least three cast parts, with a note on defects and fixes
Suggested path
  1. Choose the process: print the master and cast a silicone mould (best surface) or print the mould directly (fastest, for plaster, wax or two-part resin with release agent).
  2. Design for demolding: draft of 2–3° on vertical faces, no undercuts unless the mould is flexible, a parting line on an edge, a generous pour gate and a vent at each high point.
  3. Prepare the master: print fine (0.08–0.12 mm layers), sand, apply filler primer and sand again; seal against silicone inhibition (check your silicone's manufacturer for compatibility, since sulfur and some resins inhibit cure).
  4. Make the mould: build a box, fix the master with a release agent, mix silicone by weight, degas if possible or pour in a thin stream, and let it cure fully.
  5. Cast the final material following its datasheet, with gloves, ventilation and eye protection. Respect the pot life.
  6. Inspect and improve bubbles, flash and shrinkage; adjust gates and vents.
Safety

Resins, isocyanates in some polyurethanes and cleaning solvents are hazardous. Read the safety data sheet, ventilate, use nitrile gloves and eye protection, and never use a cast mould or resin for food contact unless it is rated for it. Review the safety page.

Acceptance criteria
  • Three cast parts without major defects, matching the master within 1 %
  • Documented mix ratios, cure times and release agent
  • A list of defects and their fixes
Hint

Do a small test before the big pour: cast a coin-sized piece of the same silicone against a printed scrap with your sealing method. If the silicone remains sticky, the print is inhibiting cure; try another sealer or print material.

Worked example

A 40 mm knob master printed in PLA at 0.1 mm, filler primer and wet sanded to 800 grit. Silicone (Shore A 20) mould in two halves with registration keys. Casting in two-part polyurethane resin, 3 parts per mould. Result: surface good, two bubbles on the top face in the first cast; adding a vent at the highest point and tilting the mould during pouring solved this. Mass of the cast part was 14.2 g vs 11 g of printed PLA, so the cast part also felt more solid.

Project 12 — Open-source release of a design

⏱ ~8 h
Brief

Publish one of your own designs so that a stranger can print, assemble and modify it without asking you anything. A good release is documentation plus files plus photos plus a license.

Deliverables
  • A repository or model page with source files (CAD or SCAD), print-ready files (STL or 3MF) and a README
  • A license file and attribution of any remixed work
  • Photos of the printed result and a build-and-assemble guide
  • An independent test: someone else prints it from your instructions
Suggested path
  1. Pick a design that works, and clean it: name parameters, remove dead features, check it prints without supports or document them.
  2. Choose a license: Creative Commons (CC BY for credit, CC BY-SA for share-alike, CC0 for free use, add NC only if you really want to forbid commercial use) for designs and an open-source license (MIT, GPL) for code such as OpenSCAD. If you remixed someone's work, make sure its license allows this and credit it. Details in Economics, licensing & sustainability.
  3. Photograph the part with a plain background, soft daylight and a scale object; include a print-in-progress photo and the assembly photos.
  4. Write the README using the checklist in Figure 13, including exact print settings and orientation, and tested printers.
  5. Test with another person: ask a friend to print it from the README alone and fix every question they ask in the documentation.
  6. Publish to a repository host or model site, tag a version and add a changelog.
Acceptance criteria
  • All the checklist items are in the README, with a license file
  • A second person printed the part successfully from your documentation
  • Source files can be opened and edited by others (no proprietary-only format without an exchange file)
Hint

Export STEP or 3MF in addition to STL, since STL has no units or colors. Put a print profile or a settings table into the repository; many readers will copy it directly. Add a section "What to change" for your parameters. The journal template on the exercises overview is a good starting point for the print notes.

Worked example

A parametric box with a living-hinge lid was released with box.scad (all parameters at the top), STL for three sizes, a 6-line print profile table (PETG, 0.2 mm, 3 walls, 15 % gyroid, no supports), five photos, a CC BY 4.0 license and a changelog. A friend printed it on a different printer and reported one problem: a hinge too tight at 0.4 mm clearance. The README now suggests 0.5 mm for printers with larger first-layer flare, and the hinge parameter has a comment explaining it.

Key takeaways

  • Write requirements and acceptance criteria before design; measure results against them.
  • Statistics (Cp/Cpk, DoE) turn "it seems better" into numbers you can defend.
  • Fixtures, batch layout and documentation matter as much as the part geometry in real use.
  • Every project ends with a document someone else could follow; a design that cannot be reproduced is not finished.
